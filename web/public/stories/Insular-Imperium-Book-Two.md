# Chapter One: The Dullest Day

**LUCAN**

There was ice on the ink.

Not much of it: a grey skin on every well down the long table, one well to each clerk, and the clerks broke it as they sat down, with the butts of their pens, and wiped the pens on their cuffs, and blew into their hands. The brazier at the far end of the room had been lit at the first hour, when they came up the stair, and not before. Lucan had worked out the figure in his first winter in the Spire: what it cost to keep a brazier in all night on this floor, against what it cost to lose the early part of the morning to cold ink and stiff fingers. The brazier had lost. So the clerks blew into their hands, and by the second hour the wells would run clear, and the room would smell as it smelled on any working day of the year, of lamp-oil and wet ink, and of wet wool drying on the benches.

He was cold himself. He noted it as he noted the weather: his feet in their felt shoes on the black floor; the ache that had come into the knuckles of his writing hand these last few winters, and went out of them by the third hour; the dryness the lamps put in his eyes. He was past sixty, and his body had begun to send him its accounts, small ones, quarterly, in arrears. He paid them, and did not dispute them.

It was a full table this quarter, and half of it had a cold. He could hear it: the small wet sounds of men who had come up a cold stair before it was light, and a cough at the far end that had come up the stair with its owner for a week of mornings, and would put a lamp in a window at home by the spring if it was not seen to. He had told Cluvius to see to it two days ago. They wore the Survey's black, with the closed hand worked white at the breast, and they sat in the order of their islands, each with his book and his well and the counting-frame at his elbow with its beads run all to one end; and no man spoke until he was spoken to.

The tall windows down the harbor side of the room gave nothing at this hour but sleet, and the grey beyond the sleet. He had looked out of them already. He had stood at the middle one at first light with the packet from the capital open in his hands, and watched the night's work end on the quay below: the last file of the working dead going down the slipway into the harbor, unhurried, in good order, as they went every morning before the town was up, until the water had closed over the last of them and there was nothing on the quay but the night's stone, stacked and squared, and the sleet coming down on it. From that height there was nothing of them to see but the tops of their heads, which was as much of them as the custom allowed in any case.

Out past the end of the mole a black ship lay at anchor, with lamps lit along her gold rail. They had been lit, by day and by night, for as long as she had lain there. The ship's book had come ashore by boat on the first morning. Its owner had not, because he had not sent for her.

The packet lay at his right hand under the sand-box: four sheets, close-written, come across from the capital in oilskin on the night tide, in the bottom of a boat that his books called the salt-boat, in a hand so fine that he had read them through once for the hand alone. He had been buying that hand since the summer. It was worth what it cost. It had brought him the last figures of the quarter, and the audit had waited for them, which it had never done for anything before.

At his left Vopiscus sat with the Survey's books squared in front of him, waiting until the last clerk had done blowing into his hands. He was the senior quaestor of the Survey, a heavy careful man whose voice went neither up nor down, and he had read Lucan the Survey's quarter at every audit there had ever been. He did not need to be told to begin. Someone had broken the ice on Lucan's own well before he came in. Lucan dipped his pen, and Vopiscus began.

He read it as it was always read, island by island, Thessa first, because Thessa had been first.

"Thessa," said Vopiscus; and read the stock in hand at the opening of the quarter, and the stock raised in it, and the stock lost, and what was carried. The clerk who kept Thessa's book had kept it since there was a Thessa to keep. He had a chilblain on the knuckle of his first finger that came back with the frosts, and he wrote round it. He ran the finger down his column, and moved a bead along its wire, and said, "Agreed." Lucan entered what was carried. Thessa had settled in a season, the first of them all, and had made the same return every quarter since, give or take a fishing boat, so that he could have entered it without hearing it read. He heard it read. That was what the audit was for. There was a rhythm in it that he had never minded: the figure read, the figure found, agreed, entered; and the next.

"Corvus." That was this island, under their feet. The clerk who kept it put his finger on a line and said, "Less one." One of the quay stock, lost off the end of the mole in the gale before the new moon, and not recovered. "Entered," said Vopiscus, without looking up from his book; and the clerk said, "Agreed." Then the rest, one after another, down the table, and Hollowmere last of them, which was new, and still settling, and short in the columns that would be long by the spring; and at each the one who kept it said agreed, and Lucan's pen went down the page, and against the windows the sleet went on with a small dry sound, like sand thrown on a wet page.

Then the islands that had made no return, which the form put at the end.

"The Survey of the Fourth Year," said Vopiscus. "Cyrene."

Nobody said agreed. There was no clerk at the table who kept Cyrene's book. Cyrene's book was at Cyrene, in the enemy's hands, with its quaestor dead in the town and the town's stone half cut.

"No return," said Vopiscus, and turned the page.

Lucan ruled a short line where the figure would have gone. Under it stood a line he had entered himself from the Navarch's last report but one: the sea-watch on the Kettle Bank, lost, by fire, in a night.

"Silverwood. The Survey's clerk for the wood: dead. No return." Vopiscus did not change his voice. Under it was the relief sent to the wood in the quarter: seven ships, and the stock they had put ashore under the mist, all of it entered lost.

Lucan ruled another line.

The Teeth came last. Vopiscus read them from the sheet Lucan had written out himself before the clerks came up, from the packet and from the black ship's book, and he read them in the voice he had read the islands in, which was the voice he read everything in: the ships by their names, and what had become of each, and what the sea had taken, which the Survey entered at nothing, since nothing could be raised from it. Down the table the clerks copied the names into their margins as they were read, because the form had a place for them. *The Navarch: taken. Silverwood's stone, in its lead: lost; how, not known. The wood: not burned.* And one line that was no business of the Survey's, which Vopiscus read because it was on the sheet: *The chair: not brought.*

Then the men held at Cyrene, by Cyrene's own count. Lucan had entered that figure himself, at the window, and did not need to hear it again. He let it be read. Down the table a pen stopped, and went on.

Then Vopiscus drew the last book out from under the others.

It was smaller than the Survey's books, and bound in plain black, with nothing on its spine: no closed hand, no title, no number. Every clerk at the table knew it by sight, and none of them looked at it. Vopiscus opened it at the quarter's page, and Lucan watched his thick finger go down the inner margin past the names, which were written very small and close in their columns, by place, a great many of them, without stopping at any, to the foot of each column, where the figures were.

"The first line," said Vopiscus, and read what had been drawn on it in the quarter, and what remained. "The second line." What had been drawn; and then a third figure, which was how far the draw had run over the census set for it; and he read that in the same voice as the rest, and waited a moment after it, the way a clerk waits who has read out a figure that may be asked about.

Nothing was asked. He went on.

"The third line." He read it. "The fourth line. Nothing drawn."

Lucan entered a nought. The fourth line was Tiberius's. It had drawn nothing since the day it was opened, which made it the cheapest line he kept; and he had never once been pleased by it.

He had taken the totals of that book every quarter since there had been a book, and never read a name in it. He had ordered it kept so himself, and it was the only book in the Spire that was. What the lines were drawn for, and what became of what was drawn, and why a draw should run over its census in a quarter when nothing had been asked of the one who drew it—

Vopiscus signed at the foot of the page, and closed the black book, and laid it under the others.

Lucan added the foot of his own page.

He added it twice, which was the rule for a quarter, and a third time, which was his own rule for a figure he had not expected; and it came out the same each time. For four years the foot of that page had closed. Every quarter since Thessa the losses had been made good inside the quarter they were lost in, with something over, and he had not had to write at the head of a new page a figure that belonged to an old one. This one did not close. The Teeth were too large for it. They had been too large for it when he first entered them, at the window, before the room was lit; he had added it then too, with the packet in his other hand.

He wrote the difference at the foot, very small, and did not rule the line beneath it.

Down the table nobody looked at him. They were Survey clerks. They knew what a foot without a line under it meant.

"The postings," said Vopiscus, "for the coming quarter," and read them: names and places, the Survey's people going where they were sent, a step up or a step along; a young man to Hollowmere, a woman to the works at Bastion; and in the middle of the list, in the same voice as the rest, "Quaestor Plautia Rusca, to the west. Stoneheart."

Lucan knew the name from her returns: a neat hand, and figures that agreed the first time. He heard the list to its end. Then he looked once more at the difference at the foot of his page, which a stone would carry, and laid down his pen.

The room heard it. Down the long table the pens stopped, and the clerks waited with their cold hands flat on their books; and Vopiscus turned his head, for the first time that morning, and looked at him.

"Stoneheart," said Lucan. "The stone, by the spring audit."

Vopiscus wrote it at the head of the Survey's instructions for the west, in his square plain hand, and turned the book on the table so that Lucan could read it.

*Stoneheart: the stone, by the spring audit.*

Lucan nodded once. He did not ask how. How was the Survey's; it was what the Survey was for. Vopiscus sanded the line and tipped the sand back into its tray, and down the table, one after another, the pens began again.

---

His study lay across the landing from the audit room, and was colder, because he kept no brazier in it. He worked better cold. It had one window, which looked down on the academy, and one door besides the door to the landing, which was shut; and a table with nothing on it but a lamp, the ink, the sand-box and a jug of water. Through the door across the landing he could hear the pens, which was the sound the Survey made when it was well.

The academy lay under the window as it had lain for two winters, roofless and black, its long walls standing open to the weather and the sleet going through them. Along the north range the towers stood with their stairs still in them and nothing at the top, and below them the ground went down in black terraces to the sea, which was the color of slate today and came in under the cliff in long slow rollers out of the Grey. He had left all of it as it was. A ruin cost nothing to keep, and a clearance would have cost a season; and the Spire had been raised out of what the fire left of the archive in one, which was a figure he had been pleased with at the time.

The black glass of his own walls he had had left dull, so that he was not followed about his rooms all day by a thin old man in black.

Cluvius brought the petitions up at the fourth hour. He was a narrow grey man who had been Lucan's secretary since before the war, and in all that time he had not brought a paper up the stair in the wrong order. There were six this morning. Five were the ordinary business of the quarter, and Lucan minuted them as Cluvius laid them down, a word or two in the margin of each, with his free hand drawn up into his sleeve against the cold. The harbor-master at Thessa wanted more stock for the new mole, and could have it in the spring. Two towns on the north coast at home asked to have their lamp-oil at the guild's price, and could not. A smith's widow at Lessa had sold the Survey nails in the summer and had not been paid; she was paid. A magistrate inland had hanged a man for a thing that was not a hanging matter, and wished to know whether he had done right; he had not, and would be told so, and would pay the family out of his own stipend, because a hanging that was not owed was a debt run up in the crown's name. And the Treasury asked, as it asked each quarter, whether the king would come home for Midwinter, and was given the answer it was always given, in one word. By the end of them his free hand had gone numb inside the sleeve.

The sixth was on the paper of the Guild of the Lamps, which smelled faintly of the oil it was stored with. Everything the guild owned did.

*To the King. From Aemilia Scaura, for the Guild of the Lamps, at the capital.*

*Majesty. My brother Lartius Scaurus, Navarch of the Southern Sea, is a prisoner at Cyrene. I ask that he be brought home, by exchange, or by ransom, or by any road Your Majesty thinks fit. The guild will bear what it can.*

*I have kept the lamps of this city a long time, for other women's sons. I would rather not keep one for my brother.*

The hand was large and plain, without a flourish in it anywhere. The guild tied a tag to every lamp it tended, with a name on it and the day the year was out, and she had written a great many of them; it showed. She wrote as if each word had to be read from the street. There had been no such guild when Lucan came to the throne. It kept the lamps in half the streets of the capital now, at a fair price, and paid for itself.

Pinned behind it was a letter from the Navarchy at Thessa, under the Navarchy's seal, from the officer who held the Southern Sea in the Navarch's absence. The Navarchy asked leave to act in the matter of its Navarch, and of its men and its hulls, held at Cyrene. Folded inside the letter was a second sheet under a second seal, endorsed in the same hand: *Particulars, for His Majesty's eye, if His Majesty wishes it.*

Lucan read the petition once and the letter once. Then he sat a moment with the sister's sheet under his hand.

The Navarch had been the best clerk the Navarchy ever had. He wrote everything down. He had made his name at the Teeth, on his first command, with a lead box and a still night, and Lucan had seen him once afterward: a long young man who came up the stair at home with his own book under his arm, and read his report out of it standing, because he had not been asked to sit, and did not once look up from the page until he reached the foot of it. Lucan had given him the Southern Sea before he was out of the room. He had lost both, the name and the sea, at the same reef, on a morning, to a drunk with a penal cohort.

"The lady's answer, Majesty?" said Cluvius.

"The Navarch is entered as a loss."

Cluvius's pen did not move at once. He was a careful man, and he had sat through the audit. "He is held, Majesty," he said. "At Cyrene. He is living."

"A loss is a figure," said Lucan.

A figure could be entered, and a line ruled under it, and the page turned. A man held in a fishing town could not. He was a line left open, which would want carrying from quarter to quarter at a cost nobody could name, until somebody else decided what he was; and Lucan did not keep lines open for other people to close.

Cluvius wrote it down, on the form for the answer to a petition, which had a place for the answer and no place for anything else.

The Navarchy's letter Lucan minuted himself, across its foot, in his own hand.

*The Navarchy may act, at its own cost.*

The second sheet he did not open. He turned it over once in his fingers. The wax was green and cold, with a galley's beak pressed into it, and someone at Thessa had pressed it very hard. He laid it on top of the letter, seal up, unbroken; and Cluvius, who had been his secretary long enough, took the two up together and did not ask. A seal unbroken was a thing he had not been told. Whatever the Navarchy meant to do, it would do out of its own books, and whatever it spent would come to him at the end, if it came at all, as one line at the foot of somebody else's page. If it spent men, they would be the Navarchy's men. It was the cheapest leave he had ever given.

"Thank you, Cluvius."

Cluvius took the petitions down the stair with the Navarchy's particulars on top, and the green seal whole.

---

When the stair was quiet he unlocked the bottom drawer of his table, the only one with a lock, and took out his own book.

It was not a Survey book. There was no hand on it. It was the latest of the Aeridor books, which he had kept since he was a young man in his brother's treasury with a lamp and other men's figures; the others stood on the shelf behind him, thirty years of them, with a famine in every fourth winter, the oldest with their spines gone soft as cloth. He kept the newest in the drawer because the drawer locked, and because of what lay under it.

The drawer smelled of dust and old paper. He did not take out what lay at the bottom of it. He knew what it said: a half-sheet in a poor hand: so many measures of wheat, received, against one shard of the stone of Thessa; and at the foot the name of Thessa's lord, who had not written his own name often enough in his life to do it well. Folded under it was the letter Thessa's lord had sent across with the shard, so that whoever had the shard should know he had asked his own king first: the crown's apologies, on excellent paper. It was excellent paper. Lucan had always thought so.

The shard had gone to the Teeth in a lead box, and Aeridor's fleet had followed it there.

He opened the book at the Aeridor pages, and warmed his writing hand a moment under his arm, and entered the packet under the capital, in the small upright hand that looked the same at the end of a day as at the beginning.

*The harbor bell rung in daylight: the first time since the year of Thessa.*

*The* Audit *berthed in the first of the royal berths, the* Concord*'s. Renamed: the* Answer.

He looked at that a moment. The Navarchy named its ships out of the ledger, and the boy had answered one. It was a good name. He would not have chosen it, and he could not fault it.

Three lines above it stood a figure he had not had to touch since the day he wrote it: *Royal berths under the Old Hill, empty: thirty-one.* He drew one line through the figure, and wrote above it: *thirty.* He had the names of the ships that had lain in them as well, in the oldest of the books on the shelf, with their masters and their burden, in a young man's hand. He had never said them aloud. There had been no reason to.

*The council to thank the prince in public; then to ask who gave him leave.*

He could have written that minute himself. Aeridor's council stood on the shelf behind him, every year of it, and it had never yet thanked a man without asking him afterward who gave him leave.

*The king refused the nephew the packet, and the Navarch.*

The packet called its writer the nephew all the way through, as if there were another hand in the isles that might be taken for that one. In the months Lucan had been buying it, Aurelian had refused it nothing. A king who had begun to refuse his nephew things was a line to be watched; Lucan made a small mark in the margin against it, which was how he watched a line.

Then he turned back to the page for the royal house.

*Aurelian. King.* Under it, *Titus. Heir.*, and three lines of Lucan's own, the best he had ever written of an Aeridorian: *Reads ground. Asks for what he needs. Is refused.* After them, in the ink of a few weeks ago: *Dead, at Hollowmere.*

And under Titus, the line that had been the second son's.

*Heir: none of consequence.*

The verdict had stood on the second son's line since the stair, three years ago. He had moved it up a line himself when Hollowmere was entered, and seen no reason to change a word of it in the moving; a drunk who had run from a stair was none of consequence wherever he stood on the page. Lucan had the name of the tavern.

He read the quarter's lines again, the packet's and his own. A fishing town retaken with a cohort out of a harbor prison, and held. A wood held, and signed to the crown. A Navarch taken with his squadron at a reef that could not be passed, and a Tharsian three-banker sent across the Grey to lie in the first royal berth, so that a whole city came down to the water to look at her; and a council that would have to thank the boy for it in public before it could ask him anything at all.

*None of consequence* had been the right entry since the stair. It was not the right entry now.

He drew one line through it, cleanly, so that it could still be read. Above it, small, he wrote:

*Heir: the runner.*

---

At the sixth hour the books went back into their presses, and the cloth was laid on the audit table, because the quarter's dinner had been eaten off that table since the Spire was raised and nobody had found a reason to move it.

They sat down to it in their order: Vopiscus at the king's left, and below him the quaestors who had come in for the audit, and below them their deputies, a dozen living officers of the Survey in their black, with ink in the grain of their fingers that no soap had shifted in years. The sleet had stopped. Through the tall windows the harbor lay flat and grey, and the stone on the quay had gone dark with the wet; and beyond the mole, all along the west, the Grey stood up out of the sea without a top or an end to it, so close in this weather that the black ship at her anchor seemed to lie against it. A white sun hung low over it and put no warmth through the glass. Nobody at the table looked at the black ship.

The steward and two boys brought the dishes in. A saddle of mutton roasted with garlic and rosemary, the fat crackled brown and running into the dish; an eel pie with pepper in the crust; a coil of black sausage, split and fried; and the white bread of the home granaries, warm, in baskets. The smell went down the table ahead of the dishes, meat and garlic and hot fat and the sharp green of the rosemary, and got into the room's own smell of oil and ink and wet wool and sat on top of it; and when the pie was cut, pepper, and the brown smell of the river the eels had come out of. The officers' faces changed when it reached them. They had been at their books since the first hour.

The wine was the red of the home vineyards, so dark in the cups that it looked black, and there was a jug of it at every second elbow. At Lucan's elbow the steward poured from another jug, and nobody remarked on it. The steward was an old soldier who had to turn his whole body to look at anything, and had poured for him since the old palace. Once, in the Spire's first winter, a young deputy had looked from the king's cup to his own and opened his mouth, and Vopiscus had trodden on his foot. The young man had been posted to Bastion the next quarter, which was preferment, and had nothing to do with it, though the Survey had never believed so.

Lucan ate two slices of the mutton, cut thin, and some bread. He tasted all of it: the garlic, and the resin of the rosemary, and the faint tallow at the edge of the fat where it had caught; and he stopped when he had had enough. The water came up from the cistern in a stone jug. It tasted of the stone it was kept in, and it was very cold, and it found the bad tooth on the left side of his jaw; he drank it all the same.

The Survey did not drink in the field. It was proud of that. At the king's quarter table it drank, because the wine was poured and to leave it would be a discourtesy; and it drank carefully at first, as if the cups might be audited. He watched the wine go into them the way damp gets into a ledger, unseen, until the pages will not lie flat.

They talked of the Survey's business, because there was nothing else they all knew. Ofella, who kept Hollowmere's book, told the table that the ford road at Hollowmere had been paved to the town in a month, all of it by night, so that the town had woken every morning to another stretch of it and never seen a stone laid; and the table was pleased, because it was true, and because it was theirs. The Navarchy had had a bad quarter, and the Survey, which had had a bad quarter too, found that a comfort. The quaestor beside Ofella said the Navarchy would want hulls by the spring, and men to row them, and would have to come to the Survey for the men, and the Survey would see what stock it could spare. Down the table they laughed, because the Navarchy rowed with the living and was proud of it, as the Survey was proud of its water. A deputy from Thessa said that the Navarch had been a fine clerk, whatever else he had been; and they drank to him, as if he were dead. Somebody said he would be writing it all down wherever they had put him, and would have the fishing town's census done by Midwinter, and that went down well too.

Below Ofella sat a young quaestor called Tadius, who had come out from home on the last boat before the audit and been read out that morning for Hollowmere. It was his first posting west. He told the end of the table about his wife, who had wept when the letter came and then gone out and told the whole street, because her father had been a clerk of the roads all his life and never been sent west; and while he told it he turned his cup round and round on the cloth by its foot, and did not drink from it. Then he asked Ofella what he ought to write home about Hollowmere.

Ofella had been at Hollowmere since the ford. She considered him.

"That it's cold," she said, and passed him the sausage.

Nobody who came home from the west described it; Tadius would learn that by the end of his first winter. He laughed, uncertainly, and took some of the sausage, and drank.

By the second cup he had stopped turning it. He had been at the Spire less than a week, and had not yet learned how far a voice carried in a room with a black floor. In a pause, while the steward was taking away the mutton, he leaned out past the man beside him and spoke up the table to the king.

"When it's settled, Majesty," he said. "Out there. All of it." His hand went out toward the windows, and the Grey. "When the Calm's come to the west, and there's nothing left to settle. What will the Survey do then?"

The table went still. Ofella looked at her plate. Vopiscus set his cup down without a sound, which in a heavy man was a kind of noise.

Lucan looked down the table at the young man for a moment: at his cup, and his clean cuffs, and the ink on his fingers.

"Count," he said.

They laughed. It was a little too loud, and it went on a little too long, and Tadius laughed hardest of them all, with relief, and lifted his cup.

"To the Calm, then," he said. "Out there."

They drank to it, out there, all down the table; and Lucan lifted his water, and drank to it too.

Vopiscus drank with them. He sat with his back to the windows; he had sat so at every quarter's table. His son had been quay stock since the winter the Spire was raised, and the quay was under them. Below the windows, below the quay, under the flat grey water of the harbor where they had walked down at first light, the night's men stood in their rows in the cold and waited for dark.

---

He sent the boat out for her at noon, and she came up the stair at the ninth hour, when the light was going.

The afternoon went as the afternoons of audit days went. The quarter's totals were copied fair, and the fair copies read against the foul, line by line, by two clerks who did not like each other and were kept together for that reason; the Survey's instructions for the west were sealed and sent down to the boat; and the clerks who had come in from the islands went down the stair in ones and twos to find their berths for the night. The wind had gone round into the north while they were at table and come on to blow, and all afternoon the black ship had pitched at her anchor beyond the mole with her lamps swinging along the rail. He had not had the study's lamps lit. There was light enough for what he had to say.

Lucan heard her come before he saw her: Cluvius's voice on the landing, saying the form of words, and the guards at the stair-head shifting their feet. They were men of the garrison who had drawn the lot for Corvus that year, and would not draw it again. They looked at the wall while she went by. A draught came up the stair with her and laid the flames over in the landing lamps; and Cluvius showed her in, and shut the door, and she stood just inside it with the last of the window's light on her face.

She had not changed; the dead did not. Black hair, with the two silver pins in it. Black silk worked with gold, in a pattern that moved when he did not look at it directly and lay still when he did. The violet eyes she had had alive, and kept. He supposed she was beautiful. He had been told so often enough to have entered it.

She held her right hand out from her side. The first two fingers and the thumb were grey to the second joint, a dry dull grey, like sealing-wax that has been melted too many times to take a mark. From her other wrist the thread hung over the back of her hand. He counted the lights on it before she had taken a step, and they came to thirty-six: the small ones, quick and bright, that moved on the thread all the time, and the larger ones, dimmer and steady, that did not move. She smelled of her ship, of tar and of the cold oil of the lamps that burned along her rail by day.

"Nine days," said Livia. "I counted them, Lucan. I thought you'd want them counted."

He did not answer. He had his pen in his hand, dry, over the page, and he left it there.

She waited to see if he would. She did not sit; she never sat in his rooms unless he told her to, and he did not tell her. Then she came a few steps into the room, and told it.

She told it well. It had been her gift at Corvus, when she was alive and a chair and talked to rooms of the young about books they would never read. She told it as a story is told to a table, with places left open in it where a listener would catch his breath; and he left them open. At first light on the morning of the Teeth she had gone into the bow of the prince's ship, in the middle of his fleet, and taken the little chair out of it, and nobody had seen her do it but an elf, who had shot a marine for it. She had taken her across to the Navarch's ship, and put her hands in the Corvus irons: the old pair out of the glass case in the long gallery, with the card on them.

"You'd have liked the card," said Livia.

She had meant to bring her to him by sunset, as he had asked. And then the prince had come through the Teeth on the young flood, with his ships and his drum and his fish-people, through a reef that could not be passed.

"The Navarch said so," said Livia. "He wrote it down."

She had watched it from the stern windows. The prince's people had made the sea go round: the whole pool behind the reef turning on itself, slowly, with the Navarch's ships in it, and something in the water under them that she had not been able to see. She told him that part with real pleasure. He did not enter the pleasure. He entered the sea going round, and the something under it, and that she had not been able to see what it was.

While they were coming through, the chair had heated the irons. With her own fire, from the inside, until the pins went soft. No rod.

"I always said she could do it without the rod." Something came into Livia's voice when she said it. He left it out of the account.

When the ships met and it was time to go, she had taken the chair by the wrist to bring her away, and the iron at the wrist had been hot. She lifted the grey hand an inch, as evidence, and let it fall. So the chair had broken an iron off on the corner of the Navarch's table and stood there with fire in her bare hand; and Livia had come home.

She held up the thread and let the lights swing.

"It cost me two," she said.

He had counted them already.

First light, she had said; and the Teeth forced on the young flood. There had been the best part of a morning between the two. The errand as he had given it had wanted an hour of that morning at the most: somewhere quiet, gently, and done, and then raised, and brought to him, to sit at this table and be asked what she knew. Livia had asked him for the errand, in the way she asked for things, so that he might think the giving had been his own idea; he had let her think he thought so, and given it to her because she asked. He did not ask why the chair had still been alive at the young flood. He had never asked any of them anything of that kind, in any quarter, and it was not because he could not guess.

A guess was not an entry.

Livia did not like the silence. She had never liked a silence in a room. She went to the window and looked down at the academy for some time, at the long black walls with the dusk coming into them, and the towers, and the gold moved in her silk while she stood there. She stood there longer than there was anything to see. He supposed she was looking for the long gallery, which had run along the side that faced the sea, and was not there. Then she came back, and touched his sand-box with one grey finger, and turned it a little on the table, so that it was not square.

"I told her why it burned," she said. "Corvus. Something on a shelf. I told her in the cabin, while we waited. I thought she ought to know what she was for." She watched his face. "She was Chair of the catalogue, Lucan. She had her hand on every card in that building. She'll work it out. She'll have it in a column by the spring, and wait till summer to be sure, because she always did."

Something under his breastbone went cold and small, and stayed so.

He had burned the shelf, and the building round the shelf, because something had been on it. He did not know what. He had not known then, and had burned it all the same, because a thing he could not count was a thing he could not let stand. Afterward he had had the chairs counted, the living and the dead, and the count had come to one short. He had sent for the one. He was still at one.

He felt it, as a living man feels a thing. He felt all of it, and gave her none.

She was watching for it. He knew what she watched for in a face: the moment a thing understood that it was hers. He gave her his face, which was a clean page with nothing written on it, and she read it from the top to the foot, and found nothing there, and read it again.

"I told her you'd be cross," said Livia. "When I left. I said, Lucan is going to be so very cross with me." She smiled. "Are you?"

The first line in the black book was hers. It had been hers since Corvus was entered, and it had never run over; she was careful with it, as she was careful with her lights.

He dipped his pen at last. He drew a half-sheet toward him and wrote one line on it for Vopiscus, and sanded it, and tipped the sand back into the box, and set the box square again, and laid the half-sheet face down on the table. Then he looked at her.

"You will draw nothing until the ledger balances. Close the door."

For a moment she did not move at all.

He had never seen her do that. He had not known she could. The small bright lights on her thread went on moving, all along it; and the rest of her stood quite still in the middle of his floor, in the last of the light, with her grey hand held out from her side and her face turned toward him and nothing in it.

Then she went out, and closed the door behind her, softly, with her good hand.

---

When the lamps had been lit he added the foot of the page once more, alone and without hurrying, in case the figures had been waiting for the room to empty. They had not. He turned to a clean page for the new quarter and wrote the difference at the head of it, and beside it a word he had not had to write at the head of any page since Thessa.

*Carried.*

He sat and looked at it while the ink dried.

Nothing in his books was meant to be carried long. Every figure he had ever entered was owed somewhere and would be paid somewhere, and the whole of the work, the stones and the Survey and the war, the roads paved by night, the Settling of the isles, was only the paying of it, quarter by quarter, until the day came when there would be nothing on any page that was owed to any other: every book closed, and nothing carried, and nothing owed, anywhere, ever again. He had a word for that day, the Balance, and he had never said it aloud, or written it in any book.

He had always known it was a long way off. He had not known, until today, that it could go further.

He put the book away and locked the drawer. He poured his water from the jug on the corner of the table, and took the cup in one hand and the day-book in the other, and went through the other door.

It was the coldest room on the floor, and kept so. Old Tullus stood by the door in a quilted coat and mittens; he was older than either of them now, and his hands shook when the cold got into them, and he would not sit down. There were shelves on three walls, and on the shelves twenty years of day-books in their order, the first of them from the old palace at home, with their spines gone pale. He had never taken one down again. There was nothing in any of them that had not been read aloud once already, to the only listener they had ever had. The window in the fourth wall gave on the quay, where the night's work had begun. He could hear it through the glass: the long scrape of a sledge on wet stone, and the knock of stone set down on stone, and no voices.

His brother sat with his back to the window.

Tullus had put him in the winter wool tonight, the dark grey with fur at the collar, and passed the razor over his face, as he did every morning, the razor he had used on him alive, though in twenty years it had found nothing to take; and combed his hair, and set his hands on the arms of the chair, and laid a rug over his knees, though there was no cold he could feel. The wool smelled of the cedar press it had come out of. His eyes rested on the floor in front of his feet. In the lamplight he looked as he had looked on the night he died, except that his face was calm; and it had never once been calm while he lived. It had wept at figures. It had wept at the famine figures in the bad winters, every one, and promised, and promised, and never done the sum. Lucan was older now than his brother had lived to be, by some years. He had passed him one winter without remarking it.

"He's in the winter wool, Majesty," said Tullus.

"Good."

Lucan sat down in his own chair across from him, and felt the cold of the room in his knees, and drank. The water was very cold, and found the tooth. He opened the day-book on his knee.

He read him the day.

He read it in the voice he used for everything, which was the voice the Survey read in; Vopiscus had learned it from him a long time ago, and neither of them had ever said so. He read him the Survey's quarter, island by island, Thessa first; and the granaries at home, which were full; and the price of lamp-oil at the capital, which had gone up in the quarter, as it went up in any quarter with a sea in it. He read him the petitions and the answers to them, and the postings, and the line for the west, and the line he had sent down to Vopiscus at the ninth hour. He read him the Teeth as Vopiscus had read them in the morning, ship by ship, without hurrying: the *Surety*, on the reef; the *Impost*, burned, as she had been fitted to be; the *Toll*, burned and sunk, with the fire-hulk that was driven onto her, a hulk the Navarchy had never named because it had been bought to be burned; the *Audit*, and the *Levy*, and the *Tithe*, and the *Arrears*, taken. The Navarch, taken. Silverwood's stone, lost. The wood, not burned. The chair, not brought.

His brother did not look up. He never did.

Lucan turned the page.

"Held at Cyrene," he read, "by Cyrene's own count. Four hundred and twelve."

There was a place here.

There had always been a place, after a figure like that. Once, at a figure like that, his brother would have said something. He would have been sorry out loud, at length, with his hands; he would have wanted to know whether they were warm, and whether they were fed, and whether anyone had written to their mothers. It would have been foolish, and kind, and it would have cost something, and in the morning Lucan would have gone down to the treasury and found out what.

He waited. He always waited, here.

Nothing came.

His brother's hands lay on the arms of the chair, where Tullus had put them. Below the window a sledge went by on the quay, and stone knocked on stone, and nobody spoke. Tullus, at the door, did not move.

Lucan drank his water. He did not enter it.

He read him the rest.

# Chapter Two: The Cold Grounds

**MARCUS**

Old Baebius voted against the ham.

He did it on his feet, with the snow still melting on the shoulders of his cloak and his hand in the air, exactly as he did it on the sea-gate roof whenever the council sat. The length of the table, which had been smelling the ham since the snow began, groaned at him.

"It's the last one," he said. "Out of the smokehouse. You don't eat the last one."

"Then what do you do with it?" said Vindex the cooper.

"Keep it."

"Till when?"

Old Baebius gave him the look his Lanciarii got when they asked what a drill was for. "Till it's wanted."

"It's wanted," said Rufo.

"The town," said Orso Netmender from the far end, with his mouth already full of bread, "says eat it."

"Crown's seat says no," said Old Baebius. "On principle."

Half the table said *on principle* with him, which he ignored. Rufo counted the hands, from habit, and did not trouble to give the total. Baebius sat down. Then, since he had been outvoted, and since a thing that was going to be eaten might at least be cut properly, he drew his knife and pulled the board to him and carved it himself.

They ate in the garrison's old mess under the north wall, a long low vaulted room next to the stable, where the spring came up under that side of the hill and kept the flagstones warm enough to stand on in stockings. The council had sat on the sea-gate roof that afternoon while the first snow of the winter came down on the hill. It had come up to eat afterward, as it did now on council days, because Marcus had asked it to once and no one had thought to stop. Through the wall Thistle kicked a board, which she did whenever Roderic was near and had not come to see her. Tam the stable-boy, who was Orso's son, whistled at her through his teeth, and she kicked it again.

The table was the garrison's, black oak polished by Tharsian elbows, with the white closed hand of Tharsis burned into the middle of it as big as a shield. Out of the hand, since the first night, one finger stood up. Fingers had improved it with his knife, and it had never been planed off, and Gaius had looked at it once through his lenses and said that the cut was very clean.

Baebius carved thin, against the grain. The slices came off the blade in leaves the lamplight showed through, rose at the heart and gold at the rind, with a rim of white fat that tasted of salt and oak smoke and the cold of the garrison's cellar. He laid the first slice on Marcus's trencher without a word, and the table forgave him.

After the ham there was ling, the first out of the cold grounds, stewed in the big copper with leeks and barley until it fell apart in white flakes and the room smelled of the sea and of onion; and Fausta's dark bread, which came up the hill every afternoon in a basket on a boy's back and was still warm when it reached the gate. There was the Tharsian red out of the *Levy*'s stores, thin and sour and tasting faintly of its cask, and the council drank it as men drink a thing they have taken off an enemy: with satisfaction, and no pleasure.

Marcus's cup had water in it. It always did now. Felix saw to it before the benches filled, so that there was nothing to watch.

It was the spring's water, and it tasted, as it always had, of iron and of eggs a day too old. *The water is foul,* he had written once to his father, on a scrap folded into a report. *I am drinking it.* He was still drinking it.

When they first came home from the Teeth the table had gone quiet whenever the water came round, for the length of a breath, while men looked at his cup and then took care to look at something else. He had minded that. Tonight Orso reached across him with the red for Vindex, talking about ling, and poured it past his elbow without a check, and went on talking. Marcus had been waiting for the check. It had not come. He minded that too.

He drank his water. The door he had bricked up was where he had left it. He did not go and lean on it to see.

Across the table Sylara had watched him wait. She said nothing. She reached over and took his bread, since he was not eating it, and he let her.

"Thick out there," Orso was saying. "The ling. Proteas says you could walk to the Grey on their backs." He wiped his beard. "My grandfather wouldn't go past the Kettle after the leaves came down, not for ling, not for anything. The water stops a man's heart, he used to say, and the weather comes off the Grey with no more warning than a slap."

"He went past it every winter of his life," said Prisca the fisher-wife, without looking up from her bowl. "He just never told your grandmother."

"Just as well they're thick," said Vindex. "Four hundred of them down there on the quay, and every one of them eats like a cooper."

"Half the boys in the town," said Valeria, "are behind the net-lofts with broom handles, drilling at each other." She tore her bread in two. "Badly."

"So was I, at their age," said Roderic.

"You had a horse."

"I had a broom handle and a goat, my dear. The goat was the better soldier."

Marcus listened to that and thought about spears. Four in five of them on this island were the town's, by his own word. Sooner or later somebody would have to show the town which end went forward.

Gaius came in when the ham was half gone, with sawdust in his hair and his lenses pushed up into it. He sat down in the place that had been kept for him and began to eat as if he had that moment remembered how. Liora, beside him, picked a long curl of oak shaving out of his hair and laid it by his plate.

"She's had the last of the iron," said Gaius, to the ling.

Marcus waited.

"The bloom the garrison left in the undercroft. The smith drew the last of it this afternoon. Her rudder-irons, and the nails I can't do with treenails, and that's the end of it." He ate. "After her, everything waits on iron."

"Then we'll find iron."

Gaius looked at him over the lenses, which had come down onto his nose of their own accord, with the face he kept for a joint that looked sound and might not be. Then he went back to his stew.

Beside Marcus, Alana had the weather-book open by her plate and was writing the snow into it, with the hour, as if the snow might want to dispute it later. From the first of next week she was to have a room at the Baths for the town's children, to teach them their letters. She had told him so in bed three nights ago, in the voice she used for a bearing she had already checked twice, as though he might argue. He had not argued. He had not been sure, at the time, that she was asking.

After supper he went up on the wall.

The snow was coming down thick and slow out of the dark, and the wall-walk was white with it and squeaked under his boots, and the cold took hold of his face the moment he stepped out of the stair. Below him the town went down the hill in steps of white roofs to the harbor, and at the bottom of it the long quay ran out under its torches. The prisoners were at their supper there. They sat on the warm stones along the steam-box wall in a line that went the length of the quay, with their bowls in their laps, while Ancus the garrison's old cook stood over the town's pots with a ladle and the town's women stood over Ancus, and complained, and filled the bowls. A boy came down Netmakers' Row with a basket on his back and set it down at the end of the line and ran back up the hill. The bread went up the line from hand to hand.

Between the roofs the gutters steamed. Even now, with the snow on them, there were children sitting along the curbs with their feet in the warm water, and somewhere a mother was calling one of them in, and the child was not coming.

The snow came down over all of it. Where it met the steam it went out; and in the whole of the lower town not one flake of it reached the street.

There was ice in the bilges of the boats on the hard that morning, and none on the slips.

The slips never froze. The culvert ran down to them from the fort through the rock, under the town, and broke out hot into the two steam-boxes at the head of the yard, and in cold weather the whole yard smoked like a cookhouse. Men worked there in their shirts. The prisoners worked there in less.

When Marcus came down, a strake was coming out of the first steam-box: a plank of oak as long as a house, limp as a wet hide, with the steam pouring off it. A gang of Tharsians under it in a line ran it up the slip to the hull before it could cool and set. Their own man called it for them in Tharsian, *and one, and two, and one*, and they ran to the count with their bare feet slapping on the warm stone. Gaius was waiting at the hull on the staging with a maul, and Arruns and Velthur with the clamps. The plank went up against the frames and was clamped and wedged and pinned while it still smoked.

They did everything to a count, the Tharsians. They hauled to it and rowed to it and, as far as Marcus could tell, ate to it. Orso said it put him off his food. Gaius said it was the best thing about them.

The yard smelled of hot wet oak, somewhere between new bread and vinegar; and of pitch from the kettle by the second slip; and under both, as everywhere in Cyrene, of the spring. The steam off the box-door wet his face as he passed, and was cold on his skin a little way on.

She stood on Slip One in her frames and half her planking. Her frames were doubled and staggered, each pair set a little off its neighbor, so that wherever one had a joint the next had sound timber. Gaius had read that off the *Concord* on the reef, by the light of the sea-people's jars, in the little time the *Forward* had going over her. From the head of the slip she looked like a file of the Lanciarii closed up two deep, every man covering the gap in the man in front. *She'll last a hundred years,* Gaius had said, the night he laid her keel. *If nobody burns her.*

He came down the staging ladder when he saw Marcus, rung by rung, with his lenses down and his boxwood rule in his belt and pitch to the elbows.

"Nerva's been," he said.

"What did he say?"

"Nothing. He comes down every morning at first light and stands at the foot of the slip and looks at her mast-step, and then he goes away." Gaius took his lenses off and rubbed them on his shirt, which made them worse. "Every morning."

"He thinks it's a hand too far forward."

"I know what he thinks. You told me." Gaius put the lenses back on. "He can tell me."

"I had a letter from the clerks," said Gaius, as they walked along under her.

"Which clerks?"

"The Navarch's. In the west port's tower." He sounded aggrieved. "They wrote on the back of one of Orso's fish tallies, offering to keep the yard's books. They say mine are the worst they have seen in the southern sea, and they have seen all of the southern sea's."

"Are they?"

"Yes." Gaius laid his palm flat on a new strake under the turn of her bilge, as if it had a pulse. "I said no. Orso would hang me. *Especially the clerks.*"

Arruns and Velthur had gone back to a strake on the trestles at the slip-foot, planing it, one from each end, and Marcus stood to watch. They worked toward each other with long even strokes, heads down, and the oak came off the two planes in curls that fell to the same beat. They met in the middle so exactly that the plane-irons all but touched. Then they turned the plank over between them and began again from the ends. They talked while they did it, low and unhurried, in half-sentences that nobody else in the yard could follow.

On the day of the slip gate they had put their spears into the same marine at the same moment. The cohort had not stopped talking about it since. The brothers never talked about it at all.

At noon the pot came down from the town, a stew of cod heads and barley with the fat lying on it in gold coins, and the yard knocked off to eat. The prisoners ate along the steam-box wall, where the heat came through into a man's back. Among them was a boy Marcus had watched come off the ships on the night they came home, in a rower's shirt and nothing else, with his arms wrapped round himself and his teeth going. He was warm now. He was complaining in Tharsian about the barley.

The marines ate apart from the rowers, on the steps at the head of the slip, in what was left of their red cloaks. They sat straight and complained about nothing; and one of them, a lean grey corporal, watched Marcus all the way down the slip and did not look away when Marcus looked back.

At the end of the line a man was asleep.

He sat with his back to the wall and his legs out in front of him and his bowl upright in his lap, full, with the spoon standing up in it. His chin was on his chest. He was asleep wholly, with his mouth a little open, at peace. A short thick man, with a rower's shoulders and a rower's cracked bare feet and a round mild face, a week unshaven.

Pacuvius, who had the yard that day with two of the Lanciarii, came along the wall and stood over him and reversed his lancea to stir him with the butt; and then saw Marcus, and did not.

"That's Tithe, Highness," he said.

Marcus looked at him.

"The one we found. The night you came in." The long hill-man's face did something that in another man would have been fond. "We come up one short, and went back down, and there he was, under a bench in the *Tithe*'s bilge. Been there since the morning. Slept through the end of the Teeth, near enough, and all the way home." He considered the sleeper. "He's been Tithe since. He doesn't mind."

As if he had heard his name, the man opened his eyes. He looked up at the two of them without alarm, and then down at his bowl, and seemed pleased to find it still there. He took the spoon out of it and began to eat.

"Highness," he said, through barley.

"You were asleep."

"Yes, Highness." He swallowed. "I'm a rower. You sleep when the drum stops."

"There's no drum here."

"No, Highness," said Tithe. He thought about that without resentment, and ate another spoonful. "So it's always stopped."

Pacuvius made a noise in his nose.

"At home I'm a cod man," said Tithe. "Ostra, on the south shore. My father had a boat." He looked past them down the yard to the harbor, and the gap, and the Grey beyond the gap, and his round face went thoughtful. "Put me in a boat, Highness. I never slept in a boat in my life."

"He sleeps in everything else," said Pacuvius.

"Not a boat. Not my own." Tithe scraped the bowl. "You'd be surprised."

Marcus said he would ask Orso. Tithe said thank you, Highness, as if that settled it. When Marcus had gone back up the slip and turned at the top to look at the hull, Tithe was asleep again against the warm wall, with the bowl empty and upright in his lap and the spoon in it.

"Push," said Tuccius. "Step. Push."

The boys pushed, and stepped, and pushed. There were two ranks of them at the seaward end of the long quay, where the stones were broad and flat and too far out for the steam from the gutters, so that the frost lay on them grey all morning. They were fishermen's sons and net-makers' sons and a cooper's grandson, the eldest perhaps sixteen and the youngest certainly not, with ash poles and boathooks for spears and hatch-covers and fish-baskets for shields. Kaeso stood at the end of the line with the crown's drum and beat it for them, slowly and gravely, because he was fourteen and had beaten it at the Teeth, and these were children.

The quay smelled of frost and fish-scales and the tar on the bollards, and the town came to watch every morning now, in order to say what it thought. The women at the net-loft windows called the step a beat ahead of the drum, so that the rear rank never knew which to follow. A grandmother with a basket of wet nets on her hip fell in behind the rear rank and marched there, upright, all the way to the end of the quay and back, to applause. Old Baebius stood at the head of the sea-gate steps with his arms folded and did not laugh at any of it. When the rear rank lost the step he called it for them once, on the drum, in the voice he used for the Lanciarii, and they found it.

"Push what?" somebody called from a window. "Push *who*?"

"Push," said Tuccius, who had been thirty years at sea before he was ever a lanciarius, and had been shouted at by better than net-wives. "Step. Push."

He walked behind the line as he said it, with his lancea for a stick. He walked well now. The spear the marine had put through his thigh on the *Audit*'s bridge had left nothing behind but a white mark and a stiffness on cold mornings, which Liora said would go and Tuccius said was weather. He had a square grey face like a split log, and a nose that had been broken so long ago it had healed into a shape of its own. When he was thinking he rubbed it with his thumb.

At the end of the quay, where the frost was thickest, a boy in the front rank went down.

His feet went from under him both together and he came down flat on his back with his hatch-cover on top of him, so hard that Marcus heard the breath go out of him from the sea-gate steps. The town laughed. Then it stopped laughing, because the boy stayed down, and because Tuccius had crossed the quay in a few strides, stiff leg and all, and was standing over him.

He did not help him up. He took him by the back of the collar and stood him on his feet in one movement, hatch-cover and all, and held him there until his feet were under him.

"I knew a lad went down on frost," he said. Not loudly. "Feet wider. Under your shoulders. Like that." He let go, and walked back to his place behind the line. "Push."

Marcus knew which lad. So did half the quay. Caelius had got up off the frost at the slip gate and died all the same, an hour later, on the long stepped lane, of a cut behind the knee he had shown nobody. Tuccius had carried the front end of the door. He had sat with him on the steps until it was done.

When the drill broke Marcus went down onto the quay. Tuccius saw him coming and straightened. He did not salute, because Marcus had asked the Lanciarii not to on the quay where the town could see it and make remarks.

"They're terrible," said Tuccius, before he could be asked.

"They're better than last week."

"They were terrible last week, Highness. Now they're terrible in step." He watched the boys go off up Netmakers' Row in a crowd, poking at one another with the ash poles. "They'll do. The big one with the boathook'll do. He doesn't like being laughed at."

"Good."

They stood and watched the boys go. Then Marcus looked up, past them, at the ridge of the biggest net-loft on the Row.

Someone had sat on it every morning that week: an elf, cross-legged on the ridge-tiles in a grey cloak, with an unstrung bow across her knees, looking out through the gap at the sea. She was old, the first old one of Faelar's hundred Marcus had seen close to, with a face all seams and weather, brown as an oar-loom a lifetime in use, and a white braid that had never been cut lying down her back and along the tiles. She had come down from the pines on the first morning of the snow and climbed onto Sylara's old roof. She had not said why, and had not been asked. Faelar had told Marcus her name, and that she had been a warden a long time, and that she had seen enough ships leave. He had said it as if it were a great deal.

"Maelis," said Marcus.

Tuccius looked up at the roof. His face did not change. It was not a face that went in much for changing.

"She's nothing to do all day but watch the sea," said Marcus. "Your boys could learn to watch it with her."

He had heard Tuccius on the *Forward*'s deck, with the fog down and Alana gone. *Who's to say who reads the elf's report?* He had seen what Rufo's face did then, and heard what Valeria said after. He had forgotten none of it. He said none of it now.

Tuccius rubbed his nose with his thumb.

"On purpose, Highness?" he said.

"Yes."

Tuccius went on looking at the roof. Then he nodded once, the way a pilot nods at a sounding he does not like and means to steer by anyway. He turned and went off along the quay after his boys, roaring for the big one with the boathook to come back here and bring the hatch-cover with him, it was the town's.

When Marcus looked up again, the ridge was empty.

He found her a little later at the end of the quay, standing beside Tuccius at the head of the steps. She had come down without being asked. Neither of them was looking at the other. They were both looking out through the gap at the Grey. Presently Tuccius pointed at something out there with his chin, and the old elf looked where he pointed, and said a word, and Tuccius grunted.

He went up to the Baths by the long stepped lane.

It was steep and narrow and walled on both sides, and climbed from the harbor in flights of worn steps with a turn at every landing, so that a few shields could have held it all day against a crowd; and the steam from the gutter that ran down beside it hung in the turns and wet the walls. Halfway up there was a step the town walked round. It had not been told to. It walked round it anyway, and Marcus, who knew why, walked round it too.

He heard the class from the foot of the last flight: a roomful of children talking over one another, in stone, which carries.

Liora had given Alana the long room at the back of the Baths, with the high windows and the channel running round the foot of its walls. The beds were gone from it now and the floor scrubbed, and on the warm flags in rows sat the children of Cyrene with trays of black sand from the beach across their knees, writing in it with sticks. After the lane the room was as warm as a bakehouse, and smelled of children and the spring.

"It was the long ward," said Liora. She had come to the door beside him with her sleeves rolled and her hair bound up in its cloth, smelling of the Baths' soap and of warm stone. She watched the children. "I wanted a noise in it."

Then she went back to her work, and Sennius went after her with her basin, and Marcus stood in the doorway and watched.

Alana went up and down the rows with her skirt gathered in one hand out of the sand and her violet sleeves pushed to the elbow, and ink on her cheek, though there was no ink in the room. She bent to every tray. *Again, Lucia. Better. Servius, your* s *is looking at the sea; turn it round to look at the hill. Septima, that is a very good* a. *Make another exactly like it. No. Exactly.* She knew every name. Marcus thought of a name he had written for her by itself at the bottom of a page of her weather-book, *Sextus*, the porter's boy at Corvus who had carried her books up a tower stair for four years; and of her telling him, with her eyes shut, that she had never once asked.

Septima wanted to know whether there had been as many books at Corvus as there were fish in the cold grounds.

"More," said Alana. "On shelves, up to the roof."

She said it lightly. Then, in the time it took Septima to pick up her stick, her face went somewhere Marcus could not follow and came back again; and she told Septima to make another *a*.

Tertulla was in the front row: the goatherd's girl from the hill, ten or so, with straw in her hair and a smell of goat about her that the warm room brought out. She was writing her own name in the sand with her tongue between her teeth and such ferocity that the boy beside her moved his tray away. She wrote it, and looked at it, and wiped it out with the flat of her hand, and wrote it again, harder.

"Why does she wipe it out?" said Marcus, when Alana came by the door at last.

"Because she can write it again." Alana pushed a strand of hair back with her wrist. "She's quicker than my students at Corvus were. Not quicker. Less frightened of being wrong; no one has ever told her it matters."

"Does it?"

"Not in sand," said Alana.

Then Vindex the cooper came in past him, small and bald and quick and pink from the climb, and looked round the rows.

"I've come about the x," he said.

Alana looked at him.

"With an x," said Vindex. "My name. I've told every herald in the southern sea to write it down, and never done it myself, and I'm not having that said of me." He looked at the trays. "Is there one going?"

There was. He sat down on his heels at the back among the children with a tray of black sand across his knees, and Alana knelt and showed him; and he made the two strokes, and looked at them.

"That's an x," said Alana.

"That's two sticks."

"That is what an x is."

The cooper considered it. Then he made another beside it, carefully, and another; and the boy next to him leaned across to look and said it was good, and Vindex told him to mind his own tray.

When the children had gone shouting down the long stepped lane into the dusk, Marcus helped her smooth the trays. You did it with the edge of a board, drawn across, and the black sand came level and fine and blank, ready for morning. Some of the letters were deep. Tertulla's had gone almost to the wood.

"It costs nothing," said Alana, beside him, smoothing. "Sand, and sticks, and a room Liora didn't want." She drew the board across. "Corvus had towers and an endowment and a porter on every stair. This does the same work." She laid the tray on the pile. "I keep waiting to find out what it costs."

He took a stick and wrote in the last tray before she could get to it.

She came round to his side to read it, because he had written it facing himself; and read it; and looked at him.

"Spelling," said Alana.

She smoothed it out with the edge of the board. But she did it slowly, and afterward she looked at the place a while, when it was only sand.

They went out to the cold grounds before dawn, with the hot water.

The town filled the skins at the culvert's mouth under the yards, where the spring came out of the rock too hot to hold a hand in: goatskins and old wineskins, stoppered and wrapped in fleece and stowed under the thwarts. They were for the sea-people's hands. Marcus carried two of them down to the hard himself, one under each arm, and felt them through the fleece against his ribs, heavy and live with heat.

He went because he had asked.

He had gone down to the foot of the water-gate stair the morning after the council said the prisoners would work, and stood on Nonius's bottom step, and asked. Old Pherusa had come up out of the dark water and listened to him with her face giving nothing away, and said what she always said.

*It is not our war.*

*It isn't a war,* he had said. *It's fish.*

She had looked at him as if he were a very young seal that had said something clever. Then Proteas had come up the steps beside her, a grey-haired hunter, broad and scarred and unhurried, whose son had gone down on the Kettle Bank in the autumn; and he had said that he would go. The others had come after him.

"If you drown," Valeria had said on the hard, in the dark, "I'm the one who has to write it up." Then she had held the bow of Orso's boat for him while he got in.

Orso's boat and the *Prisca* were to shoot the ring-net between them, and the town's other boats would work lines along the edge of the grounds. Orso had his own steering oar. Marcus had the third thwart on the larboard side, between a Tharsian off the *Levy* and a net-maker's son who kept apologizing for his elbows. Florus had the bow oar. Florus could not swim, and had asked to come anyway, and Marcus had not asked him why.

The *Prisca* lay alongside: Prisca's dead husband's boat, named for her, broad and old and patched in three colors, which Prisca had lent to the prisoners without anybody asking her to. Tithe had her steering oar. Orso had put him in a boat, as asked, and then in a better one, and then in this one. He had given him the oar on the fourth day and said nothing about it, which was as near as Orso came to praise. Tithe stood at it now in the dark with the loom under his arm, wide awake and grinning, the first man in the harbor ready to go.

"Look at him," said Orso. "He'll sleep tonight. Not before."

They pulled out through the gap in the first grey of the light. The harbor steamed behind them. Outside the moles the sea smoked too, in long slow drifts that came apart round the blades, and over the Kettle it smoked so thick that they rowed through it blind for a while, with the warm wet in their faces and the smell of eggs, and came out on the other side into the cold.

The smoke simply stopped. One stroke the water was steaming round the blades, and the next it lay black and flat without a breath on it, and the cold came up off it into their faces and up through the boards into their feet. Ahead, a mile off or less, lay the Grey, yellow-grey and still, and it smelled, even at that distance, of brimstone.

"Cold grounds," said Orso, and spat over the side, for luck or against it.

They shot the net in the full light, between the two boats, in a long curve of corks. The sea-people came up round them out of nowhere: Proteas first, and Halia, who was not yet seventeen and had towed a drowning Tharsian to the *Rope Street* at the Teeth, and Actaea, who had pulled Florus out from between two hulls there, and a few of the young ones. They hung in the water by the boats with only their heads out, sleek and dark and silent. Then Proteas lifted one webbed hand, and they went down, all of them, without a splash.

Marcus felt it before he heard it, if he heard it at all: a note in the planking, low and long, that came up through the thwart into his bones. A song. The Tharsians did everything to a count; the sea-people went down on the song and came up on it, and the song told them when. Orso had once asked Proteas how long they could stay down, and been sung at.

The water inside the curve of corks began to shiver. Then it began to boil, silver and grey-green, and the fish came up out of the dark ahead of the sea-people, cod and ling packed so thick that the net bellied and the corks went under. The boats leaned to it, and every man in them hauled. The cod came in over the gunwales slapping, gold-bellied, cold and heavy in the hand, and filled the bilges to the thwarts.

The sea-people came up after the fish. They came up grey.

He had seen them at the Teeth and on the water-gate stair, dark and sleek and at home in water that would have killed a man. He had not seen them come up out of the cold. Their lips were the color of slate, and the webs between their fingers had gone white as candle-wax, and they reached up over the gunwales for the skins of hot water without a word and held them to their chests in both arms. The steam went up off the fleece into their faces, and their teeth chattered so hard he could hear it over the fish.

Actaea came up by Florus's oar. She hung there with a skin held to her breast and her eyes shut and the steam going up round her face. Florus looked at her. His lips moved. Nothing came out of them. Her eyes stayed shut. He looked away and took a fresh grip on his oar; and Marcus, who had seen men rehearse things before, did not ask what.

They shot the net again, and again. By the third shot the sun was up, a white smear behind high cloud, and the boats were low in the water with fish.

Orso stopped talking.

Marcus saw his face, and turned on the thwart to look where Orso was looking.

The Grey had gone dark.

Not all of it. A stretch of it, low down off the larboard bow, had gone the color of a bruise and was coming toward them, and along the water in front of it ran a thin white line, and the line was moving.

"Net in," said Orso. He did not raise his voice. "Net in, net in, *net in*—"

Then he raised it.

The wind came first. It came across the flat water with nothing in front of it and struck the boat on the bow like a thrown bench, and every man in her lurched; and behind the wind came the snow, not falling but flying, sideways, so thick that the *Prisca* went grey, and then went out.

"Heads to it!" Orso had the steering oar in both arms and his whole weight on it. "Pull, starboard! *Pull!* Bring her round!"

They pulled. The sea got up out of nothing. A moment ago it had been flat as a floor, and now it was short and steep and white on top and coming at them in rows. The boat climbed the first and fell off the back of it with a crack that went up Marcus's spine, and the next came green over the bow and took his breath and his sight together.

He rowed with his back to it, the snow driving into his neck and the loom bucking against him. The skin came off his palms on it in the first strokes. He felt it go, and pulled.

The snow tore open, and he saw the *Prisca*.

She was broadside to the sea, beam-on, with half the net in over her gunwale and all the weight of the fish in it down on her lee side. Tithe was at her steering oar with his whole body on it, trying to bring her head round, and her men were pulling, and she would not come. A sea came along under her and lifted her, and went on lifting. The net slid across her, fish and all, and she went over.

She hung on the face of the sea with her keel showing and her men going off her into the water; and then the sea broke on her and she was bottom up, and the snow closed.

"*Pull!*" Orso was roaring. "Pull, or we're next! You can't do anything for them, *pull*, damn you, *pull*—"

Marcus pulled.

The sea-people came. He saw it in pieces, through the snow: a head in the water off their quarter, and an arm, and then another head beside it, dark and sleek, and a webbed hand taking a drowning man by the hair and turning him on his back and bringing him on. A sea-woman came up under a man's shoulders as he went down and held him up by the belt. A girl towed a boy by the collar with his face out of the water and her own under it. They brought them to the boat. The men on the lee side shipped their oars and leaned out and took them by the collar and the belt and the hair, whatever they could get, and dragged them in over the gunwale and let them lie among the fish, and went back to their oars.

A big bearded Tharsian came in over the side by Marcus's own oar, hauled by the net-maker's son and the man off the *Levy*, and lay on his back in the cod and coughed up the sea, and said something in Tharsian over and over that might have been a name and might have been a prayer. Then the boy came in over the bow, the one whose teeth had been going on the night they came home, grey-faced and limp, and it was Florus who had him, Florus who could not swim, leaning out over the stem with both arms locked round the boy's chest and his own eyes shut. The girl who had brought him, who was Halia, did not wait to see him in. She turned in the water and was gone.

Marcus could not leave the oar. He knew it as he knew his own name: if he let go of it the boat would fall off broadside as the *Prisca* had, and go over, and the sea-people would have twice as many to find. So he did not let go. He sat on his thwart with the snow at his back and rowed, and watched them do it, and could do nothing else.

It went as fast as it had come.

The wind dropped between one stroke and the next. The snow stopped flying and began to fall, straight down, large and soft and silent, and the sea was a long grey heave with the tops blown off it, and the light came back. A little way off the *Prisca* floated bottom up, with the net trailing from her into the deep. Round her in the water were the sea-people's heads. The other boats were coming up, rowing hard. In Orso's bilge, among the fish, men coughed and shook under the fleeces, and somebody was being sick.

"Where's Tithe?"

Somebody said it; then everybody was saying it, in two languages.

Halia came up beside the *Prisca*'s hull with a fistful of torn net in her hand and her eyes wide, and took one breath, and went down again before anyone could speak to her.

Proteas went down after her.

They waited. The snow fell. It was a long time.

Proteas came up with her across his chest. Her eyes were shut and her lips were blue-black and the webs of her hands were white to the wrist; and he lay on his back in the sea with her on him, holding her face up out of the water with one hand under her jaw and the other flat over her heart, and did not move.

"Is she—" said Marcus.

"She'll live." Proteas did not open his eyes. "He was in the net. It went down with the fish, all of it, and him in it, tangled, and she went down after him. She got her hand to him." His voice was flat. "It's too cold down there. Too cold to follow."

He lay in the water with the girl on his chest and the snow coming down on them both, and the sea lifting them and setting them down.

They had a boat full of cod. They had more cod than the town had seen out of the cold grounds in a generation, and every man in the bilges under the fleeces had been in that sea and been brought out of it alive; and somewhere under them, too far down for the light and too cold for the sea-people, a net full of fish was going down slowly into the dark with a cod man from Ostra in it who had never slept in a boat in his life.

They rowed home with the *Prisca* on a line astern, bottom up. Halfway to the Kettle the sea began to smoke again round the blades, faintly and then thick, and the warm wet came into their faces; and some of the men in the bilges began to cry, quietly, as the warmth got into them.

Pacuvius was on the hard with the Lanciarii when they came in. He helped hand the men out of the bilges in their fleeces, one at a time, and looked along them, and grinned.

"Where's Tithe?" he said. "Asleep?"

He got no answer, and did not notice. He was looking at the *Prisca* coming in on her line, bottom up.

"He'll be asleep somewhere," said Pacuvius. "Under a thwart."

At dusk the sea-people lay in the warm surf off the black beach to drink Ione's broth.

They lay where the spring ran out under the sand and the surf came in steaming, in a long dark row along the tide-line with only their heads and shoulders out of it. Old Ione, their healer, went along the row with her pot. It was seal-fat broth. It smelled of hot fat and fish oil and of something green and bitter out of the deep, and they drank it out of shells with both hands. Pherusa had stood at the north passage and touched them home, every one; Ione said so as she passed, to nobody in particular.

Halia lay nearest the spring, in the warmest of the water, wrapped to the chin in a fleece that some woman of the town had brought down to the beach and left. Her color had come back. Proteas lay beside her, a little to seaward, between her and the open water, and watched the Grey.

Marcus sat down on the sand above the tide-line. It was warm through his breeches, and black, and it gritted in the seams of his boots.

"You needn't go out there again," he said. "Any of you. The town knows what it owes."

Proteas did not look round. He watched the Grey with the broth steaming in the shell between his hands.

"It's fish," he said.

Up the hill, the men were at the Baths.

Liora had them in the warm room, on pallets along the channel at the foot of the walls, under the painted fish, where the steam went up into the old vault and hung there. She went from pallet to pallet with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder. Before each man she warmed her hands in the steam over the channel, and then laid them on him, on his chest and on the back of his neck, and kept them there. When she took them away he had stopped shaking. Sennius went behind her with the lamp. Hostus came after them, thin and stooped and particular, with a kettle of mutton broth and a ladle and his clean hands, and made every man drink a bowl of it whether he wanted it or not. The room smelled of mutton fat and barley and the spring.

Marcus went along the pallets after Hostus and asked them their names.

He knew the cohort by name, every man of it, and the dead of it, and the ninety-one before them. He had had these men since the Teeth.

The big bearded one sat up on his pallet to answer. "Calenus, Highness. Of the *Levy*. Fourth bench, larboard." They all gave their benches. It was how a Tharsian rower said who he was: his name, and his ship, and the place he sat in her. The boy Florus had pulled in over the bow was asleep with his mouth open, and Marcus let him sleep. Hostus gave him the name from the next pallet. "Crescens. Of the *Arrears*. He'll do."

At the end of the row Liora was washing her hands in the channel. She looked up.

"The one they didn't bring up," she said. "What was his name?"

He knew why she asked. She would say it tonight with the rest, on the beach or on the floor of the still-room or wherever she said them now: the eighty, and the cohort's dead, and her own, and this one, in his place among them.

"Tithe," said Marcus.

Liora looked at him.

"That's a ship," she said.

He turned to Calenus. The big man looked at him from the pallet, and then down at his bowl, and then along the row at the others, who looked back at him.

"He wasn't ours, Highness," said Calenus. "He was *Tithe*'s." He turned the bowl in his hands. "We called him what you called him."

He had fed them and worked them and watched them asleep against a wall, and he had never once asked.

They had kept the Navarch in the old harbor-master's room over the slip gate since the autumn, with a window onto the harbor and two of the Lanciarii outside his door. The stair up to it was cold, and Marcus's shoulders had begun to stiffen from the oar before he reached the top.

The Navarch was awake. He was always awake, whatever the hour. He sat at the table under the lamp in his long plain black coat, with the red book open in front of him. The room smelled of lamp oil and ink and of the cold that came in round the shutter. He stood when Marcus came in, tall and stooped under the beams, and inclined his head.

"Prefect."

"Navarch."

His right hand went toward his ear for the stylus that was not there, and came down.

"I saw the boats come in, one under tow, keel up, and I have written it," he said. "I have not got the man."

"A rower," said Marcus. "The Lanciarii found him asleep in the *Tithe*'s bilge the night we brought you in: a short man, round in the face, from Ostra."

He had brought the rest of it up the stair with him, everything he had: the shoulders, the bowl upright in the lap, *so it's always stopped*. He did not need it. The Navarch's pale heavy-lidded eyes had already gone still.

"Volusius," said the Navarch. "Of Ostra, on the south shore; rower, the *Tithe*, eleventh bench, larboard." He paused. "Entered twice for sleeping at the oar in his first year, and not since."

"You remember him."

"I signed the entries."

He sat down again and turned the red book to the place, and squared it against the edge of the table with one finger, and dipped the pen. As he wrote he spoke, quietly, in his Tharsian vowels, as he always did.

"*Day sixty-eight. Cyrene. Volusius of Ostra, rower, the* Tithe. *Drowned on the cold grounds, fishing.*" The pen stopped. He looked up. "Was he brought up?"

"No."

"*Not recovered,*" said the Navarch, and wrote it.

Marcus watched the pen. He knew what that column was for. In Tharsis a man who was brought up was not finished with. This one would be let alone; the sea had him. He was glad of it, and did not like being glad.

The Navarch blotted the line.

"Why do the sea-people go into the cold for your boats?" he said. "It is not their water."

"I asked them."

The Navarch considered this.

"That is not a reason," he said.

"It's the one I have."

The Navarch looked at him with his pale eyes, courteously, as he might have looked at a figure he could neither strike out nor carry forward. Then he dipped the pen again and wrote something more, short, at the end of the line, this time without a word. Marcus did not ask him what.

Alana was awake when he came up to the tower room, which was not usual any more. For a while after the Teeth she had lain awake most nights with her hands palm-up on the cover, as the healer had told her; he could not have said when she stopped, only that most nights now she slept. She was sitting up in the narrow bed with the weather-book on her knees, and the squall was in it already, the hour and the bearing it had come from, which she had got from Orso on the hard.

He sat down on the end of the bed and took the oilskin out of his shirt. It was cold from the stair and stiff with old salt. The roll of the ninety-one was in it, and his father's letter with the line blacked out, and Titus's order, and the page from the back of her book with the cohort's dead on it. He unfolded the page. At the bottom of it, apart from the others, was the boy. *Nonius.*

Alana held out her pen without being asked.

He wrote *Volusius, of Ostra* under the boy's name; and Alana reached over and moved the lamp closer, so that he could see to blot it.

They asked for the lamp the next evening, at the pots.

Marcus had come down to the long quay, as he did most evenings now. The snow had given over in the afternoon. The stones of the quay were black and wet and warm underfoot. The pots steamed, and the quay smelled of cod stewed with leeks out of the cold grounds' catch, and of the bread from Fausta's basket, and of wet wool drying on the men along the steam-box wall.

Calenus got up from the line when he saw him, and came with his bowl in his hand. Behind him, all along the wall, the spoons had gone still.

"Highness," said Calenus. "We'd ask a thing."

Marcus waited.

"A lamp," said Calenus. "And a window to put it in. That looks on the sea."

Marcus did not understand him, and his face must have said so. Sennius had come down from the Baths with Hostus's empty kettle to be filled, and stood by the second pot with it. He set it down.

"At home, Highness," he said, "when a man dies, his people keep a lamp for a year in the window that looks on the quay. So that when—"

He did not finish it, and none of the men along the wall finished it for him.

"This one wants the sea," said Calenus. "The sea's got him."

"The sea keeps them," said Sennius. He looked down at the kettle. "My mother keeps one for me. She'll have had the letter by now, and lit it."

Marcus looked at him: nineteen, a smith's son from Lessa on the north shore of Tharsis, alive and fed on the long quay of Cyrene, with a lamp burning for him in a window across the sea.

He could have given them a window in one of the port towers, which were the crown's. He did not want to. It was the town's sea the man had drowned in, fishing for the town.

"The windows are the town's," he said, and waited to see who along the pots would answer.

Orso was at the end of the pots with his arms folded, where he stood every evening to see that the town's fish went into the right bowls, and he had heard all of it. His face did not move.

"Mine," said Prisca.

She was at the second pot with the ladle, and did not look up from it. Orso turned his head.

"Prisca—"

"It was my boat," said Prisca.

Her house was the last on Netmakers' Row, on the hard, with an upper window that looked out over the harbor and through the gap to the sea. Calenus brought the lamp. It was a garrison lamp, red Tharsian clay with the closed hand on its shoulder. Prisca took it from him at her door without looking at the hand, and filled it from her own oil jar, and went up her stair with it; and after a little the window lit.

It was a small light, a fishing-town light, and it smoked. All along the long quay the prisoners had stopped eating and sat looking up at it, with their bowls going cold in their laps; and the town stood at its pots with its ladles in its hands and looked at them looking. The town had never seen a Tharsian lamp before. It had only ever seen Tharsians.

It was still burning at midnight, when Marcus looked down from the tower stair: one small light over the black harbor, in a window that looked on the sea.

Nerva came down to the slip on the morning the *Patience* came in, with the coil over his shoulder.

It was the rope he had been splicing on the long rock every night since the autumn: new hemp, tarred, laid up right-handed and coiled sunwise, with an eye in one end so neat it might have grown there. Away from the steam-boxes there was frost on the slip-yard, and the yard smelled of tar and hot oak and the cold. Nerva walked up the slip under the hull, past Gaius on the staging and the brothers at their trestles. He climbed the ladder at her bow and hung the coil on her stem-head by its eye. Then he came down again, and stood back, and looked at her.

"What's that?" said Gaius from the staging.

"Her forestay."

"She hasn't got a mast."

"She's got a place for one," said Nerva. "A hand too far forward."

Gaius took his lenses off.

"I took that step off the *Concord*," he said. "In proportion. To the finger."

"The *Concord*'s on a reef."

"Not because of her mast."

"No." Nerva did not look away from the hull. "The *Concord* had a stern on her like a barn door, and you've given this one a run aft like a knife. Step the *Concord*'s mast in her and the first following sea she meets, she'll put her nose in and root."

Gaius said nothing. He put his lenses back on and looked down at the keelson, at the step, for some time. Then he looked along the run of her aft, strake by strake, to the sternpost, and back.

"You scaled her frames," said Nerva. "You never scaled her run."

The brothers had stopped planing. Arruns looked at Velthur across the plank, and Velthur looked back at him. Then they both looked at the floor of the yard, which Marcus had never seen either of them do.

"That's a good splice," said Gaius at last.

Nerva grunted. "It's an eye-splice. Any boy on a quay can do an eye-splice."

"Not like that one."

Gaius took his boxwood rule out of his belt, and a stub of chalk, and went aft along the staging to her sternpost, and began to measure her run, strake by strake, chalking the figures on the oak as he went. Nerva stood at the foot of the slip with his hands behind his back and watched him at it, as he had watched the step every morning since the keel was laid, and said nothing; and this time he did not go away.

The bell went at noon: two strokes and two, for a ship and a friend.

She came in out of the Grey under oars, long and low, with the blue banner of Aeridor at her stern and the silver dolphin pennant at her masthead and her name in old gilt on her bow. She had brought Valeria to Cyrene in the autumn, with a writ in a bag. Marcus watched her come in now from the slip-head, and his heart was going.

Her master was ashore first, a short grey man named Avitus with a streaming cold, who blew his nose into a rag on the slip quay, apologized for it, and gave Marcus a packet sealed in the council's wax.

"From the Old Hill, Highness. Into your hand." He sniffed. "We sail for home tomorrow on the morning ebb, if you're to be there for Midwinter. The Grey's been thick." He put the rag away. "Your thief sends his duty, Highness. Pell. From the black ship. He won't go up the hill. They washed him, last time."

Pen was there before the mail-bag was off the plank, as Pen always was, with his weight on one hip and his long white hands out for it.

"I'll take it up, Highness. Save your legs." He hefted it, and began to sort the letters through the canvas by feel, as a man sorts coins in a purse he cannot see into, and looked up with his long crooked smile. "Who's for the capital? So I don't go putting a man's letter in the bag with him."

"I'll tell you tonight," said Marcus, and broke the seal.

It was in a clerk's hand, the old chancery hand of the Old Hill, so fine and so even it might have been cut in stone.

*To the Prefect of Cyrene, greeting.*

*The Council of the Realm, sitting in the King's name, desires the attendance of the Prefect at the Old Hill before Midwinter, there to receive the thanks of the Council for the late service at the Teeth, and to give the Council such account of that service as the Council shall require.*

*The Prefect will bring with him such of his officers as he shall think fit.*

*Captain Aulus Nerva, master mariner, will attend, at the King's particular wish.*

Under it were the councillors' names in a column, the Marshal's first, and their seals.

He read it twice. The thanks took him no time. The account took longer; it was the longest line in the letter, and he had been a prince long enough to know which line in a letter is the one it was written for.

Alana read it over his arm. "You owe me a hall," she said.

Valeria read it over the other one. "You needn't choose me," she said. "I'm ordered."

Roderic had come down to the slip quay at the bell on the grey mare, as he came down to every ship, with his beard combed. Marcus went to his stirrup.

"Will you hold it for me? The town, I mean. Till we're home."

"Ask me something hard, my boy," said Roderic. His eyes went over Marcus's head to Valeria, and hers came up to meet them, and Marcus did not try to read what passed. Gaius had a hull and Liora had the Baths, and he did not insult either of them by asking. Felix would come whether he was asked or not.

He folded the letter and stood with it at the slip-head in the cold, with the *Patience*'s people coming ashore round him and blowing on their fingers.

There was nothing of his father in it. Every line was the council's, in a clerk's hand and the old formula, *greeting* and *desires* and *shall require*. He had been waiting for something else, as he had waited for the check at the table, and it had not come either. Except in the one line. Nobody on the council had any particular wish for an old sea-captain out of the harbor prison. That line was the king's.

So was the warrant that had put Nerva in a cell. Marcus had gone down to the long rock in the autumn meaning to tell the old man whose order it had been, and had not: the warrant was the king's, and so was the telling. Now his father had written Nerva's name, through a clerk, at his particular wish. Marcus did not know what he meant by it. For three years he had been sure he knew what his father meant by everything, and he had been wrong every time.

He went down the slip to tell Nerva.

Nerva was up on the staging at her bow, with his back to the harbor and the *Patience*, looking at the coil on her stem-head.

"I heard the bell," he said, without turning.

"You're asked for," said Marcus. "At the Old Hill. For Midwinter." He held up the letter, and since the old man had not turned, he read the line out to his back.

The old man's back did not move.

"Last time the crown wanted me particular," said Nerva, "it sent four men and a chain."

"This time it sent a galley."

"Galleys have holds."

Marcus said nothing. There was nothing he could have said to that which was his to say.

Nerva went on looking at the rope.

"Who's to mind my mast?"

"Gaius."

"That's what I'm afraid of," said Nerva; and he stood at her bow with his back to the harbor and the galley in it, and did not turn round.

# Chapter Three: Who Gave Him Leave

**VALERIA**

Valeria was sick before the *Patience* had cleared the moles, which was early, even for her.

She was sick again where the warm water ended and the cold began, at a line so sharp you could have laid a rule along it. Again when the Grey came down over the bow and shut Cyrene away behind them, the black headland and the bell-tower and the smoke of the town, and the General on his grey mare at the end of the long quay, who had not waved, because she had told him not to. After that she gave up marking them. A ship was the baggage cart again. You sat in it, and it took you where it was going, and other people did the work. There was nothing for you to do but hold on, and listen to the noises it made, and guess from them what was coming.

The Grey was thick that winter. It was less a fog than a country, yellow-grey and close and without a sky, and it stank of brimstone, the bad-egg reek of the fires under the sea, so that the smell got into her plaits and her cloak and the back of her throat and lived there. Frost furred the stays in the night. The oars came up out of the Grey with ice on the blades, and went down into it, and came up. The drum in the waist kept them at it with a dull thud she could feel in her back teeth. Forward, the copper was on. The smell of salt pork boiling came aft along the deck and met her at the rail.

She had come south in the *Patience* in the autumn with a writ in a bag, sick the length of the sea. She had told herself that a body which had carried a lancea up and down the isles since the Long Field would learn the trick of a deck in the end. It had not. It did everything else she asked of it. It would march all day and stand all night and get up in the morning and do both again; it was square and hard and quick under the mail still, and in private she was vain of it. And it would not stand on a deck in the Grey for the length of a hymn without sending back whatever she had last put into it.

Avitus was sorry about that. Avitus was sorry about most things. He was a short grey man with a cold that had gone south with him in the autumn, and come north with him, and gone south again, and was going north now in the best of health. He blew his nose into a rag and begged her pardon for it, and on the evening they sailed he came aft with a raw onion on a board and a twist of salt.

"For the stomach, Captain. Saving your presence." He sniffed. "Bite and salt. Bite and salt. It settles some."

She bit, and salted, and bit. The onion went down like a coal off a hearth and came back up the same way, with company.

"Ah," said Avitus, and put the board under his arm. "Some it doesn't take."

It was Avitus who had carried the Marshal's letter to her in the autumn.

She kept the letter inside the neck of her mail, where it had been since that day, and she had no need to take it out to read it. *You will not sail with him again. If you cannot hold him, say so, and I will send a captain who can.* She had not said so. She had sailed with him to the elves' wood and back, and out to the Teeth. Twice she had written the Marshal the same sentence, in her own square hand under her own plain seal, and sent it north to be read by his fire.

The summons she had read over the prince's arm on the slip quay, and once since, in Avitus's cabin by the lamp, while Alana slept. *The Prefect will bring with him such of his officers as he shall think fit.* She read orders the way she read ground, for the dead places and the ways out. The recall in the king's drawer named *the Prefect of Cyrene, and such of his officers as sailed with him*. Different clerks had drawn the two papers, a season apart, and they were not the same. They were two fields with one hedge between them, and she was standing in the hedge, where the thorn grew thickest.

The list was older than the Marshal's letter, and older than the Grey. *Things that might kill me* at the top; and beside it, in its own column, *Things to deal with later*. On the *Patience* it ran: *The Grey. This rail. Avitus's onion.* The council was not on it. A council could not kill her. It could break her, which was another thing, and there had never been a heading for that; so the council went under *later*, where it had stood since the autumn. There was a third column now, with no heading and only the elf in it. She left that one alone. Later lay at the far end of the Grey. It came nearer at every stroke of the drum.

Her hand had gone flat to her breastbone, over the letter. She took it away.

Nerva would not go below.

Avitus offered him his own cot, as one master to another, and Nerva thanked him, and took a blanket instead and the bare deck by the steering oar. He slept there sitting up, with his back to the sternpost and his long legs out before him, and in the mornings there was frost in his beard. He did not say why. Nobody on the *Patience* asked. A man who had last come home to the capital with the war council waiting for him on the quay might want to come in the next time on deck, awake, and on his own feet. The writ she had read on the quay at Cyrene had pardoned the prince and the General by name. It had not named him.

He watched the helmsman. He watched him by the hour, the sea-pale eyes going from the man's hands on the loom to the bow and back to the hands, and said nothing whatever about it, which in Nerva was a kind of fast.

The Chair sat under the stern awning on a coil of cable, in a blanket, with her weather-book open on her knees, and wrote down whatever there was to write. The wind, which was none. The bearing, which was Avitus's guess, sneezed at her from the steering oar. The stores: Avitus had asked her to keep the stores for him, since she was keeping everything else. Every evening she wrote them up in her small neat hand with her lips moving, so much biscuit, so much pork, so many onions, one fewer. Valeria had never seen anybody so pleased to be handed a list of onions. A woman had a right to her own book.

Felix was sick too, though not, he wished it understood, from the sea.

"I was in boats before I could walk, Captain," he said, coming up off the rail beside her with his face the color of tallow. "On the Steps you're in a boat before you're named. It isn't the sea."

"Understood."

"It's the pork." He looked forward at the copper with real hatred. "Strictly, it's the pork."

Then he wiped his mouth, and went and filled the prince's cup at the water-cask, and stood by while the prince drank it.

The prince was not sick. He stood at the rail forward for long stretches of every day, with the Grey going by on either hand, and looked into it. Whatever he was thinking about there, it was not the council. Valeria had seen his council face, on the gatehouse roof at Cyrene, and this was another one. She had never yet asked a prince what he was thinking, and she did not mean to begin with this one.

Avitus had given up his cabin to the Chair and herself; it was the size of a clothes-press, and smelled of his cold. One evening she opened her kit there for a dry shirt, and there was a crock in it she had not packed.

It was a squat brown crock with a wooden stopper, and the stopper was tied down with a twist of coarse grey hair from a horse's tail. She knew the tail. She knew what was in the crock before she had the stopper out, and when she had it out the cabin knew too. Old Baebius's liniment. It smelled as it always had, like a chandler's yard on fire.

She had rubbed it into the General's back in the cutting at Silverwood, on her knees over him in the cold, with her thumbs under his left shoulder-blade, where he liked them. The healer had mended that back since, at the Baths, and he had no more use for the stuff. And her own shoulders were galled where her mail had sat on them since the Long Field, and he had seen them, and never said a word. There was no letter with the crock. He was no man for letters. He had said what he had to say with a twist of his mare's tail, and he was the only man she knew who could.

If he had been there she would have told him it stank. He would have agreed with her, and been pleased with himself till spring.

The Chair looked up from her book and sniffed.

"Goose-grease," she said. "Mustard. Turpentine. And something I can't place." She laid her pen down. "I have a theory about the something."

"Keep it."

"I'll write it down, then." She held out a hand with ink in the creases of the knuckles. "Give it here. You can't reach your own shoulders, and if you try you'll put something out, and then I'll have to write that down as well."

Valeria considered refusing. Then she got out of her mail, which in that cabin was a question of tactics. She sat on the edge of Avitus's cot with her shirt down round her waist and her plaits pulled forward over one shoulder, out of the way.

She had a good back, and knew it: broad across the shoulders and narrow at the waist, with the long muscles standing hard on either side of the spine. There were the old knife-seams along her ribs on the left, white. Across the tops of both shoulders the skin was galled red and rough where the rings bore. The rest of it was hers. She had made it herself, out of drill and long roads and bad weather, and nobody had helped.

The Chair's fingers were cold, and then were not. The liniment went on cold and lay there; then it began to burn, slowly, a deep dull heat that sank into the shoulders and found the knots in them one by one and sat on each like a hen. Valeria shut her eyes.

"Hold still," said the Chair.

"I am holding still."

"You're holding still *at* me. It's different." Her thumbs kept working. After a time she said, in the voice she kept for the wind and the bearings, "You have a remarkable back. Structurally, I mean." And then, being fair: "No. I mean it the other way as well."

"I know," said Valeria.

She slept that night for the first time since the moles, with her shoulders on fire and the cabin reeking of Old Baebius, and dreamed of nothing at all.

On the last night out the Grey was so thick that the stern lantern made a yellow room in it, with walls. She was at the lee rail beyond the light with her forehead on her wrist, giving the sea the last of her supper. Someone came up beside her and gathered her plaits in one hand and held them back off her face.

She knew the hand. She finished what she was doing.

"Let go of my hair, Highness."

"In a moment."

She spat, and straightened, and wiped her mouth on the back of her wrist, and he let the plaits go. He leaned on the rail beside her.

"I'll tell them I ordered you aboard," he said.

"You didn't."

"They won't know that."

"I will."

Below them in the waist the drum went on. The oars came up, and dripped, and went down.

"You were on the wall at Cyrene," said Valeria, "my second night there. You heard what I said."

"I heard."

"Then don't take it off me."

He did not answer that. He stood by her at the rail with the Grey going past; and after a time he said, "You're frozen to the rail."

She was. The end of one plait had gone down onto the iced wood while she was busy, and stuck there. She had to work it loose a little at a time with her fingers, while the prince of the isles looked on with great politeness and did not help.

From the dark by the steering oar, where she had thought him asleep, Nerva said: "Your man's been carrying a point of larboard helm since the moles."

The prince turned his head. "Tell Avitus."

"Not my ship," said Nerva.

It was the first thing he had said about anybody's steering since Cyrene. Valeria worked the last of her plait free of the rail, and thought that it had cost him more to keep that in so long than the Grey had cost her.

---

The Grey let go of them in the forenoon. It thinned to a haze, and the haze to a pale yellow light lying on the water, and then they were out of it, and the cold came at them clean, with no smell in it at all; and Valeria lifted her head off her arms and saw the capital.

Black sea, white sky, and snow on all of it. The Old Hill came first, as it always had: the terraces of the tombs going up it one above another in white steps, and above them the dome of the Hall of Kings and the long wall of the palace, and high in the wall the one tall window that looked down over the tombs and the canals and the harbor at everything there was. Below the hill the city ran down on every side to the water, roof beyond roof under the snow; and through it, from the springs under the hill, the warm canals came down to the sea, each one smoking, so that from the water the city was ruled in grey from the hill to the harbor, like a drill-field marked out for a muster.

The moles reached out to take them in, black stone arms with the snow along their tops. On the tower at the end of each a bolt-thrower sat under a hood of sailcloth with the snow on it. Its layer stood beside it in a fur hat, stamping, and did not trouble to lay it on a royal galley. Between the moles the royal harbor lay black and steaming. As the *Patience* came in under the towers the warmth of it rose round her, and the smell with it, sulphur and weed and tar; and somewhere up in the city the bells began to ring the noon.

They rang it louder than she remembered. The Hall of Kings first, from the top of the hill; then the Fishmarket bell and the harbor bell, answering; and last, a beat behind the rest, the cracked bell on the Gaol Stair.

Nerva was standing at the rail. She had not seen him get up.

"They never mended it," he said.

They passed the mouth of the Lower Canal under the hill, where it came out between its stone banks into the harbor, smoking. You could see a long way up it from the water: the steam lying along it, and on the left bank a little way up, under its sign, the Drowned Lantern, with its canal window that had no glass in it and a sack nailed over the lower half. The prince looked up the canal once, at the window. Then he looked at the berths. Half the capital had heard how that glass went.

Along the inner quay under the hill the old berths ran away from them, empty, with their great iron rings rusted red on the bollards and their slipways climbing behind them into the shadow of the hill under the snow. The prince's grandfather had kept his fleet there. Valeria had marched past them half her life on her way to somewhere else, and had long since stopped seeing them. They were not empty now. In the first of them, nearest the hill, lay the *Answer*.

She was long and low and black, with her oar-ports shut, and her high stern swept up over her to the gilt book on its post. On her bow the red eye of Tharsis looked out through its tar. The Lanciarii had painted her rail Aeridor blue at Cyrene, roughly, in a hurry; and all along it the white closed hands of Tharsis showed through the blue, as the brand on the General's mare showed through her winter coat. Somebody from the city had climbed up and hung a bough of pine on her stem-head, as on a door.

Her people were at her rail. Valeria saw the blue cloaks of her own Lanciarii, the ones she had sent north in her with Pell, and the men of Cyrene in their sea-coats. Apart from them, in the bow, the men of the cohort who had been born under the Old Hill, standing together and looking up at it. Not at the quay. At the hill. Out of the waters of Cyrene a man of the cohort was a convict again; the writ said so, and she had read it aloud. They had not set foot on the quay since she berthed, Pell told her later. They walked her deck instead, from the stem to the gilt book and back, and looked at the hill.

Pell was in the bow too, waving his knitted cap over his head with both arms.

"Captain!" His voice came over the water thin and cracked with cold. "*Highness!* I've kept her dry!"

"I see it," the prince called back.

"I'm not going up the hill, mind!" He had a clean shirt on, and his hair had dried in tufts. At his neck on a cord hung the doeskin bag with the prince's stone in it, and he put his hand on it while he shouted, to show it had not gone anywhere. "Not for thanks nor nothing! It's carpets, up the hill. They wash your *ears*."

"Bring the stone back," said the prince, not loudly; but Pell heard him, and grinned, and put his cap on.

Felix, at her elbow, pointed with his chin across the harbor at the Fishmarket Steps, where the houses went up from the water one on another's shoulders.

"My mother's," he said. "The one smoking."

Every chimney on the Steps was smoking.

"The one smoking most," said Felix.

The *Patience* rowed on past the berths to the galley quay beyond, where the galleys lay in their row, and the warm water slopped between her side and the stone. Above the long quay the Gaol Stair came down from the harbor prison in its crooked flights, black with wet, to the quays of the Lower Harbor. Nerva looked at it, and at the quay at its foot, and did not move. Valeria knew the story. Everybody in the capital did. This was the quay.

She put Galba and Pacuvius at his back, without a word.

The quays were full. The city had heard the *Patience* was in, and had come down to see the prince for itself: men from the yards with their adzes still in their belts, women with baskets, boys on the bollards, a priest. The bakehouses along the waterfront smelled of cloves. Somebody shouted the prince's name. Then a good many people did; and somebody else shouted *Answer!*, and the boys on the bollards took it up, *Answer, Answer*, and pointed at the black ship in the first berth, in case he had not noticed her.

Valeria went down the plank with her knees still full of the deck, so that the quay came up to meet her wrong. She looked at the faces, as at any crowd, for the hands that were not where they should be. They were all where they should be. Most of them were waving.

The Chair would not go aboard the *Answer*. She stood at the foot of the black ship's plank with her bag and looked up once at her stern, at the row of small square windows of green glass across it; then she turned for the Steps. Valeria had been on that deck at the Teeth, and did not ask. The prince saw the Chair to her door, and came back down, and walked the *Answer*'s deck with the cohort men for an hour, from the stem to the gilt book and back. He did not lodge in her.

They lodged at the foot of the Fishmarket Steps, in the Corvus lodging-house, in the room the Chair had had before Cyrene. Nobody in the house had wanted it since, it being under the roof in winter. Nobody had asked the prince up the hill, and he did not go up it. Felix went home to his mother's, halfway up the Steps. Nerva stayed on the *Patience*, by the steering oar. And Valeria walked the house from the street door to the eaves, and looked: floor on floor of other people's lives, the smell of fish oil and mutton-bone broth and cabbage, a stair with a rotten tread near the top, one stove that worked for all of it, and a window under the eaves with a long drop beneath it to the water. It was the worst ground in the capital. Nobody had asked her either.

She put Galba and the file on the street door, and slept on the top landing across the prince's door, with the Farrier along the wall beside her. Going up, on the second landing, the Chair had passed a shut door, and gone by it very quietly.

At dusk a boy in the Marshal's livery came down the Steps with a lantern and a folded paper, sealed with a bull's head in red wax. The Lord Marshal's compliments to the shield-captain, and would she sup with him that night.

She read it twice. It was an order. It had only put on its good coat.

---

She went up the hill alone, in the dark, with the Farrier on her shoulder, and left Galba the door.

The lanes of the Old Hill were steep and narrow, and the snow on them had been trodden to grey ice and then sanded, and her boots knew the way without her. Lamps in the windows, and through the shutters the smell of the kitchens, cloves and frying onions and somewhere a goose. The wall of the garden of the tombs rose beside her with the snow along its top, and over it the little roofs of the tombs climbed away into the dark toward the palace. She did not look at them. The Marshal's house stood under the palace wall where the lane turned, tall and grey and narrow, with a bough of pine over its door the size of a young tree.

Ursus opened it. She knew him by his red beard, as broad as a spade, and by his nose, which had sat a little to one side since the funeral feast, when the prince had put his own head into it. He looked at her, and then for some time at the Farrier, and plainly decided that if the Marshal wanted it off her the Marshal could ask for it himself, and stood aside.

On a peg inside the door hung a white cloak of good wool with a steel clasp, the snow still melting off its hem onto the flags. It was not the Marshal's color, or his size.

Gnaeus Varro was standing at his fire, warming the backs of his legs.

He was broader than she remembered, and greyer, the grey cropped close to his skull like a soldier's, and his ribbons sat in their rows on his breast, the colors of every war since the old king's, with the firelight going over them. Over the chimney-piece a bull's head was carved in the stone, with a garland on its horns for Midwinter. In any other house it would have been a joke.

"Captain," he said. "Sit. Eat. You've been sick the length of the sea; it's on your face."

There was a table drawn up to the fire, and on it cold beef, roasted and cut thick with the fat left on, and a pot of mustard, and a brown loaf with the crust split, and a jug of wine, and cups. She sat. Nothing she had eaten since Cyrene had stayed with her, and she was not too proud to eat the Marshal's beef. It was good beef. The mustard went up behind her eyes, and she went on eating, and was angry about it, and ate.

He poured for her himself. "From my own vines," he said. "On Vessa."

She knew Vessa. The south face of it was all vines, terraced up from the sea in long steps that went gold in the autumn, the Marshal's and his neighbors', with the warm wind coming off the water onto them all summer; and behind the vines the island climbed into the hills, where nothing grew but sheep and thorn, and the villages sat in the folds with their backs to the weather, and one of them was Callow, where her father had cut thorn for other men's hedges every winter of his life, and gone down with the levy every summer there was a war, until the Long Field. In the summer Titus held the ford at Hollowmere, the Ninth had lain on Vessa, in camp below those vines, guarding them. Titus had written for the Ninth. The council had sent it to Vessa.

She held the cup. The wine smelled of sun. She held it level, and did not drink.

The Marshal seemed not to notice. He had gone back to the fire, and stood with his hands behind him.

"You'll have read the summons," he said.

"I've read it, my lord."

"*Such of his officers as he shall think fit.*" He took one hand from behind his back and looked at it, and pressed the bull's head of his seal ring into the ball of his thumb, hard, and took it away; and there was a small white bull on his thumb, which slowly filled in red. "He thought you fit."

"I was ordered, my lord."

"So you were. By me." The hand went behind him again. "Do you know what holds this kingdom together, Captain?"

She waited. She had sat through enough of the Marshal's councils to know the answer was already on its way.

"Not the Grey," he said. "Not the fleet; there is no fleet. *Leave.* A lord holds his island by the king's leave, and his levy by the council's. A captain holds her company by mine. Every man in the isles knows whose leave he stands under, and that is all the kingdom there is. Your prince sailed without it, and won. Good. I'll drink to the ship." He did not drink. "But the next prince who sails without leave will have learned it from this one, and he'll lose. And if Tharsis had taken that ship off him at the Teeth, the council would have asked me what I did on the day I had your report."

"You'd have done nothing, my lord. His Majesty keeps it."

The Marshal looked at her.

"His Majesty keeps a great many things," he said.

He came and sat across the table from her, which she had not expected, and put his heavy forearms down among the beef and the bread.

"I can't touch him," he said. "You've seen the quays. This city had him face down in the Lower Canal till the autumn. Now he's put a Tharsian three-banker in his grandfather's berth, and they've hung pine on her. Before that ship came in I could have had him home in chains, and the city would have lent me the chain. Now I couldn't have him home in a cloak." He said it without heat, as a quartermaster reads out what is left in the store. "And he'll do it again. He'll go beyond the waters of Cyrene the next time it suits him, and the time after that; and one day it'll be a fleet he takes and not a ship, and he'll lose it, because he's a drunk who has had one good autumn. Somebody has to say so before that day. Not after."

He could not touch the prince. He could touch her. She sat with the cup level in her hand and waited for him to come to it.

"Say it tomorrow," said the Marshal. "What my letter asked you to say. That you couldn't hold him. It's no crime in a captain that she couldn't hold a king's son against his will; there isn't a captain in the isles who could. I'll not hold it against you. You'll keep your rank and your company, and that medal I pinned on you, and I'll find you something better to keep than a rock in the south." He let that lie on the table between them. "And the council will have it from his own shield-captain that the Prefect of Cyrene cannot be held."

There it was. She looked at it. It was well made. It would save her, and it would put the prince into the Marshal's hand out of her own mouth, and the Marshal would never have to sign a thing.

"And then you'd send a captain who could," she said.

"I'd send a captain who could."

"I could have held him, my lord."

The ring went into his thumb again. "Then why didn't you?"

"You've had my answer, my lord. Twice."

For a moment the Marshal's face had nothing in it at all. Then, unexpectedly, something that was nearly a smile.

"You write a good report, Captain," he said. "That was always what was wrong with you."

She had written him a good report once before. In the first summer of this war, on a beach she had no wish to remember the name of, a lord's levy had broken before a blow was struck and taken the flank with it; and she had set that down in her report, plain, with the name of the levy and the name of the young lord at the front of it, going the other way. He had been the Marshal's sister's son. The Marshal had asked the council to break her for insolence to a lord. And Titus, who had been at the table that day, had read her report aloud to the council from the first line to the last in his flat voice, and then asked the Marshal which word of it was untrue. The Marshal had not found one. He had been looking ever since.

She had wondered at Cyrene why he had sent her there: her, out of every captain on the rolls, the one he had once tried to have broken. She had thought it might be so that if it went wrong he could break them both together, the drunk and the woman who had written him up. Or it might be because she wrote plain reports, and the Marshal, whatever else he was, liked to know what had happened. She had never settled which. Tonight, across his table, she thought it was the first.

"Wear your medal tomorrow," said the Marshal, getting up. "The city will want to see it."

"It's at Cyrene, my lord. In a drawer."

He looked down at her from beside the fire.

"Everything is," he said.

She set the cup down on his table, full, as level as she had carried it, and got up, and took the Farrier from where it leaned against her chair. Ursus let her out past the white cloak into the snow. The cold had come down hard on the hill while she ate. The snow creaked under her boots, and the pine over the Marshal's door let fall a little of its load on her as she went out under it.

Halfway down the lane she put the Marshal at the top of the first column, above the Grey. Then she thought about the Grey, and put it back.

---

The Chair did not go up the hill.

"I'm not one of his officers," she said on the top landing in the morning, with a basket on her arm and her hair pinned up tighter than usual. "I looked. The summons hasn't a word for what I am. I could be *such*, at a stretch, but I doubt I'm *fit*." She said it lightly. She was looking down the stairwell. "There are some women on the second landing that I owe a letter. I wrote it once. I'd sooner say it."

The prince looked at her, and she at him, and that was all of it. She went down ahead of them with her basket, over the rotten tread; and on the second landing, as Valeria came down behind the prince, the Chair stopped at the shut door, and knocked.

They went up the Old Hill in the middle of the morning: the prince, and Valeria a step behind him, and Felix, and Nerva between Galba and Pacuvius, who had fetched him up from the *Patience*, and the rest of the file behind. The city came with them. It came out of its doors and walked beside them up the lanes, and leaned out of its windows, and held its children up. The bells rang for no reason she could find except that it was nearly Midwinter and the bells liked it. The prince walked up through it as through weather. Once a woman put a sprig of pine into his hand, and he carried it the rest of the way up the hill without seeming to know he had it.

At the doors of the Hall of Kings the porters were taking steel. No blade passed the doors while the council sat there. It was an old law, older than the dome. The porters kept the blades in a long rack inside the door, with a chalk mark on the wall over each for its owner. Valeria gave up her knife without a thought. Then she held out the Farrier.

The porter was a heavy young man in a leather apron, with tallow on his fingers from the hall's lamps. He took the Farrier by the middle of the haft, as a man takes a broom, and looked at the head, ground back thin as a leaf, and then at the iron spike on the butt, and then at her.

"Which end goes up, Captain?"

"The sharp one."

He looked at both ends again, and then at her again. Then he stood it in the rack head down, between a lord's dress sword and somebody's walking-stick, and chalked a mark over it, and turned to Nerva, who gave him a seaman's knife with a broken point.

She left it there. It was harder than she would have thought. Going in through the doors her right thumb went looking for the ash, for the place below the head where her father's thumb had worn it smooth, and met nothing. She shut her hand on the nothing, and went in.

The cold of the Hall of Kings came up through the soles of her boots. It always had. It was the cold of a place built for the dead, deep and old and marble, and it did not put itself out for the living. The hall smelled of wet fur and lamp-smoke and old incense. Under the dome it was full. The lords sat at the front on their benches, in furs, with their chains on. Behind them, in among the pillars where the refugees from the lost islands had been put at the funeral, stood the city, as much of it as the porters had let in, in sea-coats and aprons, with the snow melting off its shoulders onto the floor. At the head of the hall the king's chair stood on its step with a cloth over its arms, and the lamp beside it unlit. It was empty. Below it, at the foot of the king's stair, the council sat at its long table, with the clerks at either end and the Marshal in the middle in his ribbons. Behind them the door to the stair was shut, and the men of the guard stood on it.

Under the dome, where the floor rose in steps to the place the biers lay, there was nothing now but the marble and a draught. She had not been in the hall since the day she came home in the boats. It had been swept by then.

The prince looked at the empty chair when they came in, and again when they stopped below the table, and after that she saw his eyes go back to it every time the herald drew breath.

The herald was a little old man with a voice too big for him. He stood out from the end of the table with a paper and read the thanks in the old chancery form, every word of it.

*The Council of the Realm, sitting in the King's name, gives the thanks of the Realm to Marcus, the King's son, Prefect of Cyrene: for the King's town of Cyrene, taken again out of the hand of Tharsis, and held; for the squadron of Tharsis broken at the Teeth, and her Navarch taken; for a ship of three banks brought into the King's harbor and laid in the King's berth; and for tidings of the King's father's fleet.*

The city roared.

*And the Council,* read the herald, when he could be heard, *by the old law of the King's fleet, gives the crown of beaks to the first man over the enemy's rail.*

The Marshal got up and came round the end of the table with it in his two hands. It was a circlet of gilt bronze with the beaks of little ships standing up all round it, worn soft at the points by other men's thumbs, and it had not been given, Valeria had heard, since the old king's fleet was afloat. He carried it at arm's length, as he had once carried her medal. He stopped in front of the prince, and held it out.

The prince did not take it.

"I wasn't first over her rail, my lord," he said. "The Lanciarii were, and they went over a man's back to do it. Tuccius of the Lanciarii held the head of the bridge on one knee, with a spear in his thigh, and the rest went over him. Herennius of the Lanciarii died on the bridge. Lurco of Rufo's forty died on her deck. The General broke her marines from behind. Captain Nerva held his course at her ram and broke her oars, and the elves of Silverwood cleared her stern, and the town of Cyrene rowed." He stopped. "And some others, who'd sooner I didn't name them in this hall."

He held out his hands, and after a moment the Marshal put the crown into them.

"It's Tuccius's," said the prince. "I'll take it to him."

The lords did not like it. She saw them not like it, all down the front benches: a convict's name and a deserter's in the Hall of Kings, and an elf's wood, and a crown going south to a common lanciarius with a broken nose. Then from somewhere at the back, among the pillars, one of the men of Cyrene off the *Answer* bawled "*Rope Street!*" at the top of his lungs. The city, which had no notion what it meant, cheered it anyway, and went on cheering it.

Valeria would have given a good deal to see Tuccius's face. The prince had not named her. She had heard him not do it, and knew he had done it on purpose.

The herald had not finished.

*The Council thanks also the officers of the Prefect,* he read, *and among them Valeria, shield-captain of the Lanciarii, whom the Realm has honored before, for the causeway at Hollowmere.*

Somebody behind the pillars shouted *Hollowmere!* Then a great many people did, and the lords turned on their benches to look at her. She stood with her chin up and her hands at her sides, because that was how they liked her to stand; and thought of the wounded she had held the causeway for, who had got out, and had been taken out of their beds at the Baths at Cyrene on the night the fort fell, and walked into the sea. The city did not know that. The city was cheering the carts.

Her thumb went looking for the ash again, and did not find it.

Then the herald rolled up his paper, and the porters opened the doors, and the city was sent out into the snow, still shouting. The lords who were not of the council went out after it in their furs. The doors were shut.

They were shut quietly. Somebody had oiled them.

---

The hall was another place with the doors shut. The noise went out of it, and the cold came in to take the room the city had left. The clerks' pens were wet, and the empty chair stood above the table on its step. The prince stood below the table with the crown of beaks in his hands, and the sprig of pine still in one of them, as if he had forgotten both. Valeria stood a step behind him, where she had stood for his brother. Felix had gone out with the city. Nerva had not been sent out with it. He waited alone by a pillar where the city had been, with his cap in his hands and his long back against the cold stone, and the lords at the table looked at him from time to time as men look at a dog that has walked into a temple.

Her mouth had gone dry. She had stood in front of cavalry with a wetter one.

The Marshal let the quiet stand. Then he put the question; and he did not raise his voice for it, because the dome did not need him to.

"Who gave you leave, Prefect," he said, "to take the king's ships beyond the waters of Cyrene?"

The prince had been looking at the empty chair. He looked at the Marshal instead.

"Nobody, my lord."

The town of Cyrene had said go, on its gatehouse roof, in the wind: Orso Netmender first, and then Prisca, and Vindex the cooper, and old Father Annius once Vindex had said it into his good ear. Only Old Baebius had said no, from the crown's seat, on principle. The town had given the prince its leave, for whatever a town's leave was worth, on a roof. He did not say so. Valeria watched him not say it, and thought she saw why. Say it here, and a clerk at the end of that table would write a netmender's name into the council's book, and a fisher-widow's, under a charge; and there the names would stay for as long as there was a council to read them.

"Nobody," the Marshal said back, flat; and at the end of the table a clerk wrote it down.

Down the table a lord leaned forward. Sestius: he sat for an island that Tharsis had taken from him, and still wore its badge on his collar, as if it might come back.

"The council has heard of help at the Teeth," said Lord Sestius. "From the sea. And of the old king's fleet, found. Where, precisely? There's bronze in those hulls. There are masts. The council will want the place."

"It's their water, my lord," said the prince. "Not mine. If the council wants the place, it can ask them."

"Ask," said Sestius.

"Ask them."

This council had broken up Nerva's *Swift Heron* for her bronze, after his trial. Valeria thought of it, and kept her face as it was. By his pillar Nerva gave no sign that he had heard the word.

At the far end a younger lord with a new fur collar and an old name, Opimius, wanted the Navarch.

"A Navarch of Tharsis, Marshal," he said. "Alive. He ought to be brought north and questioned. By people who know how."

Valeria took him for a man who wanted a Tharsian in chains on the Gaol Stair for Midwinter, and the city brought down to look at him.

The Marshal did not turn his head. "The Navarch is not the matter before the council, my lord."

His eyes went past the prince instead, to the pillar.

"And that man," he said. "Why is that man in this hall?"

The clerk at the near end had the summons by him. He put his finger on the line. "At the King's particular wish, my lord Marshal."

"I can read," said the Marshal.

He looked up past the table at the shut door of the king's stair, and the men on it, and the empty chair, as if one of them might tell him; and none did. Valeria watched him look. He did not know either. Whatever the king wanted with an old sea-captain out of the harbor prison, the king had not told his Marshal. It was the first thing she had learned in the capital that she liked.

She followed his eyes to the door and the stair behind it and the table at its foot; and the thing she had kept off the length of the Grey would not be kept off any longer. Titus had written up this stair. She had watched him do it, in the tent on the near bank above the ford, with the river loud beyond the canvas all night and the lamp smoking, and Titus at the camp table in his shirt with his head down, writing to his father for leave to fall back, in the short hard lines he wrote everything in, the pen nearly through the paper; and she at the tent-pole with her spear, because that was where she stood. He had sanded it and left it lying. Late, with the lamp burned low, he had come back to it and written one line more at the foot, with his back to her, and sealed it; and she had never read it. In the morning a rider had carried it off up the causeway to the boats. It had gone up this stair, and come down it again to this table; and in the end the boats had brought back the table's answer: hold. He had held. They had brought him home and laid him here under this dome, with his hair combed, she supposed, which he never could abide, while she was still on her way home. And now the same council, at the foot of the same stair, had thanked his brother loudly for a ship, and shut the doors on the city, quietly, and asked him who gave him leave.

None of it was for saying. Not here. Not to them.

The Marshal turned to the thing he could touch.

"Captain," he said. "You had my letter."

"I had it, my lord."

"It ordered you to hold the Prefect within the waters of Cyrene, and, if you could not, to say so." The ring went into his thumb, and came away. "The council will hear what you say now."

She had given it to them twice, in ink. She had never yet given it to their faces.

She gave it in the voice she had read the writ in on the quay at Cyrene, in front of the town and the cohort: the drill-field voice, flat and slow and loud, each word set down by itself and left where it lay. It was the only voice she had for things that mattered.

"He sailed without leave," said Valeria. "I went with him."

The dome took it. It carried it up into the windows of the drum where the light was, and brought it down again off the curve of the stone onto the table and the clerks and the lords, word by word, as if somebody up there were saying it after her; and then it went away among the pillars, and the hall was quiet.

Somebody at the table laughed, once, short, and stopped. Lord Opimius said "*Insolence*," to nobody. At the end of the table a clerk's pen scratched, and halted, and scratched again.

The Marshal did not look surprised. He looked like a man who had been sent the same sentence twice and been right both times about what it meant.

"Enter it," he said to the clerk. "As the captain gave it."

Then he tried the table, one face after another, and none of them offered him anything. Last of all he looked up at the empty chair.

"The council will lay the matter before His Majesty," said the Marshal, "with the Prefect's answer and the captain's, in their own words. Until His Majesty is pleased to speak to it, the Prefect and his captain will hold themselves at the council's disposal." He sat back. "The council thanks the Prefect."

The clerk wrote it. He sanded it, and tipped the sand off into a crack between the flags, and laid the sheet on his pile. Nobody put a seal to it. The summons had come south with a column of seals under it, the Marshal's at the top; this had none. It was not that kind of paper. Not yet.

The doors opened as quietly as they had shut. Outside on the steps, in the snow, the file of the Lanciarii waited with the porters where she had left them, and turned as she came out; and she knew by their faces that the oak had not been thick enough. The dome had carried it.

"They'll have heard that on Vessa, Captain," said Galba.

Behind him the porter held out the Farrier to her, head down, by the middle, like a broom.

---

By dark it had gone down the hill.

Her own men had carried it from the doors, because it was theirs as much as hers; the clerks had carried it home to their suppers; and by the time the lamps were lit along the Lower Canal it had got down to the waterfront and was going from mouth to mouth in the cookshops and on the boats, a little different in every mouth that had it.

Felix came for her at dark.

"You've had nothing since the Marshal's beef, Captain," he said, on the top landing, with his hat on.

"I'm on duty."

"So's my mother." He waited. "Galba has the street door. And the Chair has His Highness."

The Chair had been on the second landing when they came down off the hill, with the door open and the women round her in their black, and had come up after with an empty basket and her eyes red at the rims; and the prince had taken one look at her and shut the door of the room under the roof.

"Strictly," said Felix, "there's nobody up here to keep him alive from but her."

His mother's house was halfway up the Fishmarket Steps, narrow, with a smell of eels about it, which she sold off a board on the bottom step every morning and had sold there, Felix said, since before he could walk. Inside it was one low room with a fire, and a great many children who were none of them Felix's, and it smelled of frying fat and pine smoke. His mother was small and broad, with forearms like a cooper's and a voice that could have called the hours from the top of the Old Hill. Her name was Gemella. She looked Valeria up and down once, the mail and the plaits and the scar and the spear, and sat her by the fire without asking who she was, and put a plate in her hands.

Blood sausage, fried with onions till the skins split. A wedge of pie, eel and pork together under a lid of crust gone black at the edges. A heel of the Midwinter loaf, dark and heavy, with figs in it, and a cup of small beer. Valeria ate all of it. The sausage was the best thing she had eaten since the autumn, and she said so, and Gemella said she knew.

They were talking about it on the Steps, naturally. Gemella had had it from the woman at the next board, who had had it from her son, who rowed for the harbor watch, who had had it from a clerk.

"And this Marshal says to her," said Gemella, with her fork, "in front of all them lords, *Captain, what have you to say for yourself?* And she draws herself up"—Gemella drew herself up, and dropped her voice into her boots—"and she says, *He sailed without leave. I went after him.*"

Felix put his cup down. "*With*, Mother."

"What?"

"*With.* Not after." He looked at the fire.

"With, after." Gemella waved the fork. "What's the odds? She went."

"There's odds," said Valeria.

Gemella looked across the fire at her for the first time.

"And who asked you, my girl?"

Felix made a noise into his beer.

In Gemella's telling the captain was a head taller than the prince, and had looked the Marshal in the eye until he sat down, and there had been a crown in it somewhere, and a fish; and the Steps were glad of her. Valeria sat with the last of the pie warm in her hands and waited for it: the thing that had come up in her every time this city admired her for Hollowmere, the thing like a hand at her collar. It did not come. They had got one word of it wrong, and it was the only word she would have minded, and Felix had put it right.

She tried it on there by the fire, as she would have tried on a new mail-shirt, for the places where it would gall. It did not gall anywhere.

---

Word came up the Steps in the morning, by one of the Lanciarii off the *Answer*, that a clerk had been down to the *Patience* with a paper: Captain Aulus Nerva to attend at the Hall of Kings at the third hour.

The prince was already up. Valeria had heard the boards of the room under the roof go before the light, and cross to the window, and stop there, and not come back; he had not slept. He was dressed when the word came. He heard it and did not look surprised. Whatever it was, he had been waiting for it since the Grey. Valeria did not know what it was. She knew the look.

She sent Galba and Laenas down to the *Patience* for Nerva, so that the harbor watch, which had a long memory and a warrant in its books, should not trouble itself about an unpardoned man walking up the hill on his own. He came between them with his coat brushed and his white-tipped beard combed down his chest, and was silent all the way up the Old Hill.

At the doors the same porter was on. He took the Farrier from her in both hands, the right way up, and said, "The Farrier, Captain," and stood it in the rack by itself, head up, and chalked no mark over it, there being no need. She did not ask him where he had got its name. She could guess.

The hall was empty. The council's table had been carried out, and the benches, and the floor swept, and the cold had the place to itself. It smelled of cold stone and old incense, and of nothing else. Light came down grey from the windows of the drum and lay on the marble under the dome. At the head of the hall the king's chair stood on its step with the cloth over its arms, and the men stood on the stair-door, looking at the far wall, and high up on a ledge in the dome a pigeon walked about and complained of the cold to nobody.

They had not waited long when the door of the stair opened, and the king's steward came down out of it.

He was an old man in black, with a lamp he did not need, smaller than she remembered him. She had seen him come down that stair a great many times, for Titus. He looked at them without anything at all in his face.

"Captain Nerva," he said.

Nerva took his cap off. He looked at the stair, and then at the prince. Then he went up it after the steward, and the door shut behind them.

Valeria had waited at the foot of this stair before. For Titus, more often than she could tell: Titus going up it at a run with a dispatch in his hand, and coming down it a step at a time; and she at the foot, reading his answer off the way he came down before ever he opened his mouth. She stood where she had stood then. The prince stood where his brother used to come down.

The cold came up through her boots and settled in her knees. The prince would not sit, though there was a step to sit on, and he would not walk about. He kept his hands behind him and his eyes on the door, and waited for whatever was coming down it; and she asked him nothing. Her orders were to keep him alive, and the stair was the one place in the isles she could not follow him; so she stood at the foot of it and kept him alive there.

It was a long time. The grey light moved on the marble under the dome. The pigeon gave up and went to sleep.

Then the door opened, and Nerva came down.

He came down slowly, with one hand flat on the wall, as a man comes down a ladder he does not trust. At the foot he stopped, and looked at the prince. His face was wet. There was no weather on the stair. His mouth was shut hard in the white of his beard, and his long sea-pale eyes had gone somewhere a long way off and not come back. His other hand was flat on his breast under his coat, over nothing that she could see.

She knew that hand. She wore one like it, over a letter. So the king had given him a paper, then; and by his face it was a bad one. She had seen that face on men coming out of a tent where a court had sat on them, and had expected to wear it herself before Midwinter.

"He told me," said Nerva, to the prince.

That was all. He put his cap on, and went past them down the hall, and out through the doors into the snow, and down the hill toward the harbor.

The prince did not ask him what, or go after him. He stood at the foot of the stair. Valeria could not read his face, and she could read most faces. It was not surprise. Whatever the king had told Nerva at the top of that stair, the prince had known it was coming down.

The steward had come down behind Nerva as far as the bottom step, with his lamp.

"His Majesty will see the Prefect tomorrow," he said. "Alone."

"Tell him yes," said the prince.

The steward inclined his head, and went back up, and the door shut on the stair.

*Alone.* Valeria looked at the shut door, and thought of the right-hand drawer at the top of the stair, where a paper lay with a blank at its foot for a king's name. The king had kept it there since the autumn and never signed it, with the Marshal standing over him. He had not come down for her sentence. He had sent for an old sea-captain instead, and now for his son.

She did not know what to make of him. She had never had to know what to make of a king. There had always been Titus in between.

She went down her list with him. He was not a thing that might kill her, though he could. He was not a thing for later; later had come north with her, and was standing at the top of his stair. In the end she put him where she had put the elf, in the third column, the one with no heading.

Then she took her place at the prince's left shoulder, at the foot of his father's stair, where she had stood for his brother, and stood to it.

Nobody had given her leave for that, either.
