# Chapter One: The Hours After

**MARCUS**

They came round the Kettle in the morning, on the young flood, with the sun behind them.

The shoal was smoking. It always smoked on a still day, a long low breath of white lying flat along the water over the warm ground, and the *Forward* went through the edge of it with her oars dipping slow and the smell of it coming in over the rail: wet stone, and sulphur, and something under those that was only Cyrene and could not have been anywhere else. Men on the benches lifted their heads at it. Nobody cheered. They had cheered the last time, coming home with the iron. This time they breathed it in and bent to the oars again, and the oars went in together, and the *Forward* came out of the smoke into the clear with the headland on her bow.

Marcus stood at the rail forward with his cloak open, because the sun was on him and he wanted it. He had slept, in the last of the dark, sitting against the foot of the mast with his head on his knees, and he had dreamed of the dip in the gorse and the torches going down into it, and woken with his neck stiff and his mouth dry and the taste of the dream still in it. Felix was not here to put water in his hand. He had got his own from the cask, and drunk it, and come forward to watch the town come.

It came all at once, as it always did: the black beach and the two moles with their towers, and the long quay white with steam, and the houses going up the hill in steps to the grey of the fort. In the morning light it seemed smaller than he remembered and nearer, like a thing painted on the side of a cup.

The quay was full.

He had not thought it would be, at this hour. The masts would have been seen from the headland at first light; somebody would have run down the hill with it; the town would have come out as it was, with the bread half made and the nets half mended. He could see the colours of it from here, a long uneven fringe along the edge of the stone, shawls and aprons and the dark coats of the fishermen, and a child up on somebody's shoulders, and Tuccius's boys in their two ranks at the foot of the quay stairs, small as pegs.

They were not waving. They were standing still, all along the quay, with their faces turned to the sea.

They were counting the boats. He could see it from the rail; he could almost hear it. The *Forward*, and the *Constancy* astern of her with Nerva's figure at the steering oar, and the transports, and the hulls the dwarves had built, and the captured Tharsian round-ship that wallowed at the back of the line like a cow following a herd: one and another, coming round the Kettle in their file, out of the smoke. He watched the fringe of people along the quay take them in. He watched the last boat come out of the smoke and the quay see that it was the last, and the whole long line of it ease, all together, like a held rope when the weight comes off it.

Every boat was there. He had brought them all back.

Then the town began on the faces, and it was still again.

He glanced up the hill, from habit, to the end of Netmakers' Row, high up, where Prisca's window looked out through the gap.

Her lamp was lit.

It was full morning. The sun was well up over the eastern mole, and the whitewash of the Row was bright with it, and in the small square window at the end the red lamp was burning, low and steady, with its back to the room. He could see it from the sea. In daylight it was hardly a light at all. It was a little warm place in the white of the wall, like a coal that has been forgotten in a grate.

He did not know what it meant. He kept it in sight all the way in, past the mole-head and the tower, where a man with a lancea stood at the parapet with his arm at his side, and through the gap into the harbor.

---

Valeria was at the rail beside him when the *Forward* came alongside. She had come up from somewhere aft without his hearing her, and she stood with the Farrier grounded beside her and her face set toward the quay.

"I've three to write," she said.

"I know."

"Their people are at Callow. Two of them. The other's from the Long Field." She had a board under her arm, with a sheet pinned to it, written closely in her square hand and then not written on for half its length. "I've started them every day since the strand."

"Do you want me to—"

"No." The thumb of her right hand was on the haft, on the worn place, and stayed there. "They were mine. They'll have it from me." She looked down at the board. "It's the bank. I want to say why I couldn't get down to them, and every way I say it, it sounds like I'm asking them to say it was all right."

He had nothing to give her for that. He stood beside her, and the plank banged down, and the quay was so near that he could see the faces now, one at a time, every one turned up to the rail.

"It wasn't all right," said Valeria, to the board. "That's the trouble. I'll write that."

She tucked the board under her arm, and took up the Farrier, and went to see her files down.

---

He went down the plank first, because he always had.

The quay met his boots hard and went on moving after it had stopped, as it did after a long passage. The steam was all round his knees. The noise of the town rose round him: not cheering, not yet, a long low rising sound of a great many people saying names under their breath, and a child somewhere asking a question over and over in a high clear voice, and nobody answering it.

"Highness."

It was Felix.

He was standing at the foot of the plank where he had no business to be, inside Tuccius's ranks, thin and stooped in a clean shirt, with a jug in one hand and a cup in the other and the expression of a man who had been told twice to stand back and had decided not to hear it a third time. He held out the cup.

It was water. It was cold, and it tasted of the fort's spring, of iron and stone, and Marcus drank it standing on the quay with the cohort coming down the plank behind him, and gave the cup back.

"You walked that down from the fort," he said.

"It's downhill, Highness. Strictly." Felix filled the cup again and held it, ready, with his eyes past him up the plank at the men coming down. Felix looked for faces and saw them, one and another, and his mouth tightened when he did not. Felix had stayed. He had kept the tower room and the fort's accounts and Marcus's other shirt, all spring, and had stood on this quay to see them go.

"Tappo," said Felix.

"Yes."

Felix nodded, and stood there with the full cup.

Tuccius was at the head of his boys, short and broad as a bollard, with his helmet under his arm and the new white seam through his eyebrow gone pale now and flat. He stepped forward a pace.

"Held, Highness," he said. "All spring. It's written."

"Was there anything?"

"Boats. Twice. They looked at us and went away." Tuccius rubbed the end of his nose with the back of his wrist, quickly, and fixed his eyes on the *Forward*'s rail. "It's written. Afternoon, if you like. I'll not keep you now." He moved his head a little at the quay behind him. "They'll want you now."

Marcus turned round, and the town was coming in off the edges.

---

It was not a rush. The cohort was down on the stones now, and the Lanciarii behind them, and the men of the transports coming down the other planks along the quay; and the town spread in among them slowly, one and two and a family, the way the flood comes in over flat sand, in a great many small channels at once. A woman got her arms round a man's neck and kept them there. An old fisherman took Macro by both his ears and studied him. Rufo stepped down with his helmet in his hand and Sylara's fair lock plaited over his ear, and three net-wives who had never been introduced to him had hold of him before he reached the bottom. Oppius never stopped on the quay at all. He went straight through it with a sack on his shoulder, toward the foot of Rope Street and the bakehouse door, and the town got out of his way.

Marcus walked up the quay.

He had thought about this on the passage. He had stood at the rail through the whole wet passage and thought about it, and made a kind of order in his head: the women of the dead first, then the people of the missing, then the rest. He had the names in his coat. He had Pen's sheet of the volunteers in its oilskin, with Galba's men on it in Pen's upright hand, and he had gone down it every night by the lantern and put a mark against the men who had come back, and the sheet had fewer marks than names.

Now he was on the quay, and the order went out of his head, because the town would not come at him in order. It took him as he was. A woman he had never seen took his hand and held it and said "*Thank* you," with her husband standing behind her grinning, whole, with a bandage on his head, and let him go. A boy ran at his legs and was snatched back. And then a man of the third century who had married into the Row at Midwinter, and whose wife's people had come down in a body, saw him through them, and took him by the arm, and walked him into the middle of them, and said, "That's him. That's the one I said." The wife's mother looked at Marcus with her hands folded under her apron, and then gave him one nod, the nod a creditor gives a man who has paid what he owed.

He went on.

He knew where the net-wife would be. She would be by the foot of the Row, where the stair came down to the quay, because that was where she had stood at Midwinter and where she had stood the last time, and where she had come out of the line to take Tappo's face in her hands and set his cap straight. He saw her from twenty paces off. She was standing on the bottom step with her shawl down round her shoulders and her arms at her sides, a small woman with a net-wife's hands, cracked across the knuckles and brown, and she was looking past him at the cohort, at the men coming up the quay, one face and then the next and then the next.

She saw him coming. She saw his face. She stopped looking at the cohort.

He stopped in front of her.

"He was at Bastion," Marcus said. "On the last day. In the retreat."

She waited.

"Liora was hurt, on the bank, and he and four others carried her down the slope on a shield, to the General. Horse rode at them. They got her there." He made himself go on. "He was the last of them. He got her to the General's feet, and he got her there alive."

The net-wife looked at him. Her mouth had gone small.

"His cap," she said.

"Liora has it. He had it on him. She took it, after, and she's kept it." "It was the last thing that was his. She's kept it in her bag."

The net-wife nodded. She nodded several times, quickly, looking at the stones of the quay between his boots.

"I sewed it at Midwinter," she said. "Out of the ends. There was a bolt of the king's red, the ends of it, and they let us have them." She was not crying. Her hands had come up and were holding each other in front of her, the cracked fingers turning over and over. "He'd no hat. He'd come in the autumn with no hat, and his ears red with the cold. I said, *that one wants a hat.*" She stopped. "He sang at me. On the Row. Every time he went by."

"I know," said Marcus. "I heard him."

"He was a thief," said the net-wife. "Did you know that? He told me. He said, I was a thief in the capital, missus, I'll not lie to you, and I said I'd lock up my nets, then." Something went across her face that was nearly a smile and was gone. "He said, I'll sing to them instead."

She looked up at Marcus then, for the first time, straight in the face.

"Did he know he'd done it? That she was alive?"

Marcus thought of the five shields going down the cut at first light, Tappo's last. He thought of what Liora had said on the road, which he had not heard, and which Roderic had told him afterward at the rail.

"Yes," he said. "He knew."

The net-wife nodded once more. Then she turned round and went up the stair into the Row, slowly, one hand on the wall.

He stood at the foot of the stair until she was out of sight.

---

Sosius's sister was harder to find, because he had never seen her face.

He knew her because she was the one person on the quay who was not looking at the cohort. She was standing by herself near the end of the fish-market, a young woman with a baby on her hip and a parcel in her other hand, tied with string, and she was looking at the *Forward*, at the plank, where the last of the men were coming down. Every time a man stepped off the plank she searched his face, and turned back to the plank for the next.

He went to her. He said her brother's name. She understood it before he had finished, and not let herself, and then let herself.

He told her the rest, as Roderic had it from Liora: that Sosius had been hit at the top of the swell, carrying the shield, and had told nobody, and had run the rest of the way down with his hand pressed to his side, and gone down twenty paces short.

She heard it all with the baby on her hip, looking at his face.

"He wouldn't tell you," she said at last. "He'd never tell you. He'd not tell you his feet were wet. That's why I send them." She glanced at the parcel in her hand as if somebody else had put it there. "I'd got these ready. For when you came in. I thought, he'll have worn through the last."

The baby reached for the string, and she let it.

"You can give them to somebody," she said. "Can you? Somebody who needs them."

"Yes."

She held the parcel out to him. He took it. It was soft and light and warm from her hand.

He went on up the quay with it, because he could not think what else to do, and he was still carrying it at the head of the quay.

---

There were others. He went to them all.

Galba's men were the hardest, because there was nothing to tell. He had Pen's sheet, with the marks against the names of the men who had come down the road at dawn; and there were people on the quay, here and there, who had come down for a man whose name had no mark against it, and who had already seen, from the plank, that he was not coming off it. A fisherman of the Row with his cap in his hands. An old woman from the yard's cookhouse, who was nobody's mother that he knew of and had fed one of them all winter. A girl. He went to each of them, and he told each of them the same thing, because it was the only true thing he had.

"He turned back," he said. "At the end, in the gorse, for men who were down. Nobody saw him after."

"Is he dead?"

"I don't know."

A death they could have taken home. The fisherman asked him twice. "You don't know." / "No." The old woman said, "Then I'll wait," and went and sat down on a bollard at the edge of the quay with her back straight and her eyes on the gap, as though another boat might come through it. Perhaps she was right.

When he had been to them all, he went and stood at the head of the quay by the harbor master's door, with Sosius's socks in his hand, and he was shaking. Not his hands. All of him, a little, like a sail held too close to the wind.

Felix was beside him with the jug.

"That's the last of it, Highness. The ones who came down for someone." He filled the cup and held it out. "Strictly, you've seen everyone."

Marcus drank the water.

"How did you know?"

"I've stood here since the masts came round the Kettle." Felix took the cup back. "I watched who walked down the hill, and who they looked for. It's not hard, from here. You can see a great deal from the head of a quay if you keep still." He held out his hand for the parcel. "I'll put that by."

"They're socks," said Marcus. "Sosius's sister. She wants them given to somebody who needs them."

Felix weighed the parcel in his hand for a moment. Then he put it inside his shirt, carefully, flat against his chest, as he had once carried Marcus's letters up the capital's stairs to keep them out of the rain.

"I'll find him," said Felix. He looked up the hill, at the end of the Row. "Prisca's had that lit since you sailed, Highness. Day and night. Oil's dear. She said she didn't care."

---

The town had begun to cheer, a little, behind him. It came up out of the crowd in odd places, where somebody had got somebody back, and ran along the quay and died away again, and came up somewhere else. Somewhere a pipe started a tune and lost it.

Roderic went by at the far edge of it, in his good coat with his beard combed out for the town, walking fast up the quay toward the crown's road and the stable with three of Tuccius's boys trotting after him, and not stopping for anyone. A fisherman tried to take his hand and was given it, shaken, and left behind. He had been away from his horse since the spring.

Liora came down the transport's plank with her bags, last, after the wounded.

She had walked the ship all night every night of the passage, and it showed. She was in the white, washed in salt water and dried in the wind until it had gone the grey of an old sail, and her hair was tied back in a knot at the nape with a piece of cord, honey-gold and dull with the salt. She stepped onto the quay and stopped at the foot of the plank and stood there with the bags on her shoulder, and the town saw her.

It went along the quay from the plank outward. People turned, and saw her, and stopped what they were saying. A woman put her hand to her mouth. And then they moved; not to her, not touching her, but toward her, a slow shuffle of them along the stones until there was a ring of the town round the foot of the plank with a space in the middle and Liora in the space; and they stood there and gazed at her.

Liora stood in the space a moment.

"I've men to get up the hill," she said. "To the Baths. Some of them can't walk." She hitched the bags on her shoulder. "If anyone has a cart."

Somebody had a cart. Somebody else had a door off its hinges for a litter. By the time Marcus had crossed the quay they were lifting the first of the wounded, and soon there were a dozen men of the town carrying them up the crown's road toward the Baths, at a walk, with Liora beside the first of them with her hand on his chest; and the ring had broken up into a line along the road to watch her go by.

Tuccius's report was on the table in the harbor master's house when he reached it in the afternoon, under a stone. It was in Ennia's big straight hand, as the last one had been, and it was longer, because the girl had been given more to write and had written all of it.

*The Prefect's home guard held Cyrene from the day the fleet sailed to the day it came back. Two Tharsian boats stood off the Kettle in the spring and went away. One stood off again at the next new moon and went away again. Nobody landed. Pullus is now a file-leader. The bakehouse oven cracked and Fausta mended it with clay from the black beach. The prisoners asked if they could stand a watch on the slip gate and the home guard said no. Then the home guard said yes. Nothing went wrong.*

*Said by Tuccius. Written by Ennia, of Netmakers' Row.*

Marcus read it twice. He put it inside his coat with the other papers. Tuccius was standing by the door with his helmet under his arm and his eyes on the window, waiting to be dismissed.

"You let the prisoners stand a watch."

"Asked, Highness. In the spring. Their own officer brought it." Tuccius's eyes stayed on the window. "Said they'd as soon not be burned in their beds by their own side as by ours. I thought about it a fortnight. Then I let them have the slip gate, nights, with two of mine." He paused. "It's in the report."

"It's a good report."

"She's particular," said Tuccius, and a muscle moved in his jaw.

Marcus nearly asked him then. He nearly said: *Do you know what I did at the gate? Has it come here yet?* He did not, because he could see in Tuccius's square stubborn face that the man did not know, and he could not bring himself to be the one who told him in this room, with Ennia's report in his coat saying *Nothing went wrong*.

"Thank you, Tuccius," he said.

Tuccius nodded, and went out, and Marcus heard his boots on the stair and then in the street, going away at a drill pace, and stood alone in the harbor master's room with the red lamp unlit on the sill.

---

The room over the slip gate smelled of lamp oil and ink and the sea, as it always had.

The Navarch stood up when he came in. He did it as he always did, unfolding from the stool by the window in sections, tall and stooped under the low beams, in the long black sea-coat, and he made the small exact bow he gave everybody, from Marcus to the boy who brought his water, and said, "Highness. You are home."

"Navarch."

"Sit, if you will. The other stool is sound." The Navarch did not sit until Marcus had. He sat, and folded his long white hands on the table in front of him beside the book, which lay open at a page half written. The pen lay across the page. The window behind him was open to the harbor, and Marcus could see the masts of his own fleet through it, at their moorings, quiet.

"I saw you come in," the Navarch said. "From here. I saw the town count your boats." He studied his folded hands. "It is a thing I have seen done on a good many quays. It is the same on all of them."

"They were all there."

"Yes. I saw that." The pale heavy-lidded eyes came up. "And then they counted the faces. That is also the same on all of them."

Marcus said nothing.

The Navarch's right hand left the table and its fingers closed on a stylus that was not there, and had not been for a year; and opened again; and took up the pen instead.

"How many did you leave on the field, Highness?"

Marcus told him.

He gave him the number he had from Liora, and from Valeria's three on the bank, and from the five who had carried Liora down the slope; and then he gave him the number of Galba's men who had not come down the road, and said that he did not know if they were dead. He said it all in a level voice, looking at the Navarch's hands, and the Navarch heard it out without moving. Then he dipped the pen and wrote, in his small Tharsian hand, and Marcus watched the figures go down on the page and could not read them upside down.

The Navarch blotted the line.

He asked nothing else. Marcus had been ready for it all the way up the stair, and it never came, and he sat with the readiness in his chest and nowhere to put it.

"You don't ask what happened," he said.

"No." The Navarch laid the pen down along the edge of the book. "You will tell me, I think, or you will not. Either way I shall hear it. Your quay talks, and my clerks hear it from the quay." He paused. "I asked you the number because the number is mine to write. The rest is yours."

"I could have had the gate," said Marcus.

He had not known he was going to say it. It was out in the small room over the slip gate in the afternoon light, flat and hard, and he heard it with something like surprise, as though somebody else had said it in his voice.

The Navarch was silent for a time.

"Tiberius has two hundred people on his books," Marcus said. "In the quarry pens. The harbor town under the stair, the ones who missed the boats. He'd written them off to Livia. At the gate he offered them to me: take the gate, and they're hers. The gate was standing open." He heard his own voice go on, level. "I turned the cohort round in it."

The Navarch's hand lay flat on the open book. It did not go to the pen.

"Yes," he said at last. "Well." He looked down at the line he had written, and then up at Marcus again, with no expression on his long pale face that Marcus could find. "That is not a thing I write, Highness. I have no column for it."

He closed the book.

---

They lit the first fire on the quay by the slip, at dusk, where they had lit them every homecoming since the town was theirs.

The town brought the meat down. It arrived on spits and in pots, carried down the crown's road and the Row and Rope Street by women and boys, without anyone having asked for it: a whole sheep, and a pig split and spitted, and sausages in long dark coils, and fish, cod and gurnard and the flat grey things the boats brought in off the Kettle that nobody outside Cyrene had a name for. Fausta's bread followed in baskets, steaming, with Oppius carrying the biggest of them and walking beside her like a man who has been told where to walk. The smoke of the fat went up straight into the still evening and hung over the harbor, and the smell of it drifted along the quay, mutton and pork-crackling and burning fish-skin and the sour hot crust of the loaves; and men who had eaten salt pork and biscuit since the strand sat down on the stones and ate with both hands, without talking.

Marcus sat on the edge of the slip with a heel of bread and a rib of mutton, and ate it, and was hungry, which surprised him. Felix had a cup of water at his elbow before he had sat down, and stood behind him in the firelight, as he had stood behind Marcus's chair at a hundred palace suppers, looking at nothing in particular.

Alana sat down beside him on the slip's edge with her own bread, and for a time neither of them spoke. She had been up the hill to the tower, and changed, and come down again, and there was clean ink on her fingers already. She ate with her left hand. Her right lay in her lap, palm up, and every now and then it trembled, a small fine shake that went through the fingers and was gone, and she kept her eyes off it.

"Rusca has the weather-book," she said, when she had eaten. "She asked for it. She wants to put something right."

"In your book?"

"In her own column. I gave her one." Her eyes were on the fire. "She's exact about it. I'm not to look until she's done."

Down the quay the cohort had got round its own fire, and somebody was singing, badly, and somebody else was telling him so.

The lamps were coming on up the hill.

They came on one by one. He had watched them come every evening of the winter from the tower: one and then another, up the steps of the town, the windows going gold with candles and then, here and there among them, the low red light of a Tharsian clay lamp set with its back to the room. Prisca's, at the end of the Row, which had burned all day. The brothers' two: Fausta's over the drying loft, Tuccius's in the harbor master's window at the head of the quay.

Then another.

It was halfway up Netmakers' Row, in a window he had never noticed. It came on as he watched, low and red, and steadied.

He had no need to ask whose.

And then, a little after, down by the fish-market, in a room over a chandler's, one more.

It was not Cyrene's custom; it was Tharsis's. But the town had seen the brothers' lamps every night all spring.

Beside him Alana had seen them too. Her lips moved, and stopped.

"Two," she said, under her breath, and then nothing else.

Down the quay the singing had stopped. He turned his head.

Roderic was standing up.

He stood at the edge of the cohort's fire with his cup in his hand, in his good coat, and the firelight on the grey of his beard, and the cohort had seen him stand and gone quiet round him, as a field goes quiet under a hawk. He turned his eyes past them to the two new lamps, and then down to the cup in his hand. Then he put the cup down on the stones beside him, carefully, and straightened, and spoke up the quay in the voice he had used on parade grounds for thirty years, not loud, and carrying to the slip.

"Bruttius," said Roderic. "Of the fourth century. A net-maker's son, of the old harbor."

The quay was still. Down at the far fire the Lanciarii turned on their benches. Up the crown's road a woman carrying a pot stopped where she was.

"Trebonius. Of the fourth. A drover. He had the bridle, and he held it." Roderic's voice did not change. "Egnatius. Of the second. A cooper's man, from Barrel Lane. He'd no shield; his shield was under her. Sosius. Of the fourth. The youngest. He was hit at the top of the swell and told nobody."

He stopped. Marcus saw his chest go up and down once.

"Tappo," said Roderic. "Of the cohort. A thief from the capital, and a singer. He got her there."

He stood a moment longer with his hands at his sides. Then he bent, and took up his cup, and sat down.

Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. Marcus could hear the fire, and the water slapping under the quay, and somewhere up the hill a dog. On the far side of the slip Liora was sitting on an upturned basket with her bags at her feet and a bowl of broth she had not touched in her lap, and she had heard all of it. Her face was turned toward Roderic across the quay, and the corner of her mouth went in, and stayed. A little way off from her Gaius sat on a bollard with his bad leg out straight and his bread on his knee, and he was looking at her, not at Roderic.

Roderic drank from his cup.

Then Rufo, at the cohort's fire, said "*Tappo,*" as if the man had only gone out of the room and might hear; and somebody else said it, and somebody else, all round the fire, under their breath, not together; and down at the Lanciarii's fire somebody said "*Sosius*"; and it went along the quay in the dark like that, the five names, in no order, until it stopped.

Then the man who had been singing began again, quietly, and this time nobody told him anything.

Rusca was at the table in the tower room when he came up, with the lamp drawn close and Alana's weather-book open in front of her and a pen in her hand.

He stopped in the door. He had come up the stair ahead of Alana, who had stayed at the fire to see Liora up the hill, and he had forgotten, coming up, that the room would not be empty.

The quaestor did not look up. She was writing, slowly, in her small upright clerk's hand, in a column ruled down the outside edge of the right-hand page that he had not seen before; and when she came to the end of a line she wet her thumb, and dried it on the square of linen in her cuff, and turned back a page, and checked something there, and turned forward again.

"I'll be finished directly, Highness," she said, still not looking up. "She said I might."

"I know. She told me."

He crossed to the window. The harbor lay black below it, with the fleet's riding lights, and the fire on the quay burned down to a red bed, and the town's windows going up the hill, and among them, here and there, the red glow of the lamps.

Behind him the pen scratched, and stopped.

"It was the dip," said Rusca.

He turned round.

She had laid the pen down. She sat with her hands flat on either side of the book, her face grey with tiredness, and looked at him.

"On the brow," she said, "you asked me what they'd send, and when. I said the fed levy, after the second hour. Among the boats." She touched the page. "That's what I wrote, at the time. I've struck it. They came at the dip, not the boats, and at the third hour, not the second, because your rearguard was in the dip and they had to go through it. I had the levy right and the place wrong." Her mouth went thin. "I don't like having the place wrong."

"You had the levy right."

"Yes." She ran her eye down the column. "I've entered them here. The ones who didn't come back."

He crossed the room and looked over her shoulder. It was in Tharsian, in her small hand, and he could not read most of it; but he could read the heading at the top of the column, which was a single word, underlined, and he knew that word in Tharsian. He had seen it in the Navarch's books and in the Survey's dockets at Stoneheart.

*Lost.*

"Not dead," he said.

"No." Rusca's thumb was on the linen in her cuff. "In our books a man you can't account for isn't dead. You can't strike him, because you don't know. He's lost. He's carried. His pay runs on, and somebody owes it, and one day somebody has to come and find out whether to pay it." She closed the book, carefully, on her finger, as though it might bruise. "I don't know what your books do, Highness. That's how ours do it. I thought she'd want it written down, so I wrote it."

"Thank you."

"It's only a column." She stood, and gave him the weather-book, and he took it. "Good night, Highness."

She left. He heard her on the stair, going down and round to the west tower, and the Lanciarius on the landing say something, and her answer, and a door.

---

Alana came up a little after, slowly, with her bad arm held in to her side.

"Liora's at the Baths," she said. "She won't sleep. She's sitting up with the boy from the *Forward* whose leg went bad on the passage. She says it'll be all right, and she says she'll sleep tomorrow." She saw the weather-book in his hands. "Rusca's done?"

"Yes."

"Did you look?"

"She showed me."

Alana nodded. She took the book from him and opened it on the table at the new column, and her eyes went to the heading, and her lips moved once over the word. She closed the book and stood with her left hand flat on its cover.

"I'm so tired, Marcus," she said.

He said, "You told me to ask you again at home."

She went still.

He had not meant to say it like that, in the middle of the room, with his boots still on and the smoke of the fire in his coat. He had meant to wait until morning, or until she had slept, or until he had some way to come at it that was not this, a beaten man standing in a tower room asking a tired woman a question for the second time.

"You said, *Ask me again at home.* This is home." He did not move toward her. "What's in the new column?"

Alana stood across the table for what seemed a long time. Her left hand stayed flat on the cover of the weather-book.

Then she smiled. It was not much of a smile; it was tired through, and her eyes were wet, and there was ink at her temple where she had pushed her hair back with a dirty finger; but it was hers, and it was for him, and it reached all the way through her face.

"Not yet," she said.

"Not yet?"

"Not yet." She came round the table, and put her good hand flat on his chest, over the place where his papers were, and stood there with her forehead nearly touching his chin. "You'll know when. I promise you'll know when."

He put his arms round her, carefully, on account of the arm. She leaned into him. She smelled of salt and smoke and ink, and her hair was coming down out of its pins on one side, and she was warm.

"Ask me again," she said against his coat. "Keep asking. I like it."

**MARCUS**

The cutter came on the eighth day, in the afternoon, on the flood.

By then the quay had the gate. Marcus could not have said who brought it; the Navarch's clerks, or the transports' crews in the wine-shops, or the cohort itself, in its sleep. He knew it had come by the way the net-wives stopped talking when he passed, and then, after a moment, went on.

He saw it from the yard. He had gone down to see Gaius's second keel, which had been laid while they were gone and which Gaius was now taking up again, timber by timber, because the man who had laid it had not laid it the way Gaius would have. He was standing on the slip with his hands behind his back being told about scarfs when one of Tuccius's boys came running down from the mole-head with the word.

A sail. Off the Kettle. White, with a sun on it.

Gaius stopped talking.

He did not say anything. He stood on the slip with his rule in his hand and his weight on his good leg. His eyebrows had grown back since the moor to a short fuzz and no more, and they were drawn down; he stared out past the end of the slip at the gap, where there was nothing yet to see.

"It'll be the Vigil," Marcus said.

"Yes," said Gaius. "I expect it will."

He put the rule away in his belt, and went on looking at the gap.

---

It was a small old cutter, white-painted and peeling, with a patched white sail with the returning sun stitched on it in gold thread gone brown at the edges. Marcus had seen it before, two days out from the fen in the spring, when it had spoken the fleet with Cassandra's report for the capital and he had turned the fleet for Ghoul's End. He knew it as soon as it cleared the Kettle.

It came in through the gap on the flood and rounded up neatly to the long quay, and two sisters in white jumped down onto the stones with lines in their hands; and half the town, which had seen the sail, came down to the quay to see who it was.

The plank came out. It came out badly, as it happened: the cutter's boy shoved it out from the rail and it came down on the edge of the quay with one corner on the stone and one corner on nothing, and rocked.

A woman came to the rail of the cutter and considered it.

She was heavyset and solid, and not tall, in a white cloak over a mail shirt that had been let out at the sides at least once, with a plain steel cap and a coif under it so white and so starched it seemed to stand off her head by itself. She had a short spear in one hand with a small bronze bell at the socket, and her own bag in the other; and she had forearms like a ship's carpenter, bare to the elbow, and a broad weathered face with grey eyes in it that were looking at the plank as though it had been brought to her for an opinion.

She put the bag down, and shifted the spear into the crook of her arm, and with one finger she tapped the end of the plank twice where it lay on the rail.

"That's laid wrong," she said. "It'll tip." She looked down at the cutter's boy. "Has nobody ever shown you? You lay a plank on a quay with the cleats *down*, so they bite on the stone, and the long end inboard. That's not a plank. That's an accident with a rope on it."

The boy stared up at her. The cleats were up.

He turned it over. It lay on the stone flat and square, and stayed.

"Thank you," said the woman, and picked up her bag, and came down it.

She stopped at the foot and took in the quay, at the steam and the town and the cohort, who had come up from the yard and the barracks to see the white sail and were now standing in a loose untidy crowd at the edge of the quay, staring at her. She went over all of them, slowly, from one end to the other, and Marcus had the clear impression that she was counting things that were wrong.

Then her eyes came to him, and stopped.

"You'll be the Prefect," she said. "The prince."

"I'm Marcus."

"Sister Domitia. Of the White." She did not bow, and she did not hold out her hand. "I'm sent to you in the healer's place, Highness. By the Abbess. There's a letter for you about it, and one for the healer." She glanced past him, up the quay. "Is she here?"

---

Liora was there.

She had come down from the Baths when the sail was seen, as half the town had, and she was standing at the edge of the crowd by the foot of the crown's road with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her hands still red from the basin. She had been working. There was a smear of something on her apron. Marcus saw her see the cutter, and the sun on the sail, and the sister on the quay; and he saw her face go still.

She walked down the quay. The crowd let her through.

The sister from the cutter's bow, a thin young woman Marcus had never seen, came to meet her halfway, with a letter held out in both hands. It was folded small and sealed in white wax, and the seal was the returning sun.

Liora took it. She studied the seal for a moment. Then she broke it with a fingernail, and opened the letter, and read it standing in the middle of the quay, with the town round her and the cohort at the edge of it and the cutter's boy coiling down a line behind her; and Marcus watched her read it.

He could not see the words. Her eyes went down the page once, and then back to the top and down it again, slowly; and her lips did not move, and her face did not change, and her hand that held the page not shake. When she had read it twice she folded it along its creases, and stood holding it.

Then she held it out to him.

He took it. It was a short letter, in a firm square clerkly hand, and it was addressed to *Liora, of the Vigil of the Returning Sun, healer with the Prefect's army at Cyrene*, and signed *Cassandra, Knight-Abbess*. The order claimed its training and its hands. It thanked her, in a formal line, for her service in the field. It required her home to the house by the Lower Canal, in the capital, for a vigil of reflection, at the order's need and for a time the order would set. It said that a sister would be sent to take her place with the army, and that the cutter would carry her north on the evening ebb.

That was all. There was no why in it. It said nothing about Bastion, or Ghoul's End, or any of the things Marcus was looking for in it as he read; and there was nothing in it, anywhere, that a man could have put his finger on and called wrong.

He gave it back to her.

"I can send it back," he said.

She looked at him.

"I'm Prefect of Cyrene. You're under my command, with the army, and the army's under the crown." He kept his voice low. The town was all round them; the cohort was at the edge of the quay, close enough to hear if they listened, and they were listening. "I can write to the Abbess that I need you here, and the crown needs you, and the order can have you back when the war's done. She can't take you off my quay if I don't give you up."

"No," said Liora. "She can't."

"Then let me."

Liora was quiet for a time.

The corner of her mouth went in.

"It's my order's house, Highness," she said. "I walked into it behind a barrow, when I'd nowhere else to walk. They took me in and taught me what I know, and never asked me to say anything I couldn't say." She turned the folded letter in her hand. "They've asked me home. They've not sent anyone to make me. They've asked."

"She hasn't said why."

"No." Liora turned the letter over in her fingers. "I expect she'll tell me, when I'm there."

"Liora—"

"I won't fight my way out of anything," said Liora. She said it quietly, not to him, to the letter; and then she lifted her head and said it again to his face. "I don't. You know I don't. I've never fought my way out of anything in my life, and I'm not going to start by fighting my way out of the house that took me in." She folded the letter once more, small, and put it in her apron pocket. "I'll go."

He opened his mouth.

"Let her be, Highness," said Sister Domitia.

She was beside them, with her bag and her belled spear, and she was looking not at him but at Liora: top to bottom, as she had looked at the plank and the quay and the cohort, as though there would be something to put right. She looked at Liora a long moment, and her finger stayed still on the spear.

"She's said she'll go," said Domitia. "That's the answer. She'll have thought about it longer than you have." Her eyes went to Liora's red hands, and the apron, and back to her face. "You'll want to finish whatever that is you've left at the Baths, Sister. I'll come up and see it, if you'll show me. The cutter sails on the ebb. That gives you till dusk."

Liora looked at her.

"Thank you," she said. "Yes. Come up and I'll show you."

They went up the crown's road together, the tired healer in the grey-white and the heavyset woman in the starched coif with the spear, side by side, not talking; and the cohort at the edge of the quay watched them go by, and not one man of them said anything.

---

They walked down again at dusk.

Liora had her bag now, and Tappo's cap was in it; Marcus knew that without seeing it. Halfway along the quay she lifted her head and looked up Netmakers' Row, once, at a window there, and then went on.

The town had come to see her off. Marcus had not asked them; nobody had asked them. They lined the long quay from the foot of the crown's road to the cutter's plank, in the evening light with the steam coming up round them, and they stood back from her as she came, and some of them touched her sleeve as she went by and some only wanted to.

The cohort was there, all of them, standing along the edge of the quay by the cutter in no order and no rank, without their helmets. As she came along the quay toward them Fingers stepped out of the line and held out his hand for her bag; and she gave it to him, and he carried it the last twenty paces to the plank and set it down at the foot of it, on the stone, and stepped back into the line, with his eyes on the stones.

Gaius was at the plank.

He had been sitting on a bollard at the foot of it since the sail was seen, with his bad leg out straight in front of him and his tablet on his knee. Marcus had seen him there all afternoon, from the quay and from the window of the harbor master's house, in the same place. He was not drawing anything. He did not get up now. He sat with the tablet on his good knee and watched her come.

Liora stopped in front of him.

Neither of them said anything. The town was behind her and the cutter behind him, and the sisters on the cutter's deck were waiting, and the boy had his hand on the line.

She bent. She put out her hand, and laid it on his knee, the bad one, flat, through the wool of his breeches.

Gaius went rigid. Marcus saw the stubble of his eyebrows go up as far as they could go, which was not far. He sat there with her hand on his knee, and Marcus could see from where he stood the red-gold come into the rims of her fingers, faint in the dusk.

Gaius let it stay there for the length of one breath.

Then he put his own hand over hers, and lifted it off his knee, and held it a moment, and gave it back to her.

"After," said Gaius.

Liora straightened, and looked down at him.

"After," she said.

She picked up her bag from the foot of the plank. She went up it, and the boy cast off, and two sisters on the stern pushed off from the quay with their spear-butts; and the cutter swung out into the harbor on the first of the ebb with the sun on its sail going red in the last of the light.

The town on the quay stood and watched it go.

Marcus did not.

He watched Gaius instead. Gaius had got up off the bollard as the lines came in, slowly, with a hand on it, and he stood on the quay with his bad leg straight and kept his eyes on the white sail all the way down the harbor and through the gap and out past the mole-head into the dusk, until there was no more sail to see; and his hand was still a little way out from his side, where it had given hers back.

# Chapter Two: Sister Domitia

**GAIUS**

The keel was down again by the morning of the ninth day, and it was right.

He had taken up every timber the boy had laid and laid it again himself, the long scarfs first, then the hog over them, and he had sighted down it from the stern end on his belly on the slip with one eye shut while the boy stood by and did not say anything. It was right now. It ran from the cradle at the head of the slip to the cradle at the foot like a line ruled on a page, and he could put his eye to the end of it and see nothing between his eye and the stem but oak.

He had slept beside it. He had not slept much.

The yard was hot already. Early summer had come to Cyrene while they were gone, and the steam off the town's gutters, which had stood up white over the roofs all winter, lay low and thin in the mornings now and burned off before the first watch was out. The pitch-kettle by the shed door was going, and the yard smelled of hot pitch and green oak and the sea, and under all of it the bad-egg breath of the springs, which a man stopped smelling after a week and started again whenever he had been away. He had been away all spring. He smelled it everywhere.

"The garboard," said Velthur's boy.

"I know."

"You said to tell you when the—"

"I know." He got up off the slip, the bad leg first, and the knee told him about the night on the shavings. "Bring it."

The boy fetched it. He was fifteen, or sixteen, a long thin dark boy with a Thessan nose and Velthur's plane in his belt, and he had laid the keel all spring with nobody to tell him how, from the frames in the loft and what he remembered, and he had laid it a finger out at the third scarf. A finger. Gaius had seen it from the quay on the first day before he was off the plank. He had said so. He had said it in front of the boy, and of half the yard, and the boy had gone the colour of the oak and said, "Yes, master," and gone and got the adze; and on the eighth day, while the cutter came in, he had still been taking it up.

It was right now. He kept his eye on it.

At the far end of the shed, past the stacked frames and the steam-box, the house stood with its door shut on the latch. He had shut it himself last night, after the cutter, because the wind had come round and it was banging. He had not gone in. He had stood in the doorway with his hand on the bar and smelled bread that was eight days old and the herbs she hung over the hearth, and the cold hearth under them; and he had taken his hand off the bar, and gone back to the slip, and lain down by the keel.

There was a smell of fresh bread coming down the yard now, from Fausta's at the foot of Rope Street. At noon, most days, she had come down the hill from the Baths with Fausta's bread under a cloth and made him eat it sitting on the keel, and argued with him about mortar.

It was the second hour. The bread was not coming.

"Master," said the boy, with the garboard plank on his shoulder.

"Not there. Under the shed, in the shade. If the sun gets on it it'll cup." "You know that. I know you know that."

"Yes, master."

"Put it down."

---

She came down into the yard a little before the third hour, on her round.

He heard her before he saw her. There was a small bronze bell at the socket of her spear, under the head, and it spoke when she walked, once at every second step, not loud, a dry little *tink*; and the yard, which had heard a great many things come down the crown's road in the last year and stopped for none of them, stopped for that. He saw two of the sawyers in the pit look up, and a boy at the pitch-kettle, and Rusticus and Salvius, who were caulking the old liburna on the hard and had been arguing about it since dawn, stop arguing.

She stopped at the head of the yard, where the crown's road came down onto the cobbles.

She was in the white, and the steel cap, in this heat, which he would not have worn for anybody; and she had a wooden case under her left arm, the kind a physician carries, with a strap. She took in the yard: the slips, the sheds, the stacked oak, the steam-box, the sawpit and the men, one after the other, and he had the feeling that she was writing something down.

Then she came down the yard, and the bell spoke, and she passed the keel without a glance and carried on round the end of the shed toward the sea-wall.

He knew what was round there. He put the adze down.

The cohort's trench was round there. He had sited it himself, in the winter, when the cohort came back from the Teeth and filled the old barracks on the sea-wall and the barracks' own pit had been under the Tharsian garrison's ash-heap for a year. He had walked the ground and taken the fall and pegged it, a deep trench and a soakaway at the low end, well away from the slips and the water-casks and down the slope from everything, and the cohort had dug it in an afternoon and been grateful. He had been pleased with it.

She was standing at the low end of it, by the soakaway, with her nose wrinkled, looking at the ground.

"Who dug this?" she said, without turning round.

"The cohort."

"Who told them where?"

He considered several answers.

"I did," he said.

She turned round then. Her eyes took him in: the leather apron, the lenses pushed up on his forehead, the adze-dust, the knee. They were grey, and not unfriendly; they were the eyes of a woman at a badly made joint, seeing how badly.

"You're the engineer," she said.

"Yes."

"I was told about you." She did not say what. She turned back to the soakaway and pointed the butt of her spear at the ground beyond it, where the slope went on down toward the sea-wall and the gutter that ran along the foot of it. "Where does that go?"

"Down. Under the wall. Out."

"Does it."

"It's the low ground. I walked it. It falls a—" he stopped himself, because she would want a number and it was none of her business— "it falls toward the wall. Everything falls toward the wall. It's a yard on a slope, Sister. I didn't put the slope there."

"No," she said. "You put a latrine on it." She walked a few steps down the slope, away from the trench, with her head bent. At the foot of the slope, in the angle of the sea-wall where the barracks' cookhouse stood, there was a stone curb in the ground and a wooden cover on it, and she stopped beside it and tapped the cover twice with the end of one finger.

"What's this?"

He knew what it was.

"The cookhouse well."

"How deep?"

"It's not deep. It's the spring. It comes up warm under the whole yard. They sank a shaft to it here so the cooks wouldn't have to carry from the fort."

"And when it rains?"

He said nothing. He was seeing it. He had walked the ground in the winter, in a hard frost, when the clay of the slope was frozen hard except where the warmth came up, and nothing soaked anywhere, and nothing ran; and he had pegged his fall and gone back to his hull. He had not walked it in summer. He had not walked it after rain. He saw the soakaway full and the wet going down the slope through the made ground, under the cobbles, through the old warm channels the spring had cut for itself; and coming out at the bottom, into the shaft, where the cooks dipped the cookhouse kettles every morning.

"It's warm ground," she said. "Warm ground drinks. Somebody told you a trench wants a fall, and you gave it one. You gave it one right into the cooks' pot." She took her finger off the well-cover. "I've seen a camp go down with the flux in a fortnight from less. The Lanciarii's camp below Hollowmere, the year before the war. We buried a score. They'd dug theirs above the horse-lines."

"Nobody's had the flux."

"It's not rained," she said. "It will."

He saw the line between the trench and the well as plain as a chalk line; and the sun came down on the back of his neck, and he hated her with a pure clean hatred he had not felt for anybody since the quaestor's man with the rod. She had seen it in the time it took to walk round the end of a shed.

"Where?" he said.

She pointed with the spear-butt. Across the yard, the other way, past the sawpit, toward the corner by the old slip where nothing was and the ground fell toward the open harbor and away from everything.

"There," she said. "Fill this one in with lime. And shift the soakaway, or don't have one. Let it go to the sea. The sea doesn't mind."

It was where he would have put it. If he had walked the ground in summer.

"I'll have it moved," he said.

"Today."

"Today."

She nodded, once, as though that were the end of a page; and climbed the slope past him, and past the keel, and up the yard toward the crown's road, until the bell was out of hearing.

He came back round the end of the shed. Rusticus and Salvius were standing on the hard by the liburna with their mallets in their hands.

"Well," said Rusticus.

"She's right," said Gaius.

"About what?"

"Get spades," said Gaius. "Both of you. And lime from the kiln. And the boy."

---

They had the new trench dug and the old one limed by noon. The adze turned on a knot after that and went into the side of his hand, below the little finger.

It was not much, and it was his own fault: he had been holding the adze too far down the haft, and a man who holds an adze angry gets what it gives him. He wrapped it in a rag and kept on, and the rag soaked red through, and after a time the boy, who had been watching him, said, "Master," in a voice he had not used before, and Gaius saw that he was bleeding on the keel.

He went up to the Baths.

It was a long way up for the knee in the heat, by the crown's road and the stepped lane. He had not been up since the morning they came home. Halfway up he stopped to rest the leg on a step, and stood there with his hand wrapped in its rag against his apron, over the yard and the harbor and the gap, where yesterday the white sail had gone out; and then he went on up, because he was dripping on the step.

The Baths were the Baths. The long hall full of steam, and the old pools, and the smell of the warm water and the sulphur and the lye; the pallets under the high windows, half of them empty now in the summer; the still-room at the end, with the herbs hung over it and the old Vigil shelves. He had slept in that still-room, back to back with her on a mat, a good many nights. He did not look at the door of it.

Hostus was at the basin.

He was a thin stooped man of the cohort's grey-heads, with a barber's long careful fingers and a face like a closed shutter, and he was washing his hands. He washed them in the hot basin from the spring, and then in the lye-water, and then in the hot again, slowly, between the fingers and under the nails and round the wrists, as carefully as other men pray; and dried them on a clean cloth, one finger at a time, and hung the cloth over the rail.

"Engineer," he said. "What have you done."

"Adze."

"Sit."

He sat on the bench by the basin, and gave Hostus the hand.

And then Sister Domitia came down the hall from the far end, where she had been standing over a pallet, and stopped beside them while Hostus unwrapped the hand.

It was a clean cut, in at the side of the palm below the little finger and along the edge of the hand, not deep, and bleeding freely.

Hostus turned it to the light from the high window, and pressed the edges of it together gently with two fingers, and let them go.

"It wants sewing," Hostus said. "Three. Four. Then he keeps it dry."

"He won't," said Sister Domitia. "You'll be in the pitch by tomorrow, and the bilge water by the next day, and the stitches will be black by the end of the week." She leaned her spear against the basin. "Sew it, Barber, by all means. And then bind it so it can't open. Not a rag. Linen, and a pad, and bound over the pad, and the hand in a sling." She jerked her chin up the hall at the pallet she had come from. "Not like *that*."

On the pallet at the far end, in the shade, lay Ear, whom she had sent up from the yard that morning on her round, with a gash along his shin from a dropped oar on the passage home and a strip of shirt tied round it, which Gaius had seen him tie himself, sitting on the quay, with his teeth.

The strip of shirt was still on it.

"I didn't like to take it off," said Ear, from the pallet, unhappily. "It's holding."

"That's not a dressing," said Sister Domitia. "That's a hope with a bandage on it." She marched back up the hall to him; and Gaius heard her, behind him, while Hostus threaded a curved needle at the window, saying things to Ear in a low flat voice about what a man ought to know by nineteen, and Ear saying *Yes, Sister*, and *Yes, Sister*, and once, *Ow*.

Hostus sewed the hand.

He was quick, and his hands were clean as a new plank, and Gaius, who had been sewn before, watched the needle go in and out and felt it from a long way off. Then Hostus washed his hands again, as before; and Sister Domitia came back down the hall from Ear with the strip of shirt in her hand, held out from her by the corner, and stopped at the basin to wash her own.

She watched Hostus's hands.

He had finished, and was drying them on the clean cloth, one finger at a time. She stood beside him at the basin with her own hands still wet. Gaius saw her right hand come up off the rim of the basin with one finger out, the finger that tapped things. It came up, and stopped, and stayed where it was, a little way above the stone, while Hostus dried the last finger and hung the cloth over the rail.

The finger went down again.

She washed her own the same way, the hot and the lye and the hot, and dried them on a cloth of her own, and said nothing.

Then she took the linen and the pad and bound up Gaius's hand herself, over Hostus's stitches, tight and flat and neat, in a figure that crossed at the back of the wrist, and tied it off; and looked at the knee.

He was sitting with it out straight in front of him, because the climb had stiffened it, and she considered it as she had considered the cutter's plank.

"That knee," she said.

"No."

"I haven't asked you anything."

"No," said Gaius.

Neither of them moved.

"It's an old injury," she said. "It's gone in the joint. I can see it from here, how you hold it. A sister could look at that. I could look at it now, it'd take me the time it takes to boil a kettle. It wants seeing to before it sets worse, and it will set worse, at your age, in a shipyard, on stone."

"It's waiting," said Gaius.

She opened her mouth, and shut it again; and something went across her face that he could not read, and was gone.

"Keep it dry," she said, and nodded at the hand, and left him.

---

The cohort ate on the sea-wall in the long summer evening, as it had every evening since they came home, at the trestles outside the old barracks where the wall was broad and warm and caught the last of the sun.

Oppius had a sheep on the spit over the cookhouse fire, and the fat dripping and spitting in the coals, and Fausta's bread in baskets down the trestles; and there were onions done in the embers in their skins, and a pot of beans with pork in it, and somebody's jar of the yellow Cyrene wine that tasted of the sulphur, going round. The smell of the mutton lay along the whole wall. The sun dropped behind the fort over the hill, and the harbor turned gold and then grey, and the steam came up off it in the cool.

Gaius ate on the end of a trestle with his hand in its sling, badly, with his left. He had stood over the new trench one-handed all afternoon, and it was right; and he had not spoken to anybody, and his hand hurt.

Sister Domitia came down from the Baths at sunset and walked the length of the trestles, slowly, looking at everybody's supper, and stopped behind Fingers and watched him eat. Fingers ate as the cohort ate, as men eat who have spent years in a prison where the slow went hungry: standing, bent over the trestle, with his knife in one hand and his bread in the other, and the meat going into him in pieces too big for the job, so fast he barely chewed.

She tapped the trestle twice beside his bowl.

"Sit down," she said. "And chew it. You'll none of you have a gut left by forty, eating like that. I've opened men who ate like that, after. Their insides were like a cart-road."

Fingers stared up at her with his mouth full.

Then he sat down. And, as the trestle watched, chewed.

She went on down the wall, and stopped behind the next man, and the next, and by the time she reached the end of it the whole cohort was sitting on the wall in a long row eating its supper slowly with its mouth shut, like a row of magistrates; and she took a bowl of the beans herself, and a heel of bread, and sat on the end of the trestle opposite Gaius, and ate them, not slowly, and went back up the hill as the light went.

They watched her go. Nobody said anything until the bell at the socket of her spear had gone all the way up the crown's road and round the turn.

"Right," said Fingers.

He put his bowl down. He stood, and went into the barracks, and came out again with a broom that had been leaning by the door, and pulled a handful of straws out of the head of it, and stood in the middle of the wall and broke them on his knee, one short, all the rest long, and shuffled them in his fist behind his back, and held his fist out, with the ends of the straws showing even above his knuckles.

"Every man," he said. "Draw."

"For what?" said somebody.

"For who has to be nice to her."

There was a silence along the wall.

Then the cohort began to laugh, in the low tired way it laughed now, and to get up off the trestles and come and draw. It passed down the wall from man to man. Fingers held the fist steady and watched each man draw, and each man drew and laughed, or swore, and threw it in the harbor. Ear drew. Pen drew, with his long white fingers, delicately, and held his straw up to the last of the light and said, "Saved by the length of a straw. It's how I've lived." Oppius drew, and Rufo, who had come down from the fort with Sylara for his supper, drew and grinned at it and put his hand up to the plait over his ear.

Salvius drew long.

Rusticus drew short.

He held it up between finger and thumb, as though a magistrate had handed it to him.

"No," said Rusticus.

"Short straw," said Fingers. "It's the law."

"It's a broom."

"It's the law of the broom," said Fingers, and opened his fist, and wiped it on his breeches, and got out of his shirt the chalk he kept for the purpose and squatted down by the barracks wall where there was a clean place on the stone. The tip of his tongue came out between his teeth. He wrote *Rusticus* on the stone, big and uneven; and under it, a line, and on the line, the odds.

"How long he lasts," Fingers said, without looking up. "Before she has him crying. I'll take a day. I'll take a week, at long odds. Nobody's taking a month. Oppius? A day?"

"A day," said Oppius. "On the first hour."

"I'll take the morning," said Pen.

"You're all very funny," said Rusticus.

"Hold on," said Hostus.

He was sitting at the far end of the wall, at the end of the last trestle, with his back to the cookhouse and a bowl of beans on his knee, where he had been all through supper. Gaius had not noticed him there. People did not notice Hostus; it was the main thing about him. He put the bowl down on the trestle beside him now, and wiped his mouth on a clean cloth, and folded the cloth, and stood up.

"I'll do it," said Hostus.

The wall looked at him.

"You didn't draw," said Fingers.

"No."

"You can't take a man's short straw if you didn't draw. It's—"

"I know. The law of the broom." Hostus came along the wall. He was not a big man, or a loud one, and he did not raise his voice now; he stopped by Fingers, over the chalk on the stone. "I work under her. Every day, up there. I'll be nice to her anyway, or we'll kill one another by the end of the week, and then there'll be nobody to sew Ear up." He turned to Rusticus. "Give me your straw."

Rusticus gave it to him, at once, and stepped back as though from a fire.

"She'll eat you," said Fingers.

Hostus considered this.

"I've pulled teeth for thirty years," he said. "I have talked kindly to worse-tempered people with my hands in their mouths."

Fingers squatted by the stone with the chalk in his fingers and his eyes on Hostus.

Then he rubbed out *Rusticus* with the heel of his hand, and wrote *Hostus*, and drew the line under it, and squatted back on his heels and turned to the wall.

"Well?" he said. "A day? A week?"

Oppius had become interested in the sheep.

"Nobody?" said Fingers.

Nobody.

Fingers crouched over the stone a moment longer; and then he rubbed the whole of it out, odds and line and name, with the flat of his hand, and stood up, and dusted his hand on his breeches, and went to get his bowl.

Hostus sat down again at the end of the wall and picked up his beans. He rubbed the back of his neck once with his free hand, and went on eating, slowly, with his mouth shut.

---

The next morning, Gaius went up to have the hand looked at, because the binding had come loose in the night and Hostus had said to come.

It had come loose because he had slept on it on the slip, by the keel. That was nobody's business. He went up the crown's road in the cool before the heat came, with the sun just over the eastern sea and the town's steam lying in the lanes, and the harbor below him flat as a plate, and the gap; and at the top of the stepped lane, by the door of the Baths, where the hot spring came out of the rock into its stone trough and steamed, he stopped, because Sister Domitia and Hostus were standing by the trough.

They had been washing at the trough before the day's work, side by side; and now they had stopped.

Sister Domitia had Hostus by the chin.

She had taken hold of it with her right hand, firmly, finger and thumb either side, the way a woman takes the chin of a child who has been at the honey, and turned his face up to the light. Hostus stood quite still and let her. He was a little taller than she was, and she had to tip his head back to do it, and he stood with his head tipped back and his clean hands hanging at his sides, as a man stands for a sentence.

"Open," said Sister Domitia.

Hostus opened.

She looked in, as Gaius had seen Hostus look into a hundred mouths at the Baths, with the side of one finger pulling down his lower lip so that she could see the gums. She turned his chin a little to the left, and to the right, and took her time about it, and Hostus stared at the sky.

Then she let go of his chin.

"Well," she said. "Those are the best teeth in the camp."

Hostus shut his mouth.

"We're taught it," said Sister Domitia, wiping her fingers on her apron. "The old books. You judge a man's diet by his teeth and his gums, before you judge it by anything he tells you, because he'll lie about what he eats and his mouth won't. I've been in every mouth in your cohort that'd let me since yesterday." She sniffed. "Bread and salt meat and sour wine, and half of them have been at the honey-cakes in the capital since they were boys. Ruins. I've seen better in a sheep." She took him in, the stoop and the long fingers and the clean apron. "Not yours. Yours are good. Every one. Who looks after them for you?"

"Nobody," said Hostus.

His voice was not quite his own.

"I've drawn other men's," he said. "Thirty years. Every tooth in Tanner's Lane." He stopped. He seemed to be looking for something, in the air above her coif, and not finding it. "Nobody's ever—" He stopped again. "That's the first time," said Hostus, "anybody's looked."

"Well, they should have," said Sister Domitia briskly. "It's a good mouth. You've looked after it. Keep on." She picked up her spear from where it leaned against the trough, and the bell spoke. "Ward's at the next bell. Don't be late."

She went in.

Hostus stood by the trough in the steam. He put up his hand and rubbed the back of his neck, slowly, and went on rubbing it, staring at the door of the Baths; and the back of his neck, above his collar, under his hand, had gone a deep and spreading red.

Gaius discovered that he had urgent business with a keel.

He went down the stepped lane again, fast for the knee, and down the crown's road with his hand in its sling and the binding loose, and into the yard, and stood at the head of the slip and studied the keel, hard, until he could trust his face.

Fingers was sitting on the sawhorse by the shed door with his breakfast.

"You're back quick," Fingers said. "She do your hand?"

"No."

Fingers considered him. Then he turned his eyes up the hill, toward the Baths, where there was nothing to see but the roofs and the steam; and the tip of his tongue came out between his teeth.

He got up, and went to the barracks wall, where yesterday's chalk was still a grey smear on the stone; and got out his chalk, and wrote *Hostus* again, small, low down where only a man looking would see it, and drew a line; and under the line, after a moment's thought, wrote *OIL*.

"For what?" said Gaius, who had followed him.

"Her lamp," said Fingers. "A man who's sweet on a sister of the White keeps her lamp in oil, all year, till Midwinter. Everybody knows that. It's the oldest joke in the army." He wrote odds. They were very short. "How long before he's buying her oil, is the question."

"You've only got him."

"Only need him." Fingers stood back from the wall. "I'll take a month," he said. "Anybody'll give me a month."

---

The *Patience* brought the mail on the fifteenth day, on the morning flood, as she did every week.

Pen met her. He always met her. He would stand on the long quay by her plank with his weight on one hip in the sun while the bags came off, and take the capital's bag on his shoulder up to the harbor master's house, and sit on the step there in the shade and sort it, as he had done all winter, by feel through the canvas before the strings were off and then by eye, into piles on the step: the Prefect's, the Navarch's clerks', the town's, the cohort's. He could tell a letter from a woman by the feel of the fold.

Gaius was not waiting for him. He was on the slip with the boy, setting up the garboard, and he had his back to the quay.

"One for the engineer," said Pen, at his elbow.

He had come down the yard without Gaius hearing, as he did, with the cohort's letters in a bundle under his arm and one held out separately between two long fingers. He held it out to Gaius now. It was a half sheet, folded small, with plain white wax and no seal in it.

"Half a sheet," said Pen, "by the feel." He was off up the yard with the bundle.

Gaius put down his end of the plank. The boy, at the other end of it, held it.

The hand was hers. He had seen it on a great many things, a list of stores and a note on a cloth over a bowl and once, on the night of the house, three lines on the back of a drawing; a small rounded clerkly hand, a court hand, from some schoolroom with a governess in it a long time ago. He broke the wax with a fingernail and read it standing on the slip.

*Gaius,*

*We came up the canal on the third morning, on the flood. The house is the same. The steps to the water are worn deeper in the middle, and somebody has whitewashed the gate. I have a room on the court side with a window that opens, and a bed, and a shelf.*

*I am working in the infirmary in the mornings with the sisters, and at the gate in the afternoons, where the canal people come with what's wrong with them. It is mostly lungs, and children's ears, and what comes of the lock-gates.*

*I eat at the bells. Tell Oppius the bread here is worse than his, and that I said so.*

*Is the keel down?*

*L.*

He read it again, more slowly, as he would have gone over another man's drawing: for what was on it, and then for what was not.

There was nothing that was not. It was a letter.

He folded it small and put it in the front of his apron, behind the bib.

"Master," said the boy, holding the plank. "Is it all right?"

"It's fine," said Gaius. "Lift your end."

---

By then Sister Domitia had begun to eat like Liora.

It went up like a wall a man walks past every day: one morning there was a course, and the next morning there were two, and on the fifth morning it was a wall.

He saw the first course of it on the twelfth day, when she stopped by the cookhouse at the sea-wall on her round, at the hour of the noon bell, and ask Oppius what the healer had eaten. Gaius was at the well at the foot of the slope, drawing a bucket for the steam-box.

"Ate?" said Oppius. He was a giant, with one eye and forearms floured white for good, and he peered down at her from the cookhouse door with the one eye as though nobody had ever asked him a harder question. "Whatever was going, Sister. She wasn't particular."

"Yes, she was," said Sister Domitia.

Oppius thought about it.

"Fish," he said slowly. "When there was fish. Bread. Greens when she could get them, which in the winter she couldn't, and she'd go up the hill for the sorrel by the spring and come back with it in her apron. Water out of the fort spring." He thought harder. "Not after dark. Not anything. You'd bring her a bit of sausage at night when she'd been working, and she'd thank you, and give it to the engineer." His eye slid across to Gaius at the well, and so did hers. "And she walked. Everywhere. Up and down that hill ten times a day, when she could have sent a boy."

Sister Domitia stood in the cookhouse door with her spear.

"That's the old rule," she said, after a time, more to herself than to Oppius. "Out of the books. The rule of the bells. Plain food at the bells, and the spring's water, and nothing after the dusk bell, and the rounds on foot." She made a small noise in her nose. "Nobody keeps that. Nobody's kept it in the tents in twenty years. You can't, in the tents. You eat what comes, when it comes, on your feet, and you're glad of it."

"She did," said Oppius.

Sister Domitia was quiet.

"What's the fish?" she said.

He gave her a gurnard, done on the coals, and a heel of bread, and she ate it standing in the cookhouse door; and then, he saw, she noticed, and went and sat on the wall to finish it, with her spear across her knees, scowling at the harbor.

After that she ate at the bells. There were no bells at Cyrene, except the fort's alarm bell and the one on the harbor master's house that nobody had rung since the raid, so she kept her own, in her head, by the sun; and the cohort learned to tell the hour by her. When she came down to the cookhouse, it was the noon bell. When she went up the hill, it was the dusk bell. She ate fish when there was fish, and when there wasn't she ate the mutton and the bread and the greens, and she grumbled about all of it, the rule and the old books and the women who had written them; and she ate it anyway, and walked her round twice a day, down the crown's road and up again.

On the sixteenth evening, Oppius brought her a piece of sausage, after the sun was down, when she had been at the Baths since dawn with a fisherman's boy who had come off a mole-head onto the rocks.

It lay on its bit of bread in front of her. Gaius, at the next trestle, could smell it: pork and garlic and fennel, still hissing.

"That's after the bell," she said.

"There isn't a bell, Sister," said Oppius, kindly.

"There's always a bell." She pushed it back across the trestle to him with the tip of one finger. "Give it to the engineer."

Oppius pushed it along to Gaius.

He ate it. It was good sausage.

---

The bag had begun before any of it, on her second morning, in the yard. She had come down the crown's road with her physician's case and a heavy roll of linen under her arm, and the spear, and her own bag over her shoulder with the next day's dressings in it, and at the head of the yard Ear stepped out from the shed, and stopped in front of her, and held out his hand.

"Can we help, Sister?" said Ear.

"I'm not a cripple, boy."

"No, Sister." Ear went on holding his hand out. He was nineteen, and the ear that Liora had mended on the beach was a pale crumpled shape on the side of his head, and he had the look of a boy who has been told to do a thing and will go on doing it until somebody tells him to stop. "Only we always ask."

"Who's *we*?"

Ear thought about this.

"Everybody," he said.

Sister Domitia looked past him at the yard: Rusticus and Salvius on the hard by the liburna, who had stopped work to watch, and the sawyers in the pit, and Velthur's boy, and Gaius on the slip. All of them were looking at her, and none of them was smiling.

She took the bag off her shoulder and gave it to Ear.

"Carry it level," she said. "There's bottles in it."

He carried it level, down the yard behind her and round the end of the shed to the barracks, as a man carries a full pail of pitch. After that somebody carried it every day. It was never the same man two days running; Gaius never saw them settle it, and he never saw her ask. Whoever was nearest when she came down the crown's road stepped out and held out his hand, and said what they always said, and she gave it to him, and said, "Level."

She stopped saying, "I'm not a cripple." It took about a week.

---

She corrected the rest of Cyrene as she had corrected his trench. The cohort learned to stand still when they heard the bell, because if they were doing something, she would see it, and it would be wrong.

She corrected how they washed. She had the barracks' blankets out on the sea-wall in the sun every third day, and every man's shirt boiled in the cookhouse copper with lye, and stood by the copper while it was done. She corrected how the Lanciarii stood a watch on the mole-head in the heat (in the shade of the tower, not on top of it, and a bucket of water by them, and a hat). She corrected the way Gaius's sawyers stood in the pit, which had nothing to do with her, and it turned out she had been a sawyer's daughter in the capital; and the pitman's back stopped hurting. She told Valeria, on the long quay, in front of the Lanciarii, that the Farrier's haft had a check in it a finger below the socket and would split in the cold, and Valeria turned the haft to the light, and there was the check.

She was wrong about half of it. She told Sylara the wrong herb for an elf's fever, which Sylara corrected; she told Fausta that bread wanted more salt, and Fausta said nothing, and sent her a loaf with more salt in it, and she ate it and drank a jug of water and never mentioned it again; she told Gaius that his steam-box was too hot, and it was not. But she was right about the other half. And she was right in such a voice, and with such a finger, that the cohort did the wrong half too, mostly, just in case.

The bell at her spear went up and down the crown's road all day. When it stopped, the cohort turned round to see what was wrong.

---

She came to the song on the twentieth night.

They had a fire on the sea-wall that night, after supper, for no reason, as they sometimes did now that it was summer: driftwood from the black beach and offcuts from the yard, burning green and blue with the salt. The cohort sat round it on the wall in the dusk and passed the yellow wine, and Oppius brought out the end of the mutton, and somebody, Rufo, with Sylara against his shoulder, started *The Angel of the Baths*.

It was the cohort's song. It had been the cohort's since the Boundary House, though the tune was older, out of the gaol yards; and they sang it the way men sing a song they have sung on a hundred nights, without listening to it, in a long low rough unison that went out over the harbor and came back off the moles. Gaius did not sing. He sat on the end of the wall with his knee out straight, and listened to it, because it was hers.

Sister Domitia was there. She had come down at sunset for her fish and stayed, which she had not done before; she sat on an upturned basket at the edge of the fire with her spear across her knees and Hostus beside her on the wall, a little way off, not too close, and she listened to the song with her head up and her mouth shut, as a schoolmistress listens to a child recite.

They sang it through. It came to the last chorus, and the cohort sang the chorus, and it ended, and somebody passed the wine.

"That's wrong," said Sister Domitia.

The wall turned to her.

"It's wrong," she said. "You've left a verse out." She lifted one finger off the spear, and tapped the shaft twice. "I've heard it a hundred times in the capital this spring, they sing it on every corner in the lower town, and in the infirmary, and at the Abbess's gate, so I'd know. There's the one about the stair, and then the one about the beach, and then there's one that goes up high before the last chorus, about the dark. Then the chorus. You went straight from the beach to the chorus. You've forgotten it."

Nobody said anything.

The fire cracked. Somebody's foot moved on the stones.

Sister Domitia looked round the wall from face to face, as if waiting for somebody to say she was wrong; and nobody did. They watched the fire. Rufo had stopped with the jar halfway to his mouth, and put it down. Sylara had lifted her head off his shoulder. Ear was looking at his hands.

It was Fingers who answered her, in the end. He was sitting on the wall on the far side of the fire, and he did not look up.

"We've not forgotten it," Fingers said. "That's Tappo's verse."

"Who's Tappo?"

"He sang it on his own," said Fingers. "The high bit. We'd all stop, and he'd sing it, and then we'd come in again on the chorus. He started the song, the first night. At the Boundary House." He was turning a chip of driftwood over in his fingers. "We don't sing it. Not since Bastion."

Sister Domitia sat on her basket with her spear across her knees.

Gaius was on the far side of the fire from her, and he could see her face in the light of it: the starched coif, the broad weathered cheeks, the grey eyes on Fingers. Her finger had come up off the spear-shaft again for the tap. It stayed up.

Then it came down, slowly, flat on the wood, and stayed there.

"Tappo," she said.

"Yes."

She was quiet. The cohort was quiet. Beside her on the wall Hostus was looking at the fire.

"Who else?" said Sister Domitia.

Fingers lifted his head.

"I don't know the names," she said. "She does. The healer. She says them every night, at the end of the day, all of them; the Abbess told me so before I came. I've not learned them." She said it in the flat voice she had used on Ear. "I'm sent in her place. I ought to know them."

The fire cracked.

"Bruttius," said Oppius, from the cookhouse door.

"Trebonius," said Rufo, after a time.

"Egnatius," said Ear, to his hands.

There was a pause, and then somebody said "Sosius", and then nobody said anything, and Sister Domitia sat on the basket and looked at each of them as they said it, and then at the fire, with her lips pressed shut; and after a time she got up, and picked up her spear, and the bell spoke; and she went up the crown's road in the dark with the bell going at every second step, and nobody offered to carry anything, and she did not look back.

Gaius sat on the wall.

He had them all; he had heard them, in their places, every night for a year, in the still-room up the hill. He had the three at the beginning too, that the cohort did not say, the ones that were not soldiers. He had not said any of them.

The next morning she asked Hostus for the rest of them, back to the stair, and Rufo, who had the roll; and that evening Gaius saw her on the long quay with Ear, walking slowly, and Ear talking, and her listening with her head bent, as she had walked the slope of the yard looking for the fall.

---

On the evenings before the *Patience* sailed, Pen wrote the cohort's letters.

He had always written them. Half the cohort could not write and the other half could not spell, and Pen could do anything with a pen that a man could ask of one, and some things nobody had; he sat on the step of the harbor master's house with his lap-desk on his knees and the cohort queued in front of him in the long light, and he wrote their letters home. To mothers in the capital, and to the women some of them had found at Stoneheart and some at the fen; to Fausta, still, for Oppius, though Fausta lived at the foot of the next street and he saw her every morning. To the net-wives of the Row, for the ones who had been courting all spring by Pen's hand without either side ever having been within a mile of the other. Pen wrote them all, and read them back aloud, in his drawling herald's voice, improving them as he went; and then copied each one fair for the bag in his upright clerk's hand, and kept the first draft.

"A man ought to keep a copy," he said, when Rusticus asked him what he wanted with it. "Of anything that matters."

Gaius did not queue. He had nobody to write to but one, and he wrote that himself.

He wrote it on the slip, on his knee, on a page torn from the back of his book of hull lines, in the plain capitals he had learned off the lintels at Corvus as a boy; and it took him three nights to write four lines. *The keel is down. The garboards are on. Domitia moved my latrine and she was right. Oppius says thank you for nothing about the bread.* He read it through on the third night and could not see that it said anything at all, and could not see what else to put; and he folded it and sealed it with pitch, for want of wax, and took it up to Pen on the step.

"For the capital," he said.

Pen took it. He weighed it between two fingers, as he weighed all of them, and set it on the pile.

He was writing a letter for Ear, to Ear's mother, who sold eels at the Fishmarket steps. Gaius stood by the step a moment and watched the pen go. It was a good hand. He had always liked to watch it, the way he liked to watch any man who knew his trade; the downstroke and the hairline, every letter the same weight as the one beside it, nothing wasted. *Your loving son.* Pen dipped, and wrote the date in the corner, the way the clerks did, with the month and the day in figures; and Gaius watched a seven go down, quick and neat, with the little turned foot at the bottom that Pen gave all his sevens. It was a good seven.

He went back to the yard.

---

The second letter came on the twenty-second day. It was shorter.

*Gaius,*

*The canal is low with the summer, and smells of it. A boy from the lock-gates came to the gate with two fingers crushed, and I kept them both.*

*The doors here are shut at the dusk bell, as they always were. I had forgotten how loud the bar is.*

*Mind the knee in the wet. Tell Domitia she's to make Hostus drink his own broth.*

*L.*

He read it standing on the slip, and then sitting on the keel, and then again that night by the lamp in the shed, with his lenses on, as though the writing were small.

A boy, two fingers, a door.

*I had forgotten how loud the bar is.*

He went over it. It was in the same hand as the first, small and rounded and level; the lines came down the page as straight as the first letter's had. But the lines were closer together. She had written it on the same size of sheet, and left half the sheet bare at the bottom, and still the lines were closer together, as though she had been saving the paper.

He folded it, and put it behind the bib of his apron, with the first.

The third came on the twenty-ninth day. It was three lines.

*Gaius,*

*Cassandra came into the infirmary today and stood at the end of the ward while I worked, and then she went out again. I am well. I am eating at the bells.*

*L.*

He turned it over. The back was blank.

The boy, at the other end of the strake they were offering up, held his end and waited and did not say anything. He had stopped saying "Master, is it all right?" sometime after the second letter. Gaius did not know when.

---

On the thirty-sixth day the *Patience* came in on the morning flood with the mail, and Pen met her, and carried the bag up to the step and sorted it; and he did not come down the yard.

Gaius was on the staging at the hull's stern, fairing the frames with an adze. He had taken the sling off the morning after the cut, against everyone's advice, and the stitches had come out black at the end of the week as she had said they would, and Hostus had taken them out without a word and Domitia had looked at the scar and said nothing either, which was worse. He worked. He kept his back to the quay. The morning went by, and the noon; and in the middle of the afternoon he lifted his head from the frame and saw Marcus coming down the yard from the crown's road, alone, with a letter in his hand.

It was not his letter. He knew that as he knew a timber was wrong before he had the rule on it. He put down the adze.

Marcus came to the foot of the staging and stood there with his eyes on the frames and the half-built hull, as he always did in the yard, as though he had come to see the work. The cut on his forearm from the gate night had healed to a pink line; he had the letter folded small in his hand, with the broken white wax on it.

"Come down," Marcus said.

Gaius came down. It took him a time, with the knee, and Marcus did not offer to help, and did not look away either.

"Read it," said Marcus, and held it out.

It was written in a secretary's hand, quick and slanting, on good paper. It was addressed to *His Highness the Prefect of Cyrene*. It presented the Abbess's compliments. It said that the healer Liora was in reflection in the order's house, as the order had required, and would write to her friends when her reflection was finished, and that the Abbess trusted His Highness would understand, and would not look for letters until then. It was signed, *For the Knight-Abbess, by her hand, Sister Philippa, Secretary.*

He gave it back.

"When what's finished?" said Gaius.

"It doesn't say."

"No."

They stood at the foot of the staging. The yard went on round them, the adzes and the mallets and the saw in the pit, and the smell of the pitch, and the sea. The boy was on the staging above them, fairing the frame Gaius had left, slowly, with his eyes on his work.

"I've written to the Abbess," Marcus said. "This afternoon. And to my father." He folded the letter once and then again. "He answers everything. In his own time. The Abbess will answer too, very politely, and it will say this again." He put it inside his coat. "I've no standing in the order's house, Gaius. Nobody has. Not the crown. They built it so, four hundred years ago. A house the king couldn't come into."

"I know."

"So I'll write," said Marcus. "And wait. And write again."

Gaius turned to the hull.

"She said she wouldn't fight her way out of anything," he said.

"Yes."

"She didn't say anything about anybody else."

Marcus turned his head.

Gaius did not look round. He kept his eyes on the run of the frames along the hull, which were fair now from the stern to the turn of the bilge, and would be fair to the stem by the end of the week.

"Don't tell me," said Marcus at last.

"I've nothing to tell."

"No. When you have," said Marcus, "tell me first." He tipped his head at the boy on the staging. "That's Velthur's boy."

"Yes."

"He's good?"

"He's good," said Gaius. "He laid her a finger out at the third scarf. I took it up."

"Did you tell him he was good?"

The boy had stopped fairing, and was standing on the staging with the adze in his hand and his back to them, very still.

"No," said Gaius.

Marcus nodded, and turned, and went back up the yard toward the crown's road, with his hand on the front of his coat where the letter was.

Gaius stood at the foot of the staging.

"Boy," he said.

The boy turned round.

"Your scarfs," said Gaius. "Apart from the third. They were good scarfs."

The boy stood on the staging above him. Then he turned back to the frame and went on fairing it, without a word, and twice he stopped and put his forearm across his eyes, and went on.

---

The book was in Netmakers' Row.

He had lent it to Alana in the winter, for her letters class, and the hot sand out of it had gone onto the Navarchy's marines on the long stepped lane: the only use, so far as he knew, that anybody had ever made of the third volume. The child in the front of the class had asked to keep it to copy the plates. Alana had said she must ask the engineer, and then the engineer had sailed.

He went up the Row in the evening. It was a steep cobbled street of net-lofts and narrow houses with the nets hung out to dry from the upper windows, and the smell of tar and fish-scale and wet hemp in it, and at the top of it, past the net-wife's window where the new clay lamp was burning, a woman at a door who called up the stairs behind her when he asked; and a small dark girl came down the stairs with the book in both arms, held against her chest.

She did not give it to him.

"You're the engineer," said Ennia.

"Yes."

"It's yours."

"Yes."

"I've copied the plates." She held it tighter. "Not all. There's the one with the crane, I hadn't done the crane." She had a thin fierce face and ink on her fingers, a great deal of it, and a front tooth missing. "Do you want it now? For good?"

"For a week."

"You'll bring it back?"

"In a week."

"You've to sign for it," said Ennia, and went back up the stairs, and came down again with a slate and a stub of chalk, and held it up to him; and he wrote his name on the slate in his lintel capitals, and she turned the slate over and checked both sides of it, and gave him the book.

He took it down the Row under his arm and sat with it on the sea-wall by the yard, with the harbor going grey under him, and opened it.

The third volume of the great Anaxis. On war-machines. He had not opened it since Silverwood, and before that not since he was a boy; nobody opened it. The leather was cracked at the spine and the boards were warped, and a child's ink was on the edges of the pages where she had turned them, and a child's slate-dust in the gutters. He turned the pages slowly. Bolt-throwers and their skeins, and the *palintone*, which he knew. Rams. Towers. The cauldrons of the hot sand, which a child had copied out somewhere in the Row, and he wondered how she had done the flames.

He was hunting for something, and did not know what until it was under his hand.

It was near the back, after the towers, a plate he had forgotten was there. Two men under a roof of shields, at the foot of a gate. Between them, on the ground, a thing like a stool: a squat oak frame with a great bronze screw standing up through the middle of it, threaded the whole of its length, and a bar through the head of the screw for two men to turn. The head of the screw was set under the bottom rail of the gate's leaf, where it hung. The text beside it was in Anaxis's dry old crabbed style, which he had loved as a boy and loved still: *A gate hung upon pintles is lifted from them, not broken; and a gate lifted from its pintles has no more strength in it than a shutter in the wind. For which the screw-jack, as here, worked by two men under cover, who raise the leaf the depth of the pintle and a little more, when it will fall outward of its own weight. It is quieter than a ram, and it wants no run-up, and it does not need to break anything.*

He sat on the sea-wall with the book on his knees until it was too dark to see the plate. Then he went up the hill to find Domitia.

---

She was at the Baths, at the long table at the end of the hall under the hanging herbs, writing.

He knew what she was writing. She wrote every night, at the end of the day, by one lamp; a page, sometimes two, in a big plain hand, and sealed it and put it in the Vigil's bag for the *Patience*. The whole cohort knew what she was writing. Some of them thought it was funny and some of them did not.

Her head came up when the door went. It was after dusk. She had eaten nothing since the dusk bell, he knew that too, and there was a cup of the spring's water by her and nothing else on the table but the page and the lamp.

"Engineer," she said. "Is it the hand?"

"No."

"The knee."

"No." He came up the hall. There was a fisherman's boy asleep on one of the pallets, with his leg in splints, and nobody else. "The house," he said. "In the capital. Your order's house, by the canal."

Her pen stopped.

"What about it?"

He had thought about this all the way up the hill, and he had not thought of a good way. He did not have good ways. He had walls, and he knew where to put the weight on them.

"Liora said in her letter," he said, "that she'd forgotten how loud the bar is. On the door. When they shut it at the dusk bell." He stood by the end of the table. "I've been trying to see it. I'm a builder. I see things by how they're made. She said the doors are hung on great strap hinges, a hand thick, iron, bolted through—"

"Pintles," said Domitia.

He waited.

"Strap and pintle," she said. "Not hinges. Hinges, he says." She put down her pen. "The straps are iron, and they run the width of each leaf, three to a leaf, and they're forged with an eye at the hinge end, and the eyes drop over iron pintles leaded into the stone of the jamb. I've oiled them myself, as a novice, every spring for four years. You stand on a ladder with a feather and a pot, and the leaf hangs over your head like the lid of a coffin, and if you drop the pot, Sister Agrippina makes you scrub the porch." She sniffed. "Bolted through. Nobody bolts a strap through the leaf on a door that size. You'd split it. They're clenched."

"Clenched," said Gaius.

"Nails, clenched over on the inside. Every hand's length. I'd have thought a builder knew that."

"It's a big door, then."

"It's two doors. Two leaves." She lifted her right arm off the table and laid it along the edge of it, from the elbow to the fingertips, the strong forearm bare where the sleeve was pushed back for writing. "That," she said, "and that again, and a bit, for the width of each leaf. And the height of the porch roof. Oak. Four fingers thick. Ledged on the inside, not braced."

"May I?" he said.

She considered him, and the boxwood rule he had taken out of his apron; and then, after a moment, she held her arm still.

He laid the rule along it, from the point of the elbow to the tip of the longest finger. It was a strong arm, broad at the wrist, with an old white scar across the back of it and the skin brown to the elbow from the summer. He read the rule, and took it off, and put it away, and did not write anything down.

"You've the wrong end of your rule," said Domitia.

He had.

"It's the same length either end," he said.

"Then it doesn't matter, does it." She took her arm back and pulled her sleeve down over it. "Anything else, Engineer? Or can I finish my letter."

"The bar."

"The bar," she said, "is oak. It was meant to be iron. Some abbess a hundred years ago ordered an iron one from the smith on the Canal Steps and died before it was paid for, and they never got round to it. It drops into iron keepers, one on each leaf and one each side in the stone, and it takes two sisters to lift it, or Sister Agrippina, who is from the hills. It's lifted from the inside." She picked up her pen. "Of course it is. What use is a bar that lifts from both sides?"

He said nothing.

He had built one. It was on the door at the end of the shed, with a lifter through a slot in the leaf, and no lock. It stood on the latch now, with nobody behind it.

"None," he said.

She dipped her pen.

"Good night, Engineer," said Domitia, and began to write again. "Mind you're not out after the bell."

He went down the hall. At the door he turned once. She was bent over the page in the circle of the one lamp, her coif white as a sail; and the pen was not moving. Her eyes were on him.

He went out.

---

He did not sleep on the slip that night.

He lit the lamp in the shed, on the bench under the loft where the frames were stacked, and opened the book at the plate, and put Ennia's slate-dust out of the gutter of it with his finger, and set his rule beside it and a clean board, planed, and a stub of chalk; and he began to draw.

He drew the jack first.

Not Anaxis's jack. Anaxis's would do for a gate in a war, with men to spare and time to set it, and a frame like a stool that two men could carry. His would have to go in under a roof of shields in a street and be set and turned in the time it took the street to notice; it would have to lift a leaf four fingers thick a pintle's depth and a little more, and do it quietly, and break nothing. He drew the screw. He drew the frame round it and threw the frame away and drew it again, lower, and then again, with a shoe to take the bottom rail of the leaf and a bed to spread the weight on stone; and the lamp burned down in the shed, and the harbor was black outside the door. He trimmed the lamp, and sharpened the chalk on the edge of the bench.

Then he drew the door. He had never seen it. He had no drawing of it but what a sister had put into the air over a table with her hands; but he had her arm, and the rule's length of it, and the height of a porch roof, and four fingers of oak; and he drew it, ledged and not braced, two leaves, with the straps across them and the pintles in the jamb, and a bar he would never see, lifted from inside. He drew it in a corner of the board, small, so he would know what he was lifting.

# Chapter Three: A Vow Is a House

**VALERIA**

They were singing it at the Abbess's gate when she came down the canal walk, and they were singing it badly, which was how she knew they meant it.

There were perhaps a score of them, and more coming. Bargewomen with their sleeves rolled to the shoulder. A lamplighter with his pole. Two boys from the tanneries, who stank of it, and a girl with a baby on her hip, and an old soldier with one leg and a crutch under the stump, who held the tune for the rest of them in a voice like a gate on a dry hinge. They stood on the stones of the Lower Canal walk in the steam, with the heat of the afternoon coming down on them off the white walls of the house, and sang up at a row of shuttered windows on the second floor; and the shutters stayed shut, and they sang anyway.

*The Angel of the Baths.* Valeria knew it as the cohort sang it, which was loud and from the barracks benches, with Tappo's voice going up over the top of the others in the last verse but one, high and alone, the verse about the dark. Since Bastion the cohort had left that verse out. Here the old soldier took it, and took it wrong, a beat early and a note flat, and the bargewomen followed him in; and it went up the white wall to the shutters, the whole of it, as if nobody in the city had been told.

She stood at the back with the Farrier on her shoulder until it was done. The heat came up through the soles of her boots. The Lower Canal smoked beside her as it did in every season, but in winter its steam was a kindness and in midsummer it was a second sun, and the sulphur in it sat at the back of the throat like a bad coin. Her mail was hot through the padding. Sweat ran down the groove of her back under the arming coat and into the band of her breeches and stayed there.

When it was done the old soldier wiped his face with his sleeve and saw her, and saw the Farrier, and drew himself up on the crutch as if somebody had called the roll.

"Captain," he said.

She did not know him. That did not mean he did not know her. Half the men who had come home from Hollowmere on the carts had known her face from the causeway, and the other half had been told it.

"Soldier."

"Have they let you in to her?"

"Not yet."

"They'll not." He spat neatly into the canal. "We come every day. She's in there. Third window." He pointed with his chin. "Sister at the gate says she's well. Sister at the gate says it every day, the same. You'd think they'd vary it."

The house of the Vigil of the Returning Sun stood where the Lower Canal bent toward the sea, in a long white wall that ran back from the water with no windows in it lower than a man on a horse could reach. The great door was in its street face, round the corner, two leaves of black oak under a porch with a stone sun over it, and it was a good door; she had taken its measure on her way down the hill the day she landed, as she did any door's, to know how it would be held and what it would cost to take, and had put it down as the kind you went round. Here, on the canal side, the wall had one way in: the poor gate, an arch as deep as a short passage, with an iron grille at its far end and a bench in the arch on either side for the people who waited. A sister in white sat on a stool at the grille. Behind her, through the bars, there was a small paved court with a well in it, and the sun on the stones.

Across the walk from the arch, at the edge of the water, an old man was standing beside a litter.

She had seen him that first day too, and had not known what he was. A small man, bent, white-haired, in a coat of good black wool, old-fashioned and cut long, that had been made for somebody broader in the chest than he was now, or than he had ever been. Four bearers in a grey livery sat on the canal steps below him in the shade of the litter's canopy, with their poles laid down beside them, and talked to each other and threw stones into the steam. The old man did not sit. The litter had a cushioned seat and curtains, and he stood beside it with one hand on its roof, as a man stands by a horse he does not like, and looked at the arch.

Behind him, between the walk and the harbor where the canal came out, there was a strip of grey sand, wet at its lower edge, with a few stakes driven into it at the top and a rope slung between them, and nothing on it.

"Who's that?" she said to the old soldier.

He looked where she looked. "Him?" He lowered his voice, which nobody on the Lower Canal ever did. "That's her father. Lord of the Old Hill, that one. Mela. Comes every afternoon at the third bell and stands there till the sixth and goes away again. Every day. Sends a basket in." He shook his head. "Sister at the gate says she's well."

"To him as well?"

"To him as well." The old soldier settled the crutch under his stump. "Same words. I've heard her."

---

She had come north with the Bastion returns.

That was the duty, and it was a true one. Somebody had to carry the army's papers up the king's stair: the dead by name, and the wounded, and the stores spent and the stores lost, and the plain account of what had been done at the gate and why. The Prefect's clerks could have carried them. But the council had sent her south with a writ in the first place, under orders to report everything, and a report was best carried by the one who had sworn to make it. So she had stood on the long quay at Cyrene on the morning the *Patience* sailed, with the returns in an oilcloth bag under her arm, and the Vigil's new sister had come down the quay on her round and stopped in front of the Lanciarii, and tapped the Farrier's haft twice with one finger, and told her in front of her own files that there was a check in the ash a finger below the socket and it would split in the cold.

Valeria had turned the haft to the light. There had been the check, a hair-line running with the grain, that she had not seen in nineteen years of looking at that spear every morning of her life.

"Thank you, Sister," she had said.

"Get it bound," the sister had said, and gone on to the cookhouse to tell Oppius about his salt.

She had got it bound. That was the first thing she had done in the capital, before the returns, before anything: gone up the Canal Steps to the smith with the green door, who was fat and bald and sweated like a cheese, and stood over him while he whipped the haft with soft iron wire, tight and close, and hammered the whipping down into the ash so that it lay flush and would not catch a hand. He had done it well. He had talked the whole time.

"Ash," he said. "Good ash. Old. Somebody's thumb on it. I don't touch the thumb-place. You'll want the thumb-place." He bit the wire off. "I had a Vigil sister in here last month wanting a hinge-pin made. I said to her, I said, I'll make your pin, Sister, when your house pays my grandfather's grandfather for the bar."

"What bar?"

"Iron bar, for their great door. Made, and never paid for. It's still in the back there, under the sacks, and their door's still got an oak one." He laughed. "I've got the bill. We've all kept the bill. It's on the wall."

It was on the wall. A slip of paper gone as brown as a dead oak leaf, in a glass case on a nail over the forge, with a sum at its foot in figures nobody had used since before the old king.

"They should pay it," said Valeria, and put down a silver bit for the wire, which was too much.

"They should," said the smith, and kept the bit.

Then she had taken the returns up the hill.

The council's clerks had received them in a long hot room off the Hall of Kings, with the shutters closed against the sun, and a clerk with a wet lip had untied the oilcloth and counted the sheets, and written the count in a book, and asked her to sign that she had delivered so many sheets. She had signed. Nobody had read anything aloud. The sheets had gone into a press with a brass lock. The council would sit, said the clerk with the wet lip, when the council sat; and the captain would hold herself at the council's disposal.

She was used to that. She had held herself at the council's disposal through one whole Midwinter. She went down the hill to see about her billet.

On the third morning a steward of the palace had come down to the old horse-barracks under the hill where the Lanciarii were quartered, an old man with a lamp he did not need in daylight, whom she knew by sight from the foot of the king's stair; and he had given her a slip of the king's plain paper, squared at the corners and folded once. There was one line on it in a small, close, old man's hand, and no name, and no seal.

*The captain will ask after the healer, and tell me.*

She had read it twice, as she read orders, for the dead places and the ways out. There were none. It was not an order. It did not say she was to go in. It said the king would know how the woman did who had saved the General's life on the field at Bastion, and that he had chosen a soldier to ask, and not a clerk, and not the Marshal.

She had put it inside the neck of her mail, where the Marshal's letter used to go.

---

The sister at the grille that afternoon was a big raw-boned woman from the hills, with forearms like a ploughman's and a white coif starched so hard it creaked when she turned her head. Her eyes went to the Farrier, and to the slip of paper, and to Valeria's face, taking their time over each.

"The king asks," she said.

"The king asks."

"He can ask." The big sister folded the paper along its own fold and handed it back through the bars. "Leave the spear."

"I'll leave it in the arch."

"You'll leave it with me."

There were three people on the benches in the arch: a man holding a boy with a cut head against his chest, and a girl of about ten with nothing to show what was wrong with her at all, who sat with her hands in her lap and her bare feet not reaching the ground. All three of them watched the Farrier. Valeria passed it through the gap at the end of the grille, haft first, and the big sister took it in one hand like a broom and stood it against the wall behind her stool, and lifted a bar out of two iron keepers on the inside of the grille, and swung the grille back a foot.

"In," she said. "Stand where I put you. Don't touch anything. If she tells you to go, you'll go."

"Who?"

The big sister looked at her as if she had asked which end of a spear went into the ground.

"The healer," she said.

The court beyond the grille was small and hot and smelled of lye, and of the well, which was a warm one, like every well under the Old Hill, and gave off its own small breath of sulphur. Along one side of it ran a roofed walk with a long table under it, and a basin, and a brazier with a kettle on it in spite of the heat, and a shelf of jars. At the table, with her back to the court and her sleeves tied back above the elbow with a strip of linen, a woman in plain white was bending over a bargeman's foot.

Valeria knew her by the hair, before anything. It was under a white cloth, as a sister's was, but the cloth had been tied by someone who had no practice at it and the weight of the hair had pulled it half off, and down her back from under it came a thick rope of honey-gold, plaited for work and tied off with a bit of string.

There were two more sisters under the roofed walk. They stood one at either end of it, in the shade, with their hands folded in front of them. They were not doing anything. They had the look Valeria had seen on men set to guard a wagon of something they had been told was valuable and not told what.

The big sister put her by the well and went back to her stool.

Liora finished the foot. She did it with her hands, both of them laid flat on the instep a moment, and then with a linen strip, wound and tied, which was for the bargeman's peace of mind and not for the foot; and she said something to him in a low voice and he laughed, and got up off the bench and stood on the foot and stamped it twice and laughed again, and went out through the grille without his boot, carrying it. Then she turned round to the basin to wash her hands, and saw Valeria by the well.

The corner of her mouth went in.

"Captain," she said.

"Healer."

"Is it Roderic?"

"He rides." She had not known she would say that first. "He rides every morning. Up the crown's road to the fort and back. He says the horse wants it."

"Which horse?"

"Thistle."

"Thistle wants nothing in this world but apples." Liora dried her hands. She was thinner. Not much. The cloth had slipped, and under it the bones of her face were nearer the surface than they had been on the beach south of Bastion, and there was a new line between her brows that had not been there at all. Her eyes went past Valeria to the grille. "Send the next, Sister Agrippina."

The big sister sent the man with the boy. Liora sat the boy on the bench and took his head between her two hands, and the boy stopped crying in the middle of a breath and stared at her.

"Who sent you?" she said, to Valeria, without looking round.

"The king."

The boy, between her hands, looked up at her face.

"I see," said Liora. "And what does the king want?"

"To know how you do."

"Tell him I do very well. I have a room on the court side with a window that opens." She bent to the cut. "Hold still, sweetheart. This won't be anything." Her eyes closed. The boy's mouth came open. Valeria had seen it done a hundred times now, at Cyrene, in the cutting at Silverwood, at Bastion in the rain with Roderic under her hands; and still she could not see what happened, only that something did, and that the woman's face went inward while it happened, like a door that has someone standing in it. When Liora took her hands away the cut was a red line, and then less. "There. Your father will want to wash that. Tell him to wash it, and to stop looking at me like that, and to give you something to eat."

The man took the boy and went out backward.

"I'll tell him that," said Valeria. "About the window."

"Tell him I eat." Liora turned back to the basin, and the water went pink, and then clear. "That's what they'll all want to know. Gaius will want to know whether the window is square and the rest of them will want to know whether I eat. Write that I'm eating."

"Are you?"

"At the bells." Liora dried her hands again, carefully, finger by finger. "There's a bell for everything in this house. It's very restful. You never have to decide anything." She looked at Valeria, straight, for the first time. "Write that I'm eating, Captain."

"I'll write what I see."

"Yes," said Liora. "You will." And she turned to the grille. "Send the girl, Sister."

The girl with the bare feet came in and stood in front of her. Liora crouched to her height and asked her name, as she always did, as the Vigil had taught her; and Valeria went back out through the grille and took the Farrier from behind the big sister's stool, and Sister Agrippina let her.

Across the walk the old man stood by his litter, and the bearers threw stones into the steam.

---

The next day the poor gate was shut.

There was a sister at the grille all the same, a different one, small and freckled and frightened, and the benches in the arch were empty. The healer, said the small sister, was in reflection. The healer's afternoons at the gate were ended, by the Abbess's order, for the healer's protection, because of the crowds. The sick of the Lower Canal might come in the mornings, as they always had, to the sisters of the infirmary.

"What crowds?" said Valeria.

The small sister looked past her at the walk, where there were nine or ten people on the stones in the heat, and the old soldier on his crutch, getting his breath for the song.

"Those," she said.

"Those are her patients."

"I'm only to say," said the small sister, "that she's well."

Valeria went away and came back the next afternoon, and the one after. The king's slip of paper said *ask*, and she had asked, and been answered; a clerk would have written down the answer and gone home. She was not a clerk. She stood across the walk from it every afternoon with the Farrier grounded beside her, in the steam, and watched the house, as she had watched the gate at Bastion from the brow of the Survey road the day before the fight: the hours, the doors, who went in and who came out and what they carried.

She learned the house. It rang its bells on the hour, a small high clear bell from the court, counting from dawn and again from noon, and the sisters inside moved to it as files move to a drum. The infirmary sisters came out at the first bell in the morning to the canal steps and took in the sick, and went in again at the third. At the sixth bell a cart came down the walk from the Old Hill with bread and greens on it, and a barrel of something that sloshed, and the big sister unloaded it alone. At the dusk bell the great door round the corner shut; she heard it from the walk, a long iron scrape and then the bar going down into its keepers, the deep wooden knock of it carrying along the water; and after that nobody went in and nobody came out until the morning bell. Two sisters stood every day at the third window on the second floor, which was not a room's window but a passage's, by a door, she worked out on the third afternoon: you could see their white through the slats of the shutter, two of them, one either side, as the two had stood in the court. They were relieved at the sixth bell and the dusk bell, like any sentries; and after the dusk bell a lamp showed through the slats, with two shadows in it.

She learned the people who came. The old soldier, every day. The bargewomen, most days. A priest from the harbor shrine, once, who went in at the great door and came out within the hour red in the face. Pilgrims off the ships, in pairs and threes, who had heard the song in some other harbor and come to see the house the Angel was kept in, and stood looking at the white wall as if it might do something, and bought a blessed candle from the boy who had started selling blessed candles on the second day and was doing well by it. Men who had been in the hospital tents after the stair, who knew the healer from there and did not say how. A great many women with children.

And the old man with the litter, at the third bell, every day, till the sixth.

She learned the city again, in the evenings. The Lanciarii had been given the old horse-barracks under the hill to sleep in, a long low stable with the stalls knocked out and a hayloft over, which still smelled faintly of horse and much more strongly of the twelve men who had come north with her; and she ate with them there at night: mutton and onions out of the barracks pot, and on the third night of her watching, eels out of a crock, which Galba had brought up the hill from the Fishmarket Steps with a message.

"From Felix's mother," he said, setting the crock down. "She says is the boy eating, and to tell him his sister's had twins, and they're both of them called Felix, and she wants to know whose fault that is."

"Whose fault is it?"

"Didn't ask, Captain." Galba sat down on an upturned bucket. His beard had grown back black where the gorse had singed it, all but one grey patch on the left side like a burn on a hide. "I thought about it on the way up. I think it's his."

The eels were good. They had been stewed in their own jelly with an onion and a great deal of pepper, and they were better cold. Her men ate them with their fingers, sitting round the crock in their shirts with the barracks door open to the dusk and the smell of the canals coming up the hill, and the bells going all over the city, and somewhere down in the lower town somebody singing *The Angel of the Baths*, every verse.

That night she lay on her cot in the loft with her arms behind her head and went down her list.

It was a short list in the capital. *Things that might kill me*: nothing. There was nothing in this city that could kill her; that was the trouble with it. *Things to deal with later*: the council, which had her returns in a press and had not sat; the Marshal, who had not sent for her, and would. And the third column, the one with no heading, where the elf had been for a year now, and the king beside him. She put the white house on the canal in it, and the two sisters at the end of the passage. And then, after a while, the old man by the litter.

---

On the sixth afternoon the basket came back while she was watching.

It came out through the arch in the small sister's hands at the fifth bell, with a cloth over it knotted at the corners, and the small sister carried it across the walk to the old man and held it out to him. One of the bearers got up off the steps to take it. The old man moved his hand, the smallest movement, and the bearer sat down again.

The old man took the basket himself. He held it in both hands. It was heavy for him; the coat moved on his shoulders as he took the weight, and Valeria saw how thin he was under it.

"The healer is well, my lord," said the small sister. "She is in reflection."

"Thank you, Sister."

"I'm only to say."

"I know you are." His voice was light and dry and very old, and every vowel in it was long, the long vowels of the Old Hill that Liora had kept. "Go in. You'll be in trouble for standing in the sun."

The small sister went in. The old man stood with the basket.

Then he turned, slowly, all of a piece as the old turn, to face her across the walk. He had known she was there. She saw that. He had known she was there every afternoon for six days, and had not once turned his head to her, as he had not once sat down in the litter.

"Captain," he said.

She crossed the walk. The Farrier came with her on her shoulder.

"My lord."

"You are the prince's captain. Valeria. Of the causeway." He said it like a man reading off a list he had made. "You went in. On the first day."

"I did, my lord."

"You saw her."

"I saw her."

He did not ask anything else at once. He stood with the basket in both hands and searched her face, as if the answer might be there, written small, if he could only get close enough to read it; and his eyes were pale and wet with age at the rims.

"Is she thin?" he said.

"A little, my lord."

"She was always thin. As a child. She would not eat what she was given." His hands moved on the basket's handle. "Does she eat?"

"At the bells."

"At the bells." He looked down at the basket. "I know about the bells. I had my cook go to the infirmary sisters and find out what their rule allows. Bread. Greens. A fish. Nothing after the dusk bell. Water from the spring." He lifted the corner of the cloth, and she saw a round loaf, and a bunch of cress, and a small cold fish wrapped in a leaf, all laid out with care, as a servant lays out what she has been told she will be beaten for getting wrong. "I sent exactly that. I thought, if I send exactly what the rule allows, the rule can have nothing to say to it." He let the cloth fall. "It comes back."

"It's their rule, my lord. Nothing from outside. While she's in reflection."

"I know their rule. My house gave them the roof of their chapel." "My great-grandmother. She was healed of something in there, I forget what. It was before I was born. There's a stone over the chapel door with our name on it." He stood a moment. "I have not been inside."

The steam went past them off the canal. Behind him on the strip of grey sand the rope between the stakes moved a little in the heat, and nothing else moved.

"You'll be thinking," said the old man, "that a father might have come to his daughter's door before this summer."

"I wasn't thinking anything, my lord."

"No. You're a soldier." He looked at the arch. "She got down out of my litter on that sand, Captain, one evening a long time ago, and went in at that gate behind a barrow, and I let her. I could have sent men after her. I did not. I thought she would come home when she was cold." His voice did not change at all. "She was never cold enough, it seems."

Valeria said nothing. There was a thing in his face now that she had seen in other faces, in men on carts, in the files coming back down the road from the gorse; and she was not one to put a name to it, and would not have thanked anyone who did.

"I write to her every Midwinter," said the old man. "She answers. She tells me who she has buried." He shifted the basket. "I am told she says their names. All of them. Every night."

"Yes, my lord."

"Is there a man of mine in it?"

She did not understand him, and then she did, or near enough.

"I don't know, my lord," she said. "I've never heard her say a name I knew was yours."

He nodded.

"You'll go in again?"

"If they let me."

"If they let you." He turned the basket a little in his hands. "Then if you see her, Captain, you might tell her the cress was from the garden. The old bed under the chapel wall. She used to pick it there, when she was small enough to get behind the bench." He stopped. "No. Don't tell her that. Tell her nothing from me. She'll know I'm here. She always knew where I was. It was the one thing she could never stop doing."

He gave the basket to the bearer, then, and got into the litter at last, slowly, a hand on either side of the door, and drew the curtain. The bearers took up the poles. The litter went away up the walk toward the Old Hill in the steam, rocking a little, and the singing crowd stood aside for it and did not know whose it was.

Valeria stood on the walk and watched it go, with the Farrier grounded against the wall at her back. The thumb of her right hand went along the side of her forefinger, hunting the worn place, and came back empty.

---

On the ninth day after she came up the hill the council sat, and did not read the returns aloud. It noted their receipt. It thanked the captain. It held the matter of the gate at Bastion over to the next sitting, at the Marshal's request, so that the Marshal might study the returns; and the Marshal, at the head of the long table, did not look at her once from the first bell to the noon one, and pressed the bull's head of his ring into the heel of his hand when the clerk read out the number of sheets.

She went down to the canal afterward with the king's slip of paper in the neck of her mail, and gave it to the small sister at the grille, and asked to see the Knight-Abbess.

She was kept waiting in the arch an hour, on the poor's bench, in the heat. The old soldier on the walk sang *The Angel of the Baths* twice through while she waited, with the high verse, flat. Then the big sister came through the court and lifted the bar out of the grille and said, "She'll see you. Leave the spear."

Valeria left it.

The big sister took her across the court, and through a door, and up a stair that turned twice in the thickness of the wall, and along a passage with the shutters closed and the light coming through their slats in bars across the boards; past a door with two sisters standing one at either side of it, whose eyes came to her and went away; and into a long room at the end.

It was the order's chapter-room, she supposed. It ran the whole width of the house, with its windows open on the court side for the air and shuttered on the canal side for the crowd, and it was whitewashed from the floor to the beams and had nothing on its walls but one great sun painted in gold on the east wall, with its rays going out to the corners, a little flaked. The floor was scrubbed boards. There were benches round three walls. At the far end, under the sun, was a table with a sister at it writing, and in front of the table, in the middle of the floor, on a three-legged stool, Liora was sitting with her hands in her lap.

Cassandra was standing.

She was standing a pace in front of the table, between it and the stool, in a white cloak over mail, clasped at the throat with steel in all that heat, and a steel cap on the white coif, exactly as she had stood a pace in front of her two hundred on the moor road in the spring. She had a ledger open in her two hands. Her spear leaned against the table behind her, a short spear with a little bell at the socket. Her long plain face was red with the heat, and her two dark brows were ruled straight across it.

She looked at Valeria, and closed the ledger on one finger.

"State your competence," she said, "or stand aside."

"Valeria, captain of the Lanciarii. The king's officer." Valeria held out the slip. "His Majesty asks after the healer."

Cassandra did not take the slip. She looked at it in Valeria's hand, and read it there, from a yard off, upside down; and her teeth clicked once.

"His Majesty may ask," she said. "His Majesty has no competence in this house, and knows it, or he would have sent a herald and not a captain. But he may ask." She turned her head a little. "Sister Philippa. Enter that the king's officer sat through this day's hearing at the king's asking, and saw that it was done by the rule."

The sister at the table wrote. She was a lean grey woman with ink on her second finger, and she did not look up.

"Sit," said Cassandra. "There." The end of the bench by the door. "You will not speak. You will not go to her. If you do either, you will be put out, king or no king. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you, Abbess."

Valeria sat. Her hand went to her side for the Farrier, and met the bench. She put both hands on her knees.

There were two other sisters on the bench across the room, old women, one with a face as brown and creased as a walnut and the other with no teeth, sitting upright with their hands folded. Witnesses. A bee had come in at the open window and was going along the whitewash under the beams, looking for the way out again, with a dry small buzz. The room smelled of lye and hot wood, and faintly, under them, of the canal.

Liora had not turned round. She sat on the stool with her back straight and her hands in her lap, in the plain white of the house, the cloth on her hair tied better today; somebody else had tied it. Her feet were bare on the boards. Valeria could see her face only in part, from the side, and it was quiet.

"From the first," said Cassandra.

---

"From the first," Liora said. "On the eighth day at Cyrene, Statius came to the Baths with his back."

Her voice was low and even. It was the voice in which Valeria had heard her tell Hostus how much of a thing to put in a cup.

"He had a bad back," said Liora. "It seized when a load turned him. It had been seizing all that winter. I knew it before he came. I had seen him carry."

"And you treated it," said Cassandra.

"I put my hands on it."

"And the warmth?"

"I kept the warmth from it."

"Say what that means. For the record."

"I laid my hands on him as I would lay them on any man, and did the Seeing, and saw the knot in the long muscle where it went into the hip, and put nothing in." Liora's hands lay still in her lap. "I did it every day for five days. He thought I was healing him. He thanked me."

The pen went across the paper at the table. The bee went along the beam.

"On the thirteenth day," said Cassandra.

"On the thirteenth day we loaded the ships at the water-gate stair. The prisoners carried. He had a cask on his shoulder. The stair turns at the foot, over the deep water, and I was on the landing above. I waited until he was at the turn, with the weight on the outside foot, and I called his name."

"You called his name."

"Statius. He turned to see who it was. His back went. The cask took him off the stair into the deep water, down into the weed, and he did not come up."

"Knowing his back would go."

"Knowing his back would go."

"Knowing the water was deep."

"Yes."

Cassandra opened the ledger on her finger and looked at something in it, and closed it again.

"Pansa," she said.

"On the eleventh day Pansa asked me for my touch. His hands were split with the frost. They were working on the staging at the hulls."

"And you gave it him."

"I gave him goose-grease, and not my hands. There was wolfsbane in the grease."

"How much?"

Liora told her how much. She told it in the old weights of the still-room, the grains and the scruples; and Valeria, who did not know a scruple from a sack of meal, saw the toothless sister on the far bench shut her eyes.

"Enough to take the feeling out of his fingers for the hours of a working day," said Liora. "Not enough to kill him, by itself. I meant it not to kill him by itself."

"And on the fourteenth day."

"On the fourteenth day he went up the ladder to the top of the staging with no feeling in his hands, and missed a rung, and fell."

"Where did the wolfsbane come from?"

Liora was quiet a moment.

"From the still-room off the Baths," she said. "The order's old still-room. The top shelf."

The pen stopped. Then it went on.

"The order's," said Cassandra.

"The order's."

"Go on."

"Atilius," said Liora. "On the fifteenth day. At the pyre on the black beach. We burned our dead and theirs on the same fire. He was across it from me."

"How far?"

"Across the fire. Ten paces. Twelve. The smoke was between us."

"And you did the Seeing."

"Yes."

"At ten paces. Through smoke. On a man you were not touching."

"Yes."

"Who taught you that?"

"Nobody taught me that. I found I could."

"Who taught you the Seeing?"

Liora turned her head then, for the first time, a little, toward the woman standing over her.

"You did," she said.

Cassandra's face did not move. But Valeria saw her hand on the closed ledger whiten across the knuckles, as a hand does on a haft when the first rank of the enemy comes into the last few strides.

"Yes," said Cassandra. "I did. In a tent on the beach below the stair. You got the words wrong for a week." Her voice did not soften, quite. "Go on. Say what you did."

"I went into him," said Liora. "I looked along the cords the heart hangs by. I found one, and put a turn in it, and drew it tight."

"Your hands."

"Cold. I did not warm them."

Nobody on the benches moved. The bee had got out at the open window, or another had; the buzzing had stopped. Outside in the court the little high bell rang the hour, one stroke, clear, and Valeria heard the big sister's boots cross the stones below to the grille.

"You went in cold," said Cassandra.

"I went in cold."

"It is the one thing we ask," said Cassandra, "of every girl we teach. Before the warmth, before the Seeing, before the oil and the bell. It is the first thing. You do not go in cold. You do not go into a living body with your hands unwarmed, to do it harm, because once you have done it you will know how, and you can never again not know." She stood over the stool. "I taught you that too."

"Yes. You taught me that too."

"And then?"

"And then I was sorry," said Liora. "I went back in, with my hands warm, and I eased the pain. I did not take the turn out." She sat with her hands in her lap. "He died before the fire was out."

The pen went across the paper, and stopped.

Cassandra stood. Her teeth were set; Valeria could see the line of the jaw under the coif's edge. She looked down at the woman on the stool, and Valeria saw her draw a breath to ask it; saw the question come up into her face, the one that came next.

Then she did not ask it.

She let the breath go. She opened the ledger again, and turned a leaf.

"You were asked to take the vow," she said. "In the tents below the stair. Again the next summer. And at Midwinter, by me, in this room."

"Yes."

"Three times."

"Three times."

"You refused it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Valeria heard it go across the room like a stone across ice, and the toothless sister on the far bench opened her eyes.

Liora looked up at the Abbess.

"Because a vow is a house too," she said. "And I'd just left one."

For a moment nobody said anything. The heat stood in the room like water in a cistern.

"And now?" said Cassandra.

"Now I'm in yours."

Cassandra's teeth clicked. Once.

"Tomorrow," she said. "From the first."

---

Two sisters came for Liora at the door, the two from the end of the passage, and took her between them, one at either elbow, not holding her, walking a half-step behind; and Liora went out with them without looking at the bench. Valeria waited until the Abbess had gone through the far door with the ledger under her arm and Sister Philippa had sanded her page, and then she got up and went out after them, into the barred light of the passage.

They had stopped at the door with the sisters on it. One of the guards had her hand on the latch. Liora turned round.

"Captain."

The two sisters looked at each other.

"A word," said Liora. "She can stand there. You can stand here. Nobody needs to touch anybody."

The sister with her hand on the latch hesitated, and then took her hand off it and stepped back to the wall, and the other did the same on the other side; and they stood with their hands folded, three paces off, and looked at the shutters.

Valeria came down the passage and stopped where she was put.

"Every day?" she said. "From the first?"

"Every day. Since the day after you came to the gate. Yesterday was the sixth time. Sister Philippa has it word for word, six times over; if anybody ever wants it, they'll be able to see that I never once said it differently." The corner of her mouth went in. "I've always been good at saying things the same way every night."

"She didn't ask you."

"No."

"She was going to."

"She's never going to." Liora looked down the passage toward the long white room. "If she asks, I'll have to answer, or refuse to answer; and either way it'll be about them, and not about me. She knows that. She's not a fool. She was never a fool." She turned back. "This is about me."

"It's a trial."

"It's a reflection." Liora said it in the house's voice, flat, with the house's word in it; and then, in her own: "It's lawful, Valeria. That's what you were let in to see. Every bell of it is in the rule. I walked in at the door. Nobody has laid a hand on me." She drew a breath. "I'm not going to fight my way out of it. I told Marcus. I'll tell you."

"I've not asked you to."

"No. But you were looking at the windows on the way up the stair. You looked at every one."

Valeria had. She had not known she was doing it until she was told.

"Don't do anything," said Liora. "Don't do anything, Valeria. Any of you. If one of those girls on the door gets a split lip because of me, or a broken wrist, or a sister's knocked down a stair, I'll have to heal it, and then I'll have to say it at night." She stood straight between the two white walls. "I've enough to say at night."

Valeria looked at her. Then at the door with the latch, and the two sisters along the wall, and down the passage at the bars of light lying on the boards, and the stair.

"I'm the crown's officer," she said. "There's nothing the crown can do in here."

"Good," said Liora.

She went in. The sister nearest the latch came off the wall and shut the door behind her, and did not bar it, because there was no bar on that side. It was a room. It had a window that opened.

Valeria went down the stair, and across the court, and took the Farrier from behind Sister Agrippina's stool, and the big sister lifted the bar out of the grille and let her out without a word.

The old man was on the walk by his litter. The crowd was singing. The steam came off the canal and went up the white wall and over it into the sky.

She stood there until the sixth bell, as he did. Then she went up the hill to the barracks and sat on her cot in the loft with her mail off and her shirt stuck to her back, and did not eat, and thought.

*There's nothing the crown can do in here.* The crown's officer had sworn to report everything, and she would. She knew what her report would do when it reached Cyrene. The Prefect would read *thinner*, and the engineer would read *a door barred at dusk*, and somebody in that yard would be measuring something before the ink was dry; and whatever came up the Lower Canal after that, at night, the crown's officer could not be standing on the walk when it came, because then she would have to write what she saw.

Below in the barracks her men were at their supper, and Galba was telling the one about the goat in the harbor shrine, and somebody laughed in the wrong place.

What she could do was be somewhere else. It was most of a soldier's life, being where she was put. And a soldier put somewhere else, by an order, could write the order into her report, plain.

The question was who would put her there.

She knew who. She had known it since the bull's head went into the heel of his hand at the council table and the Marshal did not look at her. She lay back on the cot and saw the ground of it as plain as the slope below the gate at Bastion: where he stood, and what he could see from there, and what he would do about what he saw.

*It's what I'd do,* she thought.

In the morning she put her mail on, and her good cloak over it, the blue one, though it was too hot for any cloak; and she combed out her plaits and did them again, tight, with the iron in them showing; and she went down to the Lower Canal at the first bell with the Farrier on her shoulder and four of her men behind her, and stood across the walk from the Abbess's gate where the whole city could see her, all day, in the sun.

---

She did it for seven days.

She did not speak to anybody. She did not go to the grille. She stood across the walk from the poor gate with the Farrier grounded, from the first bell to the dusk bell, with two of the Lanciarii at her back in the morning and two in the afternoon, in mail, in the heat; and she let the city look.

The city looked. The old soldier came on the first morning and stood beside her, and on the second brought a friend with two legs and a drum. The bargewomen brought her water in a jug. The boy with the blessed candles moved his tray along the walk to stand beside her, because the pilgrims came where she was, now, as well as where the house was; and by the fourth day there was a song about her, a short and bad one, about the prince's captain at the Angel's gate, which rhymed *spear* with *here* twice. By the fifth, the lower town had it that she was there by the prince's own command, to stand watch over the Angel till the prince came for her; and by the sixth that the king had sent her; and on the seventh, a woman selling cherries told her, in a whisper, as a kindness, that she was the Angel's sister.

She stood. The old man stood across from her by his litter, from the third bell to the sixth. They did not speak again. On the third day, when the basket came back, he looked at her across the walk with it in his hands, and she inclined her head to him, an inch, and he inclined his.

At the dusk bell on the seventh day a boy in the Marshal's livery came to her on the walk and gave her a folded note, and stood waiting.

*The Lord Marshal will sup with the captain tomorrow, at the first lamp.*

"Tell him yes," said Valeria.

---

The Marshal's house was the same tall grey narrow house under the palace wall where the lane turned, but it was summer, and the door stood open to the lane for the air. There was no pine bough over it. The stone bull's head over the chimney-piece had no garland on its horns, and the fire under it was out, swept, the hearth laid with a bunch of green bay in a crock. All the windows were open. From the garden of the tombs across the lane came the dry, warm, dusty smell of box hedges and the sound of somebody's sheep, which the king let graze among the tombs in summer to keep the grass down.

Ursus was at the door. His red beard was spread on his chest like a doormat, and he had his sleeves rolled up for the heat. His eyes went over the Farrier, and the new wire on it, and her.

"Bound," he said.

"Bound."

"Good wire." He stood aside.

On the peg inside the door hung a white cloak of good wool, with a steel clasp.

She knew it now. She had sat on a bench nine yards from it for the better part of an afternoon. It hung where it had hung at Midwinter, on the same peg, the clasp turned out; and somewhere above, on the stair or in a room over the hall, she heard a small high bell ring once, short, the bell at the socket of a spear as it is set down against a wall. Then a door shut, and there was no other sound.

The Marshal was at his table by the open window. It had been carried over from the hearth for the air, and the light of the long midsummer evening lay across it, yellow and slanting, so that the Marshal's ribbons in their rows were all lit down one side. He was in his shirt, which she had never seen. He was broader without the coat, and older, and the grey cropped hair on his head was wet at the temples.

"Captain," he said. "Sit. Eat."

There was a cold ham on the table, a whole one, with the fat on and a crust of browned breadcrumbs over the fat, and a dish of the little green pickled things that grew on the salt-marshes, and a round loaf, and a bowl of plums with the bloom still on them, and the jug, and the cups. And a paper.

The paper lay at the Marshal's left hand, folded once, and then once again, long and narrow, to go into a sleeve. The folds were as sharp as the edge of a blade. She did not think she had ever seen a paper folded so; it would have stood up on its edge by itself. As she sat down the Marshal put his hand flat on it, without looking, as a man puts his hand on his purse in a crowd.

She had seen the writing before he covered it, a line or two of it on the outside fold: the most beautiful hand she had ever seen on a paper. Not a clerk's. It was too good for a clerk, every letter as clean as a cut, and the long strokes going down below the line and turning up at their feet like the curl at the end of a fern; and it was too careful for a lord. She did not know it. She read *the order's rule* upside down, and *the Lower Canal*, and then the Marshal's hand was on it, and she had no more.

"You've been on the Lower Canal," said the Marshal.

"I have, my lord."

"Seven days."

"Since the morning after I sat in their chapter-room. By the king's leave." She let the word lie. "Asking after the healer."

"You've not asked anything for seven days. You've stood there." He cut the ham himself, two thick slices with the fat on, and put them on her plate. "In mail. With that. Under the house's windows, where the whole of the lower town can see the prince's captain at the Abbess's gate. They're singing about you."

"Badly, my lord."

"I've heard it." He poured the wine. "From my own vines."

"On Vessa," said Valeria.

He looked at her over the jug, and for a moment she thought he would smile, as he nearly had at Midwinter. He did not. He set the jug down and sat back in his chair with his hands on its arms, and looked out of the window at the palace wall going up gold in the last of the sun.

"I've read your returns," he said.

"Did you read them aloud, my lord?"

"To myself." He pressed the bull's head of his ring into the heel of his hand, and took it away, and watched the little white bull come up there and fill in red. "He wouldn't buy the gate."

"No, my lord."

"Two hundred, against the war. On a quarry's books." He was quiet a moment. "Would you have gone through?"

"Yes, my lord," said Valeria. "That morning."

"And now?"

"Now I've written it down."

The Marshal turned his head and looked at her, a long look, and she ate her ham. It was good ham. It had been boiled with hay and bay leaves before it was baked, and the fat was sweet, and the green salt-marsh pickles with it were sharp enough to make her eyes water, and she ate it all.

"The Abbess," said the Marshal at last, "is within her rule."

"She is, my lord."

"You'll have seen that she is. The order's claim on its training and its hands is older than this council and older than the dome on that hill. The crown does not go inside a holy house. The crown never has. Not the old king, and not his father, and not this one." He picked up a plum and turned it in his thick fingers. "His Majesty knows it. His Majesty sent a captain to ask, and the captain asked, and was answered. That's all the crown's business there is."

"Yes, my lord."

"And a woman who killed three men in cold blood," said the Marshal, to the plum, "under no vow, and no order, and no law but her own, is a matter for somebody." He put the plum back in the bowl. "If it isn't the order's, it's somebody's."

Valeria put down her knife.

"How do you know there were three, my lord?"

He looked up.

"I can read, Captain," he said. "It's in the songs. Half the canals have it. *Weed, frost, heart.*" He moved his hand on the folded paper. "Everybody knows."

She had stood seven days on the Lower Canal and heard every song the lower town had, the good and the bad and the one about her. She knew where those three words were. They were in the cohort's mouth, on the slip at Cyrene and round the fires; they had been in a ship's hold on the way to the elves' wood, and in the pace the men kept off the healer, last autumn.

She put the thought where she put such things, in the column with no heading, and picked up her knife again.

"I couldn't say, my lord. I don't sing."

"No." The Marshal almost smiled. "No, you don't."

He took his hand off the paper, then, and picked it up, and put it inside his shirt, folded as it was, against his chest. She watched it go. Then he leaned forward with his forearms on the table among the plums, as he had at Midwinter among the beef.

"I want you off the Lower Canal," he said.

"My lord."

"I don't want the prince's captain under the Abbess's windows with a lancea and a song about her. It looks like something. It looks like the crown standing at a holy house's door, and the crown isn't, and I'll not have the city thinking it is. Next it'll be a crowd at the great door at night, with torches, and the prince's captain in the middle of it, whether she likes it or no." He tapped the table with one thick finger. "The guard on the palace wall wants relieving. The Ninth's men who keep it nights are going home to Vessa at the new moon for the hay. Your company can have it."

"The palace wall, my lord."

"The Old Hill. The wall from the harbor tower to the tombs, and the lane, and the garden. All night, from the dusk bell to the morning one. From the new moon until I say." He sat back. "Where I can see you from my window."

"By whose order, my lord?"

"Mine."

"I'll want it written, my lord."

The Marshal looked at her. Then he laughed, short, through his nose; the first time she had ever heard him do it.

"You'll want it written," he said. "You'll want it in a report."

"I write everything, my lord."

"So you do. So you always did." He reached behind him to a shelf in the window for a sheet of his own paper, and a pen, and an inkhorn, and wrote it himself, there at the table among the plums and the ham, in a big square soldier's hand: four lines. He did not sand it. He folded it once, roughly, and dripped red wax on the fold from the candle a servant brought, and took the ring off his finger and pressed the bull's head down into the wax, hard; and gave it her across the table.

She put it in the neck of her mail, beside the king's slip.

Then she picked up the cup of wine from his own vines on Vessa, that she had held level and put down full at Midwinter, and drank it.

The Marshal watched her do it. She set the cup down empty.

"It's good wine, my lord," she said.

"It's the south face," said the Marshal. "Above the camp." He looked away out of the window at the wall. "Go on, Captain. Go and tell your men they've got the hill."

---

She wrote the report that night in the loft over the horse-barracks, by a lamp, with the heat still lying in the roof-beams overhead.

She wrote it as she had written every report since the first summer of the war: plain, on one side of one sheet, in her square hand, with nothing on it that she would not have stood behind at the council table while Titus, the Crown Prince, read it aloud and asked which word was untrue. She had been writing it in her head since the day of the king's slip, on the walk, at the gate, on the bench in the long white room with her hands on her knees; she knew what went in, and what did not, and in what order. The folded paper did not go in. She had read four words of it upside down, and she did not report what she had not read. When she had done, she read it through once, for the dead places and the ways out, and found none. Then she sanded it, and folded it, and sealed it with her plain leaf-shaped spear-head in the wax, and laid it by the lamp to wait for the *Patience*.

*To the Prefect of Cyrene, at Cyrene. From the captain of the Lanciarii, at the capital.*

*The returns are with the council. It has noted them. Nobody has read them aloud.*

*The healer is in the order's house on the Lower Canal. She is well. She is thinner. She eats at the bells. She has a room on the court side, with a window that opens. At the dusk bell the house shuts its door, as it always has, and two sisters stand at her door day and night, for her protection. There are no chains. It is the order's rule.*

*She worked at the poor gate in the afternoons until I first went in. The next day the gate afternoons were stopped.*

*She is questioned every day by the Knight-Abbess, in the chapter-room, before two witnesses, with a secretary writing. I sat through one day of it, at His Majesty's asking. She is made to give the details of how the three died, from the first, every day. She gives them. She is not asked why, and she does not say.*

*Her father comes to the poor gate every afternoon and stands there three hours. He is refused. He sends food in, what the rule allows. It comes back.*

*The Knight-Abbess's cloak hangs in the Lord Marshal's house.*

*The Lord Marshal knows there were three. He says it is in the songs. It is not in the songs.*

*The healer asked me not to do anything.*

*From the night of the new moon I am on duty on the Old Hill, all night, with the Lanciarii, by the Lord Marshal's own order.*

---

# Chapter Four: The Third Volume

**GAIUS**

The capital rose out of the sea on the afternoon of the third day, a smudge first, then the Old Hill, then the steam.

Gaius saw it from under the stern deck of the *Patience*, where he had sat most of the passage with his back against the rudder-trunk and the jack across his knees. It was in pieces, rolled in sailcloth and lashed with marline, and the roll was the length of a man and heavier than one, and he had not let anybody else carry it on or off anything since Cyrene. Twice a day he had unlashed it and laid the pieces out on the planking and gone over them: the screw, the nut, the frame in its four parts, the shoe, the bed, the two bars. He had greased the thread with tallow from the cook's tub until it shone, and wiped it, and greased it again. A screw that is going to be turned under a roof of shields in the dark wants to be turned slow and without a sound, and a thread with grit in it will sing.

He lashed the roll up again and went forward, past the rowers.

The *Patience* was short of oars. Avitus, her master, had said so at the head of her plank at Cyrene, to nobody in particular, with a cloth to his nose, while two dozen men of the penal cohort came down the long quay in their working clothes with their shields wrapped in sailcloth like timber; and he had said nothing else to any of them in three days. He had a cold that had lasted since the spring. He spent the passage at his helmsman's elbow with a cup of something hot, and when he had to speak to one of the new rowers he spoke to the oar. They were pulling well. Some of them had pulled oars in chains, and the *Patience*'s own free rowers had stopped laughing at them by the first noon.

Gaius stood by the mast and looked at the city.

He had seen it twice before, once as a young man with a bridge in his head and once from a prisoner's bench, and neither time as he saw it now: a building somebody else had put up, that he had to take down without breaking. The Old Hill, terraced with tombs. The long palace wall along its shoulder, and the dome of the Hall of Kings, and one tall window. The canals coming down from the springs under the hill to the sea, each one with a long grey breath standing on it in the heat, so that the city lay ruled in smoke from the hill to the harbor; and where the lowest of them bent toward the sea, before it came out between its stone banks, a long white wall.

He could not see the door from here. The door was on the street face, round the corner, under a porch with a stone sun over it. He knew it better than any door in the world, and he had never seen it.

"That's her," said Plancus, at the stroke oar below him. He did not look round; he had his eyes on the man in front of him, as a stroke oar should. He was a thick, slow, brown man who had poled barges on the Lower Canal for twenty years before he poled the wrong one, and he could smell the tide turn from a cellar. "The white one. Where she bends." He pulled. "Flood's making. High water about the dusk bell, and then it ebbs all night. You'll have the last of it before light." He pulled again. "Good night for it."

"How do you know it's tonight?"

"No moon," said Plancus. He pulled. "And it'll rain."

Gaius put his head back. It was a hard, hot, white sky, with the sun in it like a coin in milk, and it had not rained in a month.

"It'll rain," said Plancus.

---

It had begun four days before, at Cyrene, on the forty-third day after the fleet came home, with Marcus coming down the yard in the evening with a sheet of paper in his hand.

Gaius had been on the staging of the second hull, fairing a frame. He had seen the *Patience* come in on the morning flood, and had not gone to see if there was anything for him, because for a fortnight there had not been. He had seen the Prefect go down to the quay himself, later, and come back up it slowly, reading.

At dusk Marcus went into the shed, past the steam-box, to the bench at the far end where Gaius's lamp was; and Gaius finished the frame, because a frame half faired is worse than one not started, and came down the ladder, bad leg first, and went in after him.

Marcus had lit the lamp. He was standing by the bench with the paper in his hand and his eyes on the drawing pinned over it: a jack, and in the corner of the board, small, a door.

"Valeria," said Marcus, and held out the paper.

It was one sheet, written on one side, in a hand like a row of fence-posts. Gaius read it standing, by the lamp. He read it twice. The second time he read it slower, and at the end he went back to one line in the middle and read that a third time, and then he put the sheet down on the bench, flat, and laid his rule across it so it would not curl.

*She is thinner. She eats at the bells.*

"The Marshal knows things about the healer that aren't in the songs," said Marcus. That's the line I keep reading." He had his hands behind his back. "That, and the last one."

Gaius looked at the last one.

*From the night of the new moon I am on duty on the Old Hill, all night, with the Lanciarii, by the Lord Marshal's own order.*

"When's the new moon?" he said.

"Plancus says four days. The Navarch says four days and a half." Marcus did not look away from the drawing. "Avitus sails on the morning ebb."

"I know."

"I can't send anyone."

"I didn't ask to be sent."

"No." Marcus was quiet. The lamp burned. Out in the yard somebody was singing, badly, on the sea-wall, and stopped. "I'm the heir," said Marcus, like a man reading it off a list. "The heir doesn't storm a holy house. Not this one. Not with the city watching it every afternoon, and the Marshal watching me." He turned his head and looked at Gaius for the first time. The old scar along his jaw had gone tight. "If I sent a single man of this cohort up that canal, it would be the crown at the Vigil's door, and they would have her for the rest of her life, and they would be right to."

"I know."

"So I'm not sending anyone."

"No."

Marcus looked back at the drawing.

"Avitus was in this afternoon," he said. "He's got a cold."

"He's had it since spring."

"He says he's short of oars." Marcus unclasped his hands from behind his back and put one of them flat on the bench, beside Valeria's report, beside the rule; and took it away again. "I told him I'd see what the yard could spare. Rowers. For the passage." He went to the shed door, and stopped there, in the doorway, with the dark yard behind him and the harbor black past the slip. "Send me word," he said. "Whatever it is. When it's done."

"There'll be nothing to send."

"Then send me that." And he went.

Gaius stood by the bench. Then he took the drawing of the jack off the board, and rolled it, and put it under his arm; and went out to the forge at the end of the sea-wall, where the smith the *Patience* had brought down to Cyrene kept his fire in, and woke him.

---

He did not ask anybody.

He had meant to. He had lain awake on the shavings by the keel, with the bronze for the nut cooling in the smith's trough, and tried to put the words in an order that was not an order, and could not. He would rather go up the Lower Canal alone than with a man he had asked.

When he came down the yard in the grey before dawn with the roll on his shoulder, Rufo was sitting on the bollard at the head of the slip with his shield across his knees, wrapped in sailcloth, and his hazel tally-stick in his hand.

He looked at Gaius, and at the roll, and got up.

"You're late," said Rufo.

They were all along the slip behind him, in the half-dark. Gaius saw Ear, with his crumpled ear and his new scar on his shin and his shield on his back; and Sennius, behind Ear, very straight, with his hands empty. He saw Rusticus and Salvius, who had been caulking the old liburna for a week and arguing about it the whole time, not arguing. He saw Fingers sitting on an upturned bucket with a leather roll of his own on his knees, and the cousins Naevius and Lollius, who never spoke, standing side by side as they always stood; and Vatinius, and Plancus, and Strabo, and Scaeva with his shield on the wrong arm; and behind them sawyers from the slip-yard, and two of the carpenters who had built her house, and men of the cutting at Silverwood whose names he did not know, every one of whom he had seen at one time or another on a pallet at the Baths with her hands on him. Every one of them had a shield in sailcloth.

Rufo turned the tally-stick over in his fingers. He had been notching it, Gaius saw; the new cuts were white in the hazel. He did not say the number. He put the stick away inside his shirt, and his hand went up, for a moment, to the place over his ear where the fair lock was plaited.

"We heard the *Patience* was short of oars," said Rufo.

Gaius looked along the slip at them, in the grey, and could think of nothing to say about it; so he said what he would have said on any morning about any job.

"Swords stay at home."

There was a little stir along the slip. Not much.

"The job's a door," said Gaius. "It comes off. Nobody goes through anybody. If a man in there draws steel, or a man of ours does, she won't come out, and she'll be right not to, and we may as well have stayed on the slip." He shifted the roll on his shoulder. "Shields. Nothing else."

Rufo looked at him for a moment. Then he unbuckled the belt from his waist with the short sword on it and hung it on the bollard he had been sitting on, and stood back.

One after another, without hurry, they walked up the slip and hung their belts on the bollard and the next one and the stanchions of the staging, until the head of the slip looked like a sword-cutler's on a market morning. Ear had to unwind his twice before he got it off.

The last one was Sennius, who had never carried a sword in Aeridor's service at all. He had a knife in his belt, a short plain one, for bread. He looked at it. Then he took it out and laid it on the bollard on top of Rufo's belt and took his hand off it quick, as he did, as if it had come out of a fire.

"Bread's on the ship," said Rufo. "Go on."

They went down to the long quay as it got light. There were net-wives on it already, and bakehouse girls, and old men with nothing to do but see who sailed, and they saw the cohort go down to the *Patience* with shields and no swords, and Gaius with the long roll after them; and nobody called out, and a net-wife at the foot of Netmakers' Row put her hand flat on the shoulder of the woman beside her and kept it there.

Sister Domitia was on the quay.

She was at the head of the *Patience*'s plank, in her white and her steel cap, with her spear in the crook of her arm and the Vigil's mail-bag at her feet, and her letter to the Abbess in her hand. Gaius stopped in front of her because she was in the way. She ran her eyes along the roll on his shoulder, all the length of it, as she had run them along his trench and Ear's dressing and the Farrier's haft.

Her finger came up and tapped the roll, twice, near the end, where the screw was.

"Keep that out of the wet," she said. "If the thread rusts it'll sing."

"It's greased."

"Grease it again." She put her finger down. She held up the letter, sealed, in her big plain hand. "I write to the Abbess every night," she said. "This is last night's. I'll write again tonight, as I always do. It'll go on the next *Patience*." She put it in the mail-bag at her feet, which would sail when the *Patience* came back, and looked at him levelly with her grey eyes; and he understood that she was telling him the days. "Mind the knee on that plank, Master Gaius."

He went up the plank.

When he had stowed the roll under the stern deck and come up again the *Patience* was off the quay on the ebb, with the cohort at her oars and Avitus sneezing at the helm; and Domitia was still standing at the head of the empty quay, in the white, with the Abbess's letter in the bag at her feet, unsent.

---

The *Patience* rowed into the royal harbor at the end of the afternoon, between the moles, with the bolt-throwers on the mole-towers asleep under their sailcloth hoods and the heat coming off the water like the breath of a kiln.

She moored at the galley quay beyond the empty berths, where she always moored. The port-reeve's man walked down with his slate and looked at the mail-bags and at Avitus's nose, and went away. Nobody looks at the rowers of a mail galley. They went ashore two and three at a time, with their sea-bags over their shoulders and the long sailcloth rolls under their arms.

Gaius went last, with Fingers and the jack.

The city stank in summer. The steam off the canals hung in the streets and would not lift, a yellow fog that smelled of eggs and drains and fish-oil and the tanneries upriver, and under it the lower town sat in its doorways in its shirts and watched the roll go by on his shoulder without wondering about it. Everything had been carried past those doors in a sack at some time or other.

Fingers knew the way. He had been born off the Gaol Stair, and he went through the lower town like a man going home, by back lanes between the warm walls and once through the yard of a dye-house, where a woman with arms dyed blue to the elbow said, "*Fingers*," in a voice of great surprise, and he said, "Not tonight, Ma," and went on.

The Drowned Lantern stood on the left bank of the Lower Canal, a little way up from where the canal came out under the hill into the harbor. It had a badly painted sign of a lantern sunk in green water, and a canal window with no glass in it and a sack nailed over the lower half.

"That's where he went in," said Fingers. "The prince. Out of that window, the morning they buried his brother. The General tipped him out of his chair and he went straight through it into the canal." He considered the window with respect. "They never put the glass back."

"Why not?"

"Nobody's asked them to." Fingers went round the side of the house, down a passage no wider than his shoulders, into a yard behind it.

The cohort was there, sitting about the yard on the cobbles and the empty casks and the wall of the midden, as rowers sit in a tavern yard on a hot night; and the landlord, a heavy man with a wet rag over one shoulder, was going round them with a jug of small beer. He took in Gaius and the long roll, and went on pouring.

"He's been paid," said Rufo, from the casks.

"Who by?"

Rufo shrugged. "He says a man looked in an hour since with a purse and said the yard would want it for the night, and some of the men might be tired after, and to give them beer. Grey man, he says. A cold."

Gaius sat down on a cask.

The jack went down on the cobbles in front of him, and he did not unlash it. The light was going yellow. Over the wall of the yard he could see the top of the Old Hill going up gold in the last of the sun; and from somewhere up the canal to his right, beyond the roofs, under the noise of the lower town, a little high bell began to ring.

He counted it, as he had counted bells on scaffolds and slips all his life, because a bell is a measure of time and time is a thing a builder spends like oak; and lost the count. Then it stopped, and there was a pause, and then another sound carried down the water, not a bell: one heavy wooden knock, flat and loud, like a coffin-lid set down on its trestles.

It rolled along the canal and over the roofs and into the yard, and every man in the yard heard it and lifted his head.

*I had forgotten how loud the bar is.*

On the midden wall, Ear put down his beer.

---

The rain broke an hour after dark.

It came as rain comes in the capital at the end of a dry month, all at once, like a cask turned over on the city: big warm drops first, hissing on the hot cobbles and the steaming water, and then a roar on every roof in the lower town together, and the smell of it, wet dust and wet stone and the canal steaming twice as hard, so that the yard behind the Drowned Lantern stood in a cloud. Plancus, under the eaves, put his face up into it with his eyes shut and said nothing at all.

Gaius had his own face turned the other way, to the hill. He had been watching it since the light went. At the dusk bell lights had come out along the shoulder of it, where the palace wall ran from the harbor tower to the trees of the tombs: small lanterns, a dozen, more, walking the top of the wall one behind another; and below the wall, at the turn of the lane, one window lit, square and yellow. They had gone to the end of the wall and turned and walked back, at a soldier's walk, and every quarter of an hour, by the bell on the Hall of Kings, they had come to the place under the lit window and stopped there, all together, a little longer than anywhere else. Now the rain had smeared them to a row of yellow blurs. He could still see them stop.

"Is that her?" said Ear, beside him, low.

"That's the Lanciarii."

"Is she with them?"

"She's on duty," said Gaius. "All night." He watched the blurs stop under the window. "That'll be his window."

Ear thought about that.

"That's clever," he said.

"Yes."

"Is it hers or his?"

Gaius had been wondering that since Cyrene. He did not think it made any difference.

---

They waited for the middle of the night.

It was a long wet wait, under the eaves and in the landlord's lean-to among his empty casks. Some of the men slept sitting up, with their chins on their chests. Rusticus and Salvius argued in whispers about the old liburna's garboard, and Salvius was wrong, and Gaius let him be. Vatinius sat with his eyes shut and his feet keeping time to a tune nobody else could hear. The cousins sat side by side and watched the rain.

Gaius unlashed the roll at last, in the lean-to, by the light of one tallow dip that Fingers had begged off the landlord, and went over the pieces again. He greased the thread, because Domitia had told him to.

Fingers squatted down on the other side of the sack and watched him.

"How long?" said Fingers.

"To set it? Forty breaths. To lift?" Gaius turned the screw a quarter turn in the nut, and back, and listened to it. "Depends on the oak. A hundred breaths. More, if the pintles are dry."

"They'll not be dry. She oiled them."

"Twenty years ago."

"Somebody's oiled them since. There's always a novice with a feather." Fingers picked up the shoe and turned it over in his long thin hands and looked at the lip on it, which would take the bottom rail of the leaf; and the tip of his tongue came out between his teeth. "You'll want to look at the tops of the pintles first," he said. "Before you lift."

"Why?"

"Some smiths cap them. A pin through the top, above the eye. Or a nut. So nobody can do what you're going to do." He put the shoe down. "If there's a cap, I'll have it off. Without a mark. If there's not—"

"There's not. She'd have said."

"She'd not have thought of it. Sisters don't." Fingers wiped his hands on his breeches. "I'll look."

"Good."

Fingers nodded, and went back to his bucket under the eaves.

Ear sat down where Fingers had been, and watched the screw go into the nut and come out again.

He was nineteen, a long, loose, freckled lad from the lower town. A turnkey at the harbor prison had held his head to a brazier once, for a joke, and the ear had gone bad at sea; she had warmed her hands at a lamp on the wreck on the black beach and laid them on it.

"What's she like?" said Ear. "Inside, I mean. The house."

"I've never been in."

"But you know it."

"I know the door." Gaius wiped the thread. "And the stair. "And where her room is, off the passage. Domitia told me." He did not say what Valeria had written. "That's all."

Ear looked at the screw.

"She's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen," he said. He said it to the screw, plainly, as Plancus would have said the tide was on the turn. "I don't mean anything by it. I just think it, every time. When she did my ear I couldn't look at her. I looked at the floor." He scratched the scar on his shin, which Domitia had made Hostus sew, and which itched. "I'm not going to say anything to her. Tonight."

"No."

"I just wanted to say it to somebody." He got up. "Before."

He went back out to the midden wall in the rain.

Gaius greased the thread again, and wiped it; and his hands stopped, and he started them again.

---

The rain eased a little toward the middle of the night, from a roar to a steady hiss, and the Hall of Kings rang the hour; and Gaius lashed the jack up in two parts, the frame and bed in one roll and the screw and bars in the other, and stood up.

Rufo was on his feet before he was.

They unwrapped the shields in the lean-to, by the tallow dip. They were the cohort's own, plain oak faced with hide and rimmed in iron, with nothing painted on them; and every rim had been gone over with a file, Gaius saw, so that no burr would catch on anything.

Rufo formed them up in the yard.

He did it without a word above a murmur, in the rain, by touch as much as by sight; a hand on a man's shoulder, a push, a tug at a strap. Three ranks across the narrow yard, and the two files of the sides, and the middle men with their shields ready for the roof. Gaius stood at the back with Fingers and the two rolls and watched it grow, as he had watched a wall grow, course by course, and could not fault a stone of it.

The front rank formed last. Rufo took the right of it himself, as he always had. Ear went in on his left. Then there was a space, a man's width, where nobody stood. Then Sennius, at the far left, square, with his hands steady on the shield.

Rufo looked at the space for a moment. Then he looked at Ear, and Ear moved his shield a little out from his body, to the left, to cover it; and on the other side of the space Sennius moved his a little to the right; and the two shield-rims came together across the gap and met, and held it. There was nobody behind them in the gap. The men in the second rank had closed up on either side of it and left it, without being told.

Gaius looked at it, and then at the back of Rufo's neck, and then away.

"Engineer," said Rufo, without turning his head. "Where do you want to be?"

"Third rank. In the middle. With Fingers." He hefted the screw roll. "When we're at the door I'll want the front rank to open, and the roof down low, and two men to turn."

"You'll have them."

"And when we're inside—"

"I know what to do when we're inside," said Rufo. "I've been inside a lot of places." He settled his shoulder into his shield. "*Ready.*"

The landlord stood in his back door with his wet rag over his shoulder and watched them file out of his yard by the passage, by ones, to form again on the walk. When the last of them had gone by he went in and shut the door; and Gaius, at the back, heard the bar go down on it, softly, from inside.

---

They went up the Lower Canal walk in the rain.

The rain had cleared the lower town off its doorsteps, and the walk at the middle of the night was a long wet causeway between the steaming water and the shut houses, with nothing on it but puddles and the smell of eggs. The water hissed. Under the hiss Gaius could hear the tortoise: the soft shuffle of men walking in step on wet stone, and their breathing, and now and then the faint dry knock of one oak rim against another; and nothing else. No iron on any of them. Rufo called the step under his breath, so low that only the front rank could have heard him, and the rest took it off the front rank through their feet.

Gaius walked in the middle of the third rank with the screw roll on his shoulder and Fingers beside him with the frame, and could see nothing but the backs of the men in front and the underside of the roof. The rain drummed on the roof. The air under it was hot and close and smelled of wet hide and oil and men.

He did not need to see. He had walked it on the board in the shed until he could have walked it asleep. Three hundred paces from the Drowned Lantern to the bend; then the poor gate on the right, set back under its arch; then the champions' sand on the left, across the walk, with its stakes; then the end of the white wall, and the corner, and the street face, and the porch.

He counted his steps. At something over three hundred, a voice said, from the front rank, low, "*Litter*."

The tortoise did not stop. It went on, at the same pace, and Gaius put his eye to the crack between two shields on his left and looked out.

It was on the left of the walk, by the rope of the champions' sand: a litter, a long box with curtains on poles, set down on the stones, with four bearers in grey sitting under its eaves on their heels with sacks over their heads against the rain. A lantern hung from one of its poles, hooded, so that only a little yellow leaked from the bottom of it onto the wet stone. The curtains were drawn.

One of the bearers lifted his head and looked at them going by.

He did not get up or call out. He put his head back down on his knees.

Gaius took his eye from the crack.

He thought he knew whose it was. He had heard the story only in pieces, and never from her: a champion's sand, a fishwife's son, a father who had made his daughter watch, and a girl who had got down out of a litter and walked away behind a pauper's barrow. He thought about it as far as the end of the white wall; and then they reached the corner, and there was the door.

---

The street face was narrower than he had drawn it.

It was a lane, not a street, running up from the canal between the white wall of the house on the left and the blank backs of warehouses on the right, cobbled, with a gutter down the middle that was running like a mill-race in the rain. The porch was halfway up it on the left: a deep stone porch with two pillars and a roof of stone slabs, and over the arch of it, cut in the stone and gone soft and green with four hundred years of the canal's breath, a sun with its rays out round it like the spokes of a wheel. Under the porch roof, on an iron bracket, a lamp burned behind horn. The house kept it lit all night for anybody sick enough to knock. It made a little round room of yellow light in the porch, and the rain fell across the mouth of it in a bright curtain, and inside the little room, dry, were the doors.

Gaius saw them over the shoulders of the front rank as the tortoise came up the lane, and for a moment he forgot what he was there for.

They were right. Two leaves of oak, black with age and the steam, each the width of a heavy woman's forearm twice and a bit, and the height of the porch roof; four fingers thick at the edge, where the leaves met, as he could see by the shadow down the meeting-stile; ledged and not braced. Three iron straps across each leaf, clenched over on the face, their nail-heads standing in rows. And at the outer edge of each leaf, where it met the stone of the jamb, the eyes of the straps, round as rings, dropped over three iron pintles that stood out of the stone; leaded in, the lead gone white and crusted with age. He had drawn it from a sister's hands moving in the air over a table, and he had not got one thing wrong.

"Engineer," said Rufo.

"Yes." He shook himself. "The right-hand leaf. Front rank, open. Roof down. Fingers."

The tortoise climbed into the porch and filled it. The front rank stepped apart, Rufo to the right and Sennius to the left, and the shields of the second rank came down over their heads, and the roof came down low over the place where the right-hand leaf met the jamb, so low that the men holding it had to crouch; and under it, in a little cave of oak and hide, with the horn lamp's light coming in yellow through the gaps between the shields, Gaius knelt down on the wet stone of the porch floor in front of the door, on the bad knee, because he had no time to arrange it otherwise. The knee told him about it. He let it.

Fingers was already up on the step at the hinge side, with his face a span from the top pintle, close enough to kiss it; and the tip of his tongue came out between his teeth. He put up a long thin finger and touched the top of the pintle, where it came up through the eye of the strap. Then the next one down. Then the bottom.

"No caps," said Fingers, low. "Nothing. Never had." He took his hand away. "Somebody trusted the bar."

"Oiled?"

Fingers put his finger to his nose.

"Last spring," he said. "Somebody with a feather."

Gaius unrolled the screw.

He set the bed first, a flat slab of oak shod with iron on its underside, laid on the step hard against the foot of the leaf at its hinge end, and stamped it down with the heel of his hand to feel whether it rocked. It did not. He put the frame on it, the four parts, and Fingers dropped the pins in, one, two, three, four, without a sound. He put the screw into the nut in the head of the frame and ran it up by hand until the shoe at the top of it came up under the bottom rail of the leaf; and there he stopped, and felt with his fingers along the underside of the rail, in the dark, to find the place where the shoe would bite on sound oak and not on rot. The oak was as hard as iron, and dry, and cold, after the warm wet stone. He moved the shoe a finger's width toward the jamb, to where the bottom strap came across, so that the lift would go up through the iron and not through the wood. Then he put the bar through the head of the screw.

"Two men," he said.

The cousins came, Naevius and Lollius; they crawled in under the low roof and knelt, one each side of the screw, and took the ends of the bar in their hands, and looked at him.

"Slow," said Gaius. "Slower than that. As if it was asleep and you didn't want to wake it. Together." He put his hand flat on the face of the leaf, low down, to feel it move. "Now."

They turned.

The screw went up into the shoe and stopped. They turned. Nothing. They turned, and he felt, through his palm on the black oak, the whole of the leaf take the weight; a long slow strain going up through it, through the ledges and the straps, like a breath drawn in. The thread made no sound at all. They turned. Somewhere in the leaf a joint ticked, once, as a house ticks at night. They turned.

Fingers, on the step, with his face by the top pintle, said, "Moving."

Gaius took his hand from the leaf.

The eye of the top strap had lifted on its pintle. He could see it in the yellow light that came in between the shields: a thin dark line of oiled iron showing under the eye, where the pintle had been hidden in it for a hundred years, the width of a straw. Then the width of a finger. The cousins turned, and turned, and their breath went in and out together, and they did not look at anything but the bar. The rain drummed on the roof over all their heads.

The leaf came up off its pintles a finger, two, the depth of the pintle and a little more, exactly as Anaxis had said in the third volume; and there, at the top of its lift, it did a thing Anaxis had not drawn, and Gaius had.

The bar came up with it.

He heard it go, inside: a long dry creak of oak in iron. The great oak bar lay across the backs of both leaves in its iron keepers, one on each leaf and one each side in the stone, and it was lifted from the inside, Domitia had said, by two sisters or by one from the hills; and now the leaf was lifting it from the outside. The keeper on the back of the right-hand leaf rose with the leaf, and the end of the bar rose in it, and came up out of the keeper in the stone of the jamb, a finger, two, and hung there, held up at one end by the door it was meant to hold shut.

*What use is a bar that lifts from both sides?*

"Hold," said Gaius.

The cousins held.

"Front rank," he said. "Take the leaf. By the edge. When I say, it'll come out at the hinge side, at the top. Let it come. Walk it out. Don't let it fall." He wiped his face. "Rufo."

"Here."

"When it comes out, the bar comes down. Inside. On the floor." He thought about it, for one breath, the oak bar longer than a man and as thick as his thigh, falling a man's height on to stone in the middle of the night in a house full of sleeping women. He had thought about it for four days. There was no way round it that did not break something. "It'll be loud."

"How loud?"

"She said she'd forgotten."

Rufo looked at him under the roof, and his face did a thing that on another man would have been a grin.

"Then she'll know who it is," said Rufo.

"*Now.*"

The front rank put their hands on the edge of the leaf, Rufo high and Ear low, and pulled, gently, outward; and the great black leaf, free at the hinge, tilted out of the jamb at the top like a tooth coming out of a jaw, slowly, all of a piece, and the eyes of the straps swung clear of the pintles one, two, three. The men took its weight. It was heavier than anything Gaius had ever seen men take the weight of without a crane, and they took it, and grunted, and did not drop it; and as the leaf came out and away at the hinge side, turning on its meeting edge against the other, the end of the bar that it had been holding up, with nothing in the stone to hold it now, slid backward out of the leaf's keeper, and was over nothing.

It fell.

The sound went through the house. One great flat wooden crash, on stone, inside, so loud that the men under the roof flinched as if it had come down on their shields, and then a second, smaller, as it bounced, and then the long deep shuddering roll of it settling; and after it, a silence so complete that Gaius could hear the rain.

Then, somewhere above them inside the house, small and high, a bell began to ring.

"Walk it," said Gaius.

They walked it, the front rank and two from the second, with the leaf held upright between them like a ship's hatch, out of the jamb and round, step by step, the bottom rail grinding a little on the porch floor; and they leaned it against the inside wall of the porch, against the stone, upright, carefully, as a man leans a ladder; and stood back from it.

Nothing was broken. Gaius looked, because he had to. The leaf stood against the porch wall with its straps and its nail-heads and its eyes all whole, and the pintles stood out of the jamb, whole, oiled and shining, with nothing hanging on them.

He stood up off the bad knee. His hands were shaking.

"In," said Rufo. "*Shields.*"

They went in through the one leaf's width by twos, and closed up again in the hall.

---

The hall inside was long and high and dark and smelled of lye and beeswax and old incense and, under all of it, the warm sulphur of the canal coming up through the floor. There was a lamp at the far end of it, by the foot of a stair, burning in a niche, and a little more light came in behind them from the porch; and between the porch and the stair, on the scrubbed stone floor, lay the bar.

It lay across the hall at an angle where it had fallen, a great dark beam of oak, square and smooth, with the black marks of a hundred years of hands at its middle. It was not broken. Gaius, going past it in the middle of the third rank, under the roof again, saw that much through a gap, and saw too, at the far end of it, by the stair, a big woman in a white shift with her hair under a nightcap and her feet bare, standing over it and looking down at it as though it had been a calf that had got into the house.

Sister Agrippina. He had never seen her, and he knew her. She was as tall as Rufo, nearly, raw-boned, with forearms like a ploughman's below the rolled sleeves of the shift; and she lifted her head and saw the tortoise coming up her hall at her, out of the rain, a roof of shields on legs; and she did not scream, or run, or ring anything. She bent and put her two great hands under the end of the bar, to lift it back into its keepers.

Then she seemed to see that there was nowhere to put it, and straightened.

The bell was still ringing, overhead, high and fast. There were other sounds now, from above: doors, feet on boards, women's voices calling one to another, quick and low, in the dark. A lamp went by a window at the top of the stair. Agrippina looked at the tortoise, and at the stair behind her, and Gaius saw her decide. She planted herself in the middle of the hall with her bare feet apart and her hands open at her sides, in front of the stair, between it and them.

The front rank reached her and stopped.

"This is the Vigil's house," said Agrippina, in a voice like a farm gate. "You're in a holy house. *Out.*"

Rufo looked at her over the rim of his shield.

"Sorry, Sister," he said. "*Push.*"

The front rank leaned in.

They did not hit her. They put their shields against her, flat, Rufo's and Ear's and Sennius's, and across the place where Tappo was not, the edges of two; and leaned their weight into them, and walked. Agrippina set her feet and put her hands flat on the shields and pushed back, with her whole great body, and for two breaths the tortoise stopped dead in the middle of the hall, and Gaius heard Ear grunt and Rufo laugh, once. She was strong. Then the second rank put their shoulders to the backs of the first, and the third to the second, and he put his own to the man in front of him, and the whole tortoise leaned and *pushed*; and Sister Agrippina's bare feet went back across her own scrubbed floor, sliding, a foot, a pace, two paces, with her hands still flat on the shields and her face red with fury, all the way back to the foot of the stair, where she sat down on the bottom step quite suddenly, because there was nowhere else for her to go.

The front rank parted round her like water round a rock. Ear, going past, put down a hand to help her up, by habit; and she slapped it away.

"Sorry, Sister," said Ear.

They went up the stair.

---

It was a wide stone stair, worn into hollows at the middle of each tread by four hundred years of sisters' feet, and it went up in two flights with a turn, to the second floor, where the passage was. The tortoise went up it badly, the roof tilting, men stumbling on the hollows, the shields knocking; and at the turn the light came down to meet them.

They were at the top of the second flight, in the passage, a dozen of them. White shifts, white cloaks thrown on over the shifts, or nothing over them; steel caps on some, hair loose on others, and every one of them with a short spear in her hands, levelled, the heads catching the light of the lamps that the sisters behind held up; and at every spear's socket, under the head, a little bronze bell. The bells were all ringing at once, small and dry and furious, as the spears shook. They filled the head of the stair from wall to wall, and they were not going to move.

"*Halt,*" said Rufo, and the tortoise halted on the stair, three steps down.

Nobody moved.

Gaius had seen these women on the moor at Ghoul's End, in a ring round a chapel door, burning the dead as they came and not giving a step. Some of them he knew by sight. They held their spears as women hold spears who have used them, low and steady, with the butt tucked under the arm.

Beyond them, along the passage, he could see a row of doors, a bay where the passage widened, and two sisters with a lamp, standing either side of the third door.

"Let us by, sisters," said Rufo.

Nobody answered.

"We've no steel," said Rufo. "None. Look." He did not lower his shield. "We've come for the healer. That's all. We'll take nothing else and break nothing else and hurt nobody, and if anybody in this house bleeds tonight it'll not be from us." He waited. "Let us by."

A young sister in the front, with a round face and her coif crooked, said in a high shaking voice, "You broke the door."

"We took it off," said Gaius, from the third rank, before he could stop himself. "It's on the porch. Leaning on the wall. It'll go back on in a quarter of an hour, with two men and a bar." He stopped. "I'll put it back myself, if you want. It's a good door."

There was a silence on the stair.

"*Push,*" said Rufo.

The tortoise went up the last three steps into the spears.

They came in at once, as Gaius had known they would; a dozen points at the front rank's shields, all together, with all the bells on them going at once. He heard them hit. A short spear driven by a strong woman into an oak board faced with hide makes a sound like an axe going into a log, and the shields rocked back, and the front rank leaned into them and took them, and the points went into the hide and the oak a finger deep and stuck there, shaking; and the front rank pushed. The sisters pushed back on their spear-shafts. For a breath the tortoise and the spears stood locked at the head of the stair.

Then a spear got in through the gap.

It slid in low, from the left, past the edge of Ear's shield, where the edges of Ear's and Sennius's met over the empty place in the front rank, which had opened a little with the strain. It came in under the roof between the first rank and the second, looking for something, and came to Gaius. He saw it come, a long bright leaf of steel with its little bell ringing on it, a span from his belly, and stop.

Sennius had it.

He had let go of his own shield with one hand and taken the spear by the shaft, behind the head, in his fist, and stopped it there, a span from Gaius; and he held it, and the sister at the other end of it heaved on it, and he held it. Gaius saw his face, close, in the lamplight coming under the roof: white and fixed and very young. Then Sennius's hand opened on the shaft, quick, as if it had come out of a fire, and closed again lower down, and he drew the spear out of the young sister's hands, gently, and turned it, and held it out to her butt first, with the bell ringing at his end.

"Your spear, Sister," said Sennius, in his Lessa voice. "Mind the point."

She took it.

The tortoise pushed.

It went into the head of the stair, and the spears came out of the shields one after another as the sisters gave back, step by step, along the passage, with their bells ringing and their feet sliding on the boards; and the tortoise walked them down their own passage, slowly, like a plough going down a furrow, as far as the bay, where they could go round it, and did.

Nobody bled. Gaius looked. He looked at every one of them as they went by, and at the boards behind them, in the light of the lamps. There was a sister holding her wrist where it had been jarred, and swearing in a way that he would not have thought a sister knew, and one whose cap had come off. There was no blood anywhere.

Then the tortoise stopped, because there was somebody in front of it.

---

The Knight-Abbess was standing in the middle of the passage in her shift.

It was a plain white shift of coarse linen, and over it she had nothing. She had put the steel cap on over her coif. She had come out of a door beyond the bay, that stood open behind her on a dark room, and she stood between it and the tortoise, tall and thin and still, planted as she planted herself in roads. She had no spear. Her hands were empty and at her sides. There was a sister behind her with a lamp, and the light from it came over her shoulder and lit the long plain face, red from sleep, and the two straight dark brows under the rim of the cap, ruled across it like the end of a sentence.

She took in the tortoise: the front rank, Rufo and Ear and the gap and Sennius, the roof, the faces she could see under it; and the shields, with the white wounds of a dozen spear-points in their hide. Then her eyes went past the front rank to the third, and found Gaius, and stopped.

He met them. He did not know what else to do.

"The one who reads walls," said Cassandra.

He did not answer it.

The sisters had stopped too, behind the shields and beside them, with their spears up; and the bells had stopped. The house was quiet. He could hear somebody breathing hard, and far down in the house, at the foot of the stair, Agrippina saying something to somebody in a voice like a farm gate. Cassandra did not look at her sisters. She did not look at anybody but him. He waited for her to say the thing, the thing that a Knight-Abbess says to a man who has taken her door off in the middle of the night, and he waited for it to be something that could be written down and read out in a hall.

Her teeth clicked, once.

"Sisters," said Cassandra.

All along the passage the spear-heads came down, slowly, one after another, until they were pointed at the boards; and the Knight-Abbess turned her back on the tortoise, without hurry, and walked back in her shift and her steel cap, between her sisters, to the open door of her room; and went in, and shut it.

He heard the latch drop. Nothing else. No bar.

Rufo breathed out.

"Engineer," he said. "Your door."


---

The two sisters at the third door had not moved.

They had not moved all through it. They stood one on either side of the door with their backs to the wall and their spears grounded; and the lamp on its hook between them burned on, with a moth going round it. The one on the left was old and grey and watched the tortoise like weather coming. The one on the right was small, with a freckled face and pale eyelashes, and she was holding her spear in both hands by the shaft, not levelled, upright, so tight that her knuckles were white; and she was crying, without any sound.

The tortoise walked down the passage to them and stopped.

Gaius stepped out of the third rank.

He found that he had given the screw roll to Fingers, and that Ear had stepped aside, and that he was standing in the passage in front of the door with no shield, between the two sisters, with the lamp over his head; and that his hand was up, the right one, to knock, and had stopped there.

It was a plain door. Pine, not oak; ledged and braced; on two strap hinges, small ones, that would not have held a gate. A latch with a thumb-lifter. No lock, no bar, no keeper for one. Anybody could have opened it from either side.

He looked at the latch, and could not put his thumb on it.

The door opened.

She was standing just inside it, in the dark room, with a candle in one hand that she had not lit, as though she had picked it up and forgotten what it was for. She was dressed. She was in the plain white of the house, a long shift and an over-gown of coarse undyed wool, belted, with her bag over her shoulder; and her hair was under a cloth, tied badly, with a long honey-coloured end of it coming out at the side. She was thinner. He saw that first, because Valeria had written it, and then because it was true: her face had come in under the bones, and there was a line between her brows that had not been there on the quay, a short straight line, as if somebody had drawn it with a rule. She was looking at him.

Behind him the whole tortoise stood in the passage and did not breathe.

"I heard the bar," said Liora.

"It's not broken."

"No. It wouldn't be." Her eyes went past him, over his shoulder, to the men in the passage with their shields; to Rufo, and Ear, and Sennius; and to the place in the front rank between Ear and Sennius where nobody was standing. They stayed there. Then they came back to him. "Is anybody hurt?"

"Nobody."

"Anybody, Gaius. Theirs or ours."

"Nobody," said Gaius. "A wrist jarred. A sister sat down on the stair. She'll have a bruise on her pride and nowhere else." He swallowed. "Nobody's bled. Nobody's going to."

She looked at him in the doorway, in the lamplight, as she looked at a wound to see if it was clean.

Then she put the candle down on the floor inside the door, unlit, very carefully, and stood up again; and put her hand into the bag on her shoulder, and took out something red, and held it in her hand. It was a cap. A soldier's cap of felted wool, dyed with madder, crushed and stained and faded at the crown where the sun had got at it; and he knew it as soon as he saw it, as every man in the passage knew it, and there was a sound behind him from the tortoise that was not a word.

She did not put it on. She held it.

Then she came out of the room.

She did not hurry. She stepped over the sill into the passage, and stopped in front of the small freckled sister on the right of the door, who was still holding her spear upright in both hands and still crying; and Liora put her free hand on the sister's arm.

"Thank you, Helvia," she said. "For the lamp. And the bread." The corner of her mouth went in. "And for tying the cloth better than I do. I'll not need it now."

The sister gaped at her.

"Go and see to Agrippina," said Liora. "She'll have sat down hard. She'll say she hasn't." She took her hand away. "Go on."

Sister Helvia went, with her spear, down the passage, between the shields; and Liora turned and looked at the tortoise.

"Well," she said. "Make room."

They made room. The front rank stepped apart, and the second, and the roof came up; and she walked into the middle of the tortoise as if she were walking into a house, with Tappo's cap in her hand, and stood in the middle of the second rank, by Rufo's shoulder; and the shields closed over her head.

Gaius went back to his place. He took the screw roll from Fingers.

"Back," said Rufo. "Slow. *Step.*"

---

They went down the stair, and through the hall, past the bar.

Agrippina was sitting on the bottom step with Sister Helvia beside her, with her nightcap in her lap and her great bare feet on the stone, and she looked up as the tortoise came down the stair past her, and looked into the gaps between the shields, and saw who was inside it. She opened her mouth. Then she shut it. Liora, going by in the middle of the shields, did not stop; but she put out her hand through the gap between Rufo's shield and the one behind it as she went by, and touched Agrippina's shoulder, once, and took the hand back.

Then they were in the porch, and the right-hand leaf stood against the wall, and the pintles shone in the light of the horn lamp, and the rain fell across the mouth of the porch; and they went out through it into the lane.

The tortoise turned at the foot of the lane, at the corner of the white wall, and went back along the canal walk the way it had come, past the poor gate, toward the harbor and the galley quay and the *Patience*, and the litter was there, where it had been.

The curtains were open. The bearers were on their feet, the four of them, in their grey, with the sacks off their heads, standing by the poles; and the lantern had been unhooded, and hung from the pole, and threw a round of yellow light on the wet stones of the walk. And in the middle of the round of light, out of the litter, in the rain, stood an old man.

He was small. He was smaller than Gaius had thought a lord would be. He was bent at the shoulders, and his hair was white and wet and stuck to his skull, and he wore a long coat of good black wool, old-fashioned, cut for a broader man than he was now, so that it hung on him like a coat on a peg; and the rain was running off the shoulders of it, and he did not seem to feel it. He had been standing there, Gaius thought, since the bar fell. He held something in both arms against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth, as a man holds a child.

He was looking into the tortoise, into the gaps between the shields, as it came along the walk toward him; and Gaius, at the crack in the third rank, saw his face in the lantern light. It was an old face, long and fine-boned, with the skin fallen in, and pale wet eyes. He was afraid. Not of the shields. Of the shields going by.

"*Rufo,*" said Liora, inside the tortoise. "Stop."

Rufo stopped.

The tortoise stood still in the rain, in the round of the lantern's light, a pace or two from the old man, and the canal hissed and steamed beside them.

Then the shields in front of her parted, and Liora stepped out between Rufo and Ear, and stood in the light.

The old man looked at her.

The old man said, "Liora."

He said it the way the old houses said everything, with the long vowels of the Old Hill, slowly, as if it were a word in a language he had not spoken for a long time and was afraid of getting wrong.

She stood in the rain, in the white of the house, with Tappo's cap in her hand, and looked at him. Gaius could not see her face. He could see the back of her head, and the badly tied cloth, and the wet hair coming out of it at the side; and he could see her shoulders, and that they were very straight.

"Father," she said.

The rain came down on them both.

"Do you eat?" said the old man.

"At the bells. There won't be bells now."

"No." The old man thought about that. "Then when you like."

"When I like," said Liora.

He nodded. He did not come any nearer to her, and he did not put out his hand. Then he looked past her, into the opening in the shields, at the men behind her, one after another, searching, until he had Gaius. Gaius did not know who had told him. Perhaps it was the roll on his shoulder.

The old man came forward then, three steps, slowly, past his daughter, to the edge of the shields; and held out what was in his arms.

"For the engineer," he said.

Gaius came out of the third rank. He put down the screw roll on the wet stones, and took the bundle. It was heavy, and square, and the oilcloth was warm from the old man's body, and he knew what it was by its weight before he knew anything else, as he would have known a block of oak; he had carried two like it for ten years.

"It's the fourth," said the old man. "Of Anaxis. The house kept it." He looked at Gaius, with the pale eyes. "The third went with somebody. A long time ago."

Gaius did not say anything.

He held the book, and put his other hand over the oilcloth, flat, where the rain was getting at it, and kept it there.

The old man looked at that. Then he nodded, as if something had been measured and found to be the right length, and stepped back, three steps, into the round of his lantern's light, beside his litter; and stood there with his empty hands at his sides.

Liora stood in the rain and looked at him; and then she did a thing he had seen her do once before, on Midwinter night at the quay fires, to a man with jug-handle ears and a scarred hand. She sank down before her father on the wet stones of the canal walk, in the plain white of the house, the whole slow way down, in the reverence of the old court, with her head bowed and Tappo's cap held against her breast; and stayed there for a breath; and rose.

Then she turned round and walked back into the shields, and they closed over her.

"*Step,*" said Rufo, not quite steadily.

The tortoise went on along the canal walk, in the rain, toward the harbor. Gaius went in his place in the third rank with the screw roll on one shoulder and the fourth volume of Anaxis held against his chest under his shirt, out of the wet. He put his eye to the crack between the shields once, at the bend, and the lantern was still there behind them on the walk, and a small black shape standing in its light, not moving; and then the bend took it.

---

The *Patience* went out of the royal harbor on the first of the ebb, a little before the light, with two dozen of the penal cohort at her oars.

Avitus stood at the helm with his cup and his cold and watched them come aboard, and did not say anything. He sneezed. Then he said to the helmsman, "Short of oars," and the *Patience* went out between the moles.

The rain stopped as they cleared the mole-towers.

Gaius looked back once, from the stern, before the city was gone. The Old Hill stood up black against a sky going grey, and along the shoulder of it, the length of the palace wall, the lanterns of the Lanciarii were still walking, slowly, in a line, from the harbor tower to the trees of the tombs; and the window at the turn of the lane was still lit. As he watched, the lanterns came to the place under the window and stopped there, all of them.

Then one of them, at the end of the line, lifted, high, as far as an arm would reach, and held there; and came down.

He did not know if it was meant for anything. He thought about it until the hill was gone, and decided that he would not ask.

---

The sun came up out of the sea on the larboard bow, red, with the *Patience* well out and the capital a smudge behind her and the steam going pink over it, and the sea long and quiet and the colour of a pigeon's breast. The men pulled. Nobody sang. Nobody had slept, and nobody seemed to want to.

Gaius sat under the stern deck with his back against the rudder-trunk, where he had sat the whole way north.

She was beside him on the coil of rope. She had taken the cloth off her hair as soon as they cleared the harbor, and wrung it out over the side, and let it go into the sea, and watched it go; and her hair was coming down out of its knot, wet and darkened, over the shoulder of the white wool. She had Tappo's cap in her lap. She had said nothing since the canal walk, and neither had he. He could not stop looking at her, and he did not try to say anything about it, because nothing he could think of would bear the weight. The old game did not come, the turn of a bilge and a curve he would know in any light.

The fourth volume lay on his knees in its oilcloth. He had not unwrapped it. He did not want to unwrap it on a wet ship, and he did not want to put it down.

Somewhere past the first hour she put out her hand and laid it on his knee.

The bad one. Flat, through the wool of his breeches, as she had laid it on the quay at Cyrene on the night the cutter took her; and he looked down at it, and waited for the warmth to come into it, and the red-gold into the rims of her fingers, and for himself to lift it off, and give it back to her, and say what he had said then.

There was no warmth in it. Only a hand. Cold from the rain, with the knuckles a little swollen, and a scrape across the back of it, and the thin white mark at the base of the thumb where the Vigil's lye had got into a crack. Resting on his knee as a hand rests on the rail of a boat, because it is there.

He let it stay.

Fingers came aft along the gangway between the benches then, with a heel of the *Patience*'s bread in each hand, as he did every morning, and stopped at the edge of the stern deck to hand them in. He studied the hand on the knee for some time, with the tip of his tongue between his teeth, as he read a lock.

Then he put the bread down on the coil of rope beside her, both heels, and went back along the gangway to his oar; and did not take out his chalk.

Liora picked up one of the heels of bread and broke it, and gave him the bigger half.

"Is it waiting?" she said. "The knee."

"It's waiting."

"Good." She ate a mouthful of the bread, and looked out over the stern at the long pink sea and the smudge where the city was. Her hand stayed where it was. "So am I," said Liora.

# Chapter Five: The King Laughs

**MARCUS**

The clerk climbed the stair at the second hour with the paper held in both hands in front of him, like a dish he had been told not to spill.

Marcus watched him go. He was a young clerk, with ink on the side of his middle finger and a careful walk, and the paper was three sheets sewn at the corner with a green thread: the watch-captain of the Lower Canal's report of the night before last, written fair, which was how the crown learned what its city had done. The guards on the stair door let him through without a word. He left the door a hand's width open behind him, because both his hands were full, and his feet climbed round the turn of the tower and were gone.

Marcus stood at the foot of it and waited to be sent for.

The Hall of Kings in high summer was the only cool place in the capital. The dome held the night in it till noon. The light came down from the ring of windows at the top in long slanted bars, full of dust going round slowly, and lay on the floor in pale ovals that moved across it through the day like a sundial nobody read; and under the dome it smelled as it always had, of stone and old incense and lamp-smoke, without the wet fur of winter. The council's long table stood empty at the foot of the stair with its benches pushed in. The king's chair stood on its step above it, with no cloth over its arms now, and its lamp unlit. Somewhere along the pillars a sweeper was working, and the sound of her broom whispered round the hall from all sides at once.

Gaius sat on the end of the council's bench with his bad leg out in front of him and his hands on his knees, which was as near as Marcus had ever seen him come to sitting still. The engineer had not slept, either. His eyebrows were coming back in short and stiff and paler than the rest of him, so that he looked faintly surprised all the time, even now.

"He'll not see us," said Gaius.

"He'll see the paper."

"Same thing, for him." Gaius looked up the stair. "What's in it?"

"Everything. Twenty-odd men in a tortoise on the canal walk, a litter, a door on the porch wall, the alarm bell, sisters in their shifts." Marcus had read it over the watch-captain's shoulder at the guardhouse on the canal before light, upside down, while the man pretended not to see him do it. "He wrote down that nobody bled. He wrote it twice. The second time he underlined it."

"Did he spell pintle right?"

"He called it a hinge."

Gaius grunted, and watched the stair.

They had come up the hill at first light, the two of them, because Marcus would not have the king hear it from the watch alone; and they had been told at the stair door, by a guard who did not know where to put his eyes, that His Majesty was not receiving, and that the Prefect might wait if he wished. So he wished. He had waited at the foot of that stair a good many mornings as a boy, to be told something at the top of it, and he knew how to stand there. He had not known he still knew.

He had come north at a run, and arrived too late. That had been the idea.

---

He had sent nobody. He had told Gaius so in the shed, with the report on the bench between them, and had meant it; and Gaius had gone at dawn with two dozen men who had not been sent either, and Marcus had stood at the slip gate and watched the *Patience* go out through the gap, and done nothing.

He had done nothing for a day. It was the hardest day's work he had done since Bastion.

On the morning of the second day he walked down to the long quay where *Constancy* lay. Nerva was already aboard her, and her stores already coming down the quay on the backs of the Tharsian yard-hands, though he had given no order for either.

"She's a day behind," said Nerva, when Marcus stepped aboard. His eyes went aft, to the steering oar and the man at it. "If you mean to stop them, you'll not."

"I don't mean to stop them."

Nerva looked at him a moment longer, and nodded, as at a bearing he could live with, and began to shout at the yard-hands.

Alana came down with her bag and the weather-book under her arm, and did not ask why. Felix followed with a jar of the fort's spring water on his shoulder; nobody had told him the day or the hour. And Domitia arrived last, in her white and her steel cap, with the Vigil's mail-bag on her hip and her spear in her hand and a face like a closed shutter, and stopped at the foot of the plank.

"I'll go north with you, Highness," she said. "If there's room."

"There's room."

"I write to the Abbess every night. Last night's has waited on the quay for the *Patience*." She shifted the spear to the crook of her arm, and tapped the mail-bag twice with one finger. "It'll be quicker if I carry it myself."

"It will."

"I mean to tell her everything in it," said Domitia, "in person, and nothing that isn't in it." She came up the plank, and corrected the angle at which Felix was holding the water jar, and went below; and was sick, Felix reported, before they cleared the moles, and sick the whole way north, in a bucket she had chosen herself and would let nobody else empty.

*Constancy* ran north fast. She was the best hull Gaius had ever built, and Nerva drove her, and she ran with the summer wind on her quarter for two days and a night, glad to be asked. Marcus stood at the rail forward most of the way, and drank Felix's water, and thought about what he would say to his father. He had a great many things to say, by the end. By the last morning he had thrown most of them over the side.

That morning, an hour after sunrise, the lookout in the bow sang out a sail ahead; and then, a little after, that it was a galley; and then, as she lifted over the curve of the sea, that she was the *Patience*, southbound, with all her oars out.

So it was done. Marcus knew it before they were near enough to see faces. A mail galley does not come south from the capital the day after she arrives in it, at a stroke like that, unless she has something aboard she wants away.

Nerva brought *Constancy* round across her bows and hove to, and the *Patience* came on and lost way and lay rolling within easy hail, near enough to see faces, with the sea slapping between them; and there on her stern was Avitus at the helm, grey and short, with his cup; and under the stern deck, Gaius, standing up; and beside Gaius, on a coil of rope, with a heel of bread in her hand and her hair down wet over her shoulders, Liora.

She was thinner. He could see that across the water. She looked at *Constancy*, and at him, and he did not need to read her face.

Marcus put his hands to his mouth.

"Is anybody hurt?"

Gaius's voice carried back flat across the water.

"Nobody."

"Theirs?"

"Nobody."

The men on the *Patience*'s benches had stopped pulling and were sitting up to see, all of them, with their oars in. He saw Rufo's head and shoulders above the rest, and Ear's crumpled ear, and Fingers, standing on a bench to see better and being told to get down by Avitus. He saw that their belts were empty. He saw a good many faces turned toward him and waiting, and understood that they were waiting to be told whether they had done right; and that he was not going to tell them.

"Avitus," he called. "Bring her round."

There was a silence across the water.

Then Gaius said, in the same flat voice, "She's out."

"I know."

"If we go back—"

"If you don't go back, you're deserters." He said it loudly enough for the benches. "Every man of you. Your sentences were stopped while you serve at Cyrene. Cyrene's behind me. The capital's behind you. The day you walk off a ship at Cyrene after last night, you're a cohort of convicts who broke a holy house and ran from it, and the Marshal can have you all back in irons by the next *Patience*, and he'll be right. If we go back, you're a cohort of convicts who broke a holy house and stood still to answer for it. That's a different thing."

"Is it?" said Gaius.

"I'm betting your necks that it is."

Gaius looked at him across the water. Then he turned his head, and looked down at the woman on the coil of rope.

Liora stood up. She stood with one hand on the rail and the bread still in the other, and looked across at *Constancy*, and then back along the sea behind her to the north, where the capital was a smudge with the steam over it.

"I didn't run out of that house," said Liora. Her voice carried over the water as clear as a bell across a field; she had always been able to make a ward full of groaning men hear her without raising it. "I walked. I'm not going to run from the city."

She sat down again, and went on with the bread.

So the *Patience* turned, slowly, with her men grinning at each other on the benches. Her men had not slept, and Marcus had both ships lie to out of sight of the city through the heat of the day while they did, so that they would come in when the lower town was on its doorsteps to see it; and they came in under the Old Hill at dusk, with the harbor bell ringing the hour and the steam going up off the canals into a sky the colour of a hot coal. Domitia went ashore first, green, with the mail-bag, and away along the galley quay toward the Lower Canal without a word to anyone, at a pace that would have done for a forced march. She had not been on deck once since Cyrene. She had not, Marcus realised afterward, laid eyes on Liora at all.

And Marcus walked the cohort up the canal walk to the Drowned Lantern, in the last of the light, in plain sight of the whole lower town, with their empty belts and their shields on their backs and the woman they had stolen walking in the middle of them; and the lower town came out of its doors to watch them go by, and said nothing at all, and did not go back in.

---

Up the stair, a long way up, somebody made a sound.

It fell through the door the clerk had left open, and round the turn of the tower, and out into the Hall of Kings, small and broken by the stone it had come through. Marcus turned his head. It was a dry sound, a cough that had something wrong with it, or a man choking on a crumb; it went on too long for either, in short pieces, and stopped, and began again, higher; and then it came down the stair one last time, a single long breath of it, out of somebody old, and stopped altogether.

The sweeper's broom had stopped too.

The two guards on the stair door were looking at each other. One of them was a grizzled man of the old palace guard who had stood on that door, Marcus thought, since Marcus was in short tunics. His mouth had come open, and stayed.

"Is he ill?" said Marcus.

The grizzled guard shut his mouth. "No, Highness," he said; and then, less sure, "I don't think so, Highness."

Gaius had got up off the bench. His short new eyebrows had gone up as far as they had to go, and stayed there.

"What was that?" he said.

"I don't know."

He stood at the foot of his father's stair and tried to put a name to it, and could not. He thought of the pain in the hip that old men get, and of a man weeping who has not done it in years and has forgotten how it goes. He thought of the wind in a crack. None of them was it.

The clerk came down a little after, slower than he had gone up, with his hands empty, and the face of a man who has been shown something he was never taught the word for, and stopped on the last step.

"His Majesty," said the clerk, and swallowed, and started again. "His Majesty will see the parties tomorrow, at the third hour. Both parties. The Lord Marshal, the Mother Abbess, the Prefect and," he looked at his wax, "the engineer."

"Tomorrow," said Marcus.

"At the third hour, Highness." The clerk hesitated, and then said, low, not as part of the message: "He asked me to read him the part about the spear twice. The one that was handed back."

He crossed the hall with his careful walk. The sweeper began again among the pillars. The guards looked at the wall in front of them.

Marcus stood on at the foot of the stair. Then he walked out into the heat with Gaius limping beside him, and down the hill.

---

The Drowned Lantern had not charged them for anything since they came in at dusk, and would not.

Marcus had tried, the night before, with the purse on the counter. The landlord had looked at it, and then at him, with the wet rag on his shoulder and his heavy grey face giving nothing away, and pushed it back across the wood with two fingers, as he might have pushed back a coin he suspected. "Not this week, Highness," he said, and went to draw small beer for Rufo.

So the cohort ate and drank at the Lantern's charge, and slept on its floor and in its yard and the lean-to, with their shields stacked against the midden wall, and the lower town came by all day to look at them. It did not come in. It stood in the lane and at the canal window, under the sack, and stared; and then it went away, and left things on the step. A basket of eggs, the first morning, with straw in it. A string of onions. A cheese wrapped in dock leaves. A cooked fowl, still warm, under a cloth, with no name on the cloth and nobody in the lane when Fingers stepped out to see who had left it. By that afternoon there was a row of things along the Lantern's wall like the offerings along the wall of a shrine, and Plancus had stopped bringing them in and begun to sort them by what would keep.

Marcus sat at the table under the canal window. It was the table he had sat at for three years, more nights than not; the wood was scarred with the same knife-marks and the same rings. He had not chosen it. His feet had brought him to it before he noticed.

He had a cup of water in front of him. Felix had seen to that before the bench was warm.

The window had no glass in it still. There was the sack nailed over its lower half, gone grey and stiff with the canal's steam, and above the sack the empty frame, and through it the canal going by, green and smoking, with the light coming off it in bright pieces that moved on the ceiling. He had gone through that window once, headfirst, in the snow, on the night of his brother's funeral, and come up out of the canal into the steam to see Roderic in the broken frame above him, settling his cloak. He did not remember much else of the night. He remembered the window.

When the landlord passed with the jug he put a silver piece on the table, square in front of the empty frame.

The landlord looked at it.

"That's the window," said Marcus.

"I know which window it is, Highness." The landlord considered the silver, and the frame, and almost smiled. Then he took the silver, and put it in his apron, and left the cup as it was.

Upstairs, Liora was asleep.

She had slept most of the day and all the night before, in the Lantern's one good room under the roof, with the door on the latch and Sennius on a stool outside it. Nobody had told him to sit there. He had been sitting there when Marcus came back from the hill, with his hands on his knees and his back against the wall, and when Marcus came up to the head of the stair he took his hands off his knees quickly, as if they had been caught somewhere they should not be, and stood.

"She's sleeping, Highness."

"Good."

"She woke once. She asked what the time was, and if anybody was hurt." Sennius looked at the door. "I said nobody. She said she knew, she wanted to hear it again."

Marcus left him there.

She came down at dusk, in the house's white shift and the undyed over-gown she had walked out in, with her hair tied back in a strip of somebody's linen and her feet bare on the Lantern's boards; and the common room, which had been loud, went quiet in pieces, from the stair outward, and then got loud again a little too fast. She went round it first, every man in it, a hand on a shoulder here and a look at a knuckle there; and Ear held out his shin to show her where the scar had healed white, and she bent and looked at it gravely and told him it had healed very well, which he had known; and he was happy for an hour.

Then she sat across the table from Marcus, under the window, with her back to the canal.

The landlord brought her a bowl of the Lantern's mutton and barley without being asked, and a round of bread, and a cup of small beer; and stood a moment with the jug, looking at her, and went away.

"You saw him," said Liora.

"I saw his stair. He'll see us tomorrow. Me, Gaius, the Marshal, your Abbess."

"Not me."

"No."

"Good." She ate a spoonful of the barley, and then another, carefully, as people eat who have been eating by the bells for six weeks and have not got out of the way of it yet. "I don't want to stand in a room and be talked about. I've done that."

He did not ask her about it.

"They'll be punished," she said. "Won't they. For me."

"They might."

"They knew they might." She put the spoon down. "Ear told me, on the ship. He told me the way he'd tell me the weather. He said they'd talked it over in the lean-to before the bar came down, and decided they didn't mind." The corner of her mouth went in. "He's nineteen."

"So was I, once."

"You were never nineteen like that," said Liora, and picked up the spoon again.

He laughed. He had not laughed since before the shed.

She ate the rest of the bowl, and the bread, and drank the beer, and wiped the bowl with the last of the bread as the cohort did; and then sat with her hands round the empty cup and looked past him at the room, at Rufo with his tally-stick across his knees, at Fingers losing at dice to the cousins, at Gaius at the far end of the long table with the fourth volume of Anaxis open in front of him and a candle at his elbow and his chin on his fist.

"He hasn't read a word of it," she said.

"No."

"He opened it on the ship. He turned the pages one after another to the end and shut it again." She turned the cup in her hands. "He's afraid it's better than the third."

"Is it?"

"I don't know. I never read either." She set the cup down. "My father kept them in a press in the long room, with the key on his ring, and I wasn't allowed to touch them. I used to look at the backs." She stood up. "I'm going to sleep again. I may sleep for a week. Tell your father, if he asks, that I'm eating."

"He won't ask."

"Tell him anyway," said Liora, and climbed the stair to where Sennius was, and he stood up off his stool as she reached it.

---

Marcus took the stair fast. He took every stair fast now, to have it behind him.

At the first turn of the tower he stopped, and waited, with one hand on the cold stone of the wall; and Gaius came up after him at his own pace, a step at a time, the good leg first and the bad one after, with his jaw shut. He did not look up. He reached the turn and passed it without stopping, and Marcus climbed behind him, at the engineer's pace, which was slow, and they came to the top together.

The steward was waiting with the door. There was no lamp in his hand; it was high summer, and the third hour, and the tower's slit windows let in enough light to see the dust on the steps. He looked them both over, and said, "Highness. Engineer," and opened the door.

The room was full.

It was a small room for five people and a clerk. The king sat at his writing table with his back to the window and the summer light behind him, so that Marcus had to look into it to see his face, and the face was only a long grey shape with the light round its edges. The window was open. The smell of the harbor came up through it, warm weed and tar and the hot-egg breath of the canals; and on the sill, within reach of the king's chair, lay the length of carved stern-rail with the crowned dolphin cut in it, where it had lain at Midwinter. The young clerk sat on a stool at the end of the table with a board on his knees and his wax, and the watch-captain's report under his hand.

The Lord Marshal stood at the right of the table. He was in his ribbons, all of them, and he had the look of a man who has arrived early to a room in order to have been in it first. Across from him, at the left of the table, with the light from the window on the side of her face, stood Cassandra.

She was in her white, with her coif starched and her cloak over her mail, and she had taken her steel cap off and held it under her arm, as a soldier holds his helmet before a commander. Her long plain face was perfectly still. Her brows were as straight as a rule. Her eyes went to Marcus as he came in, and to Gaius behind him, and stayed on Gaius a moment longer; and went back to the king, and did not move.

"Prefect," said the king. "Engineer."

"Majesty."

"Sit down."

There were no chairs but his. Nobody sat.

The king turned his head to the clerk. "The count," he said.

The clerk looked up, startled, with his stylus over the wax.

"Not you." The king turned back. "Engineer. How many men went into the Vigil's house on the night of the new moon?"

Gaius stood with his weight on the good leg. "Two dozen, Majesty," he said. "And me."

"Armed how?"

"Shields."

"Swords?"

"On the bollards at Cyrene. They hung them up at the head of the slip before we sailed." Gaius thought, and was honest. "And a bread-knife."

The king's long shape against the window did not move.

"Mother Abbess. How many of your sisters bled?"

"None, Majesty." Cassandra's voice was level, and carried, as it would carry down a line of shields. "A wrist jarred. A cap lost."

"How many of them did he take out of your house?"

"One. Who was not a sister."

"How many locks broken?"

"None."

"Doors?"

"One," said Gaius, before she could. "The right-hand leaf of the great door, Majesty. Lifted off its pintles. It's leaning against the porch wall. I offered to put it back."

"Then put it back," said the king.

Gaius looked at Cassandra.

She looked at the king, and not at him. "He took it down, Majesty. He may put it up. Sister Agrippina will hold the lamp."

"That's settled, then." The king laid one hand flat on the writing table, square to its edge. "Two dozen men and an engineer. Shields. A bread-knife. No sister bled, no lock broken, one door, and the door will be on its pintles by noon. Is that the count, Marshal?"

"That is the count, Majesty." Varro had not moved. "I would add to it that they were convicts."

"So they were."

"Under sentence. Whose sentences were suspended by Your Majesty's own writ while they served at Cyrene, and at no other place." Varro's voice was a good deal deeper than the king's, and had a parade-ground's distances in it; it was a voice for telling the back rank something. "Your Majesty's capital is not at Cyrene. By the writ, they are what they were before it. And they have broken a holy house by night, in Your Majesty's capital, under the Old Hill, half a mile from where Your Majesty sits."

"A little more than half a mile."

"Majesty."

"I've walked it." The king turned his head. "Prefect."

Marcus had decided on the way up the stair what he would say, and he said it.

"They're mine, Majesty. They went because they thought I couldn't send them, and they were right, and I let them go. I turned them back at sea and brought them here to answer for it. Whatever the sentence is, it's mine."

"No," said the king. "It's theirs. Their sentences were theirs before you ever saw them. You can't have them now because you've grown fond of them." He was silent a moment. "The Marshal is right. By my writ they are convicts again, and they broke a holy house in my city by night, and every man of them could be sent to the quarries before the week was out, or worse; and I have no doubt the Marshal has the paper for it."

Varro said nothing.

"I pardon them," said the king.

Nobody spoke. The clerk's stylus had stopped over the wax. Down in the harbor, through the open window, a gull was screaming at something, on and on.

"Every man of them," said the king. "Of the sentences they came to Cyrene under, and of the night of the new moon. The engineer too." He looked at the clerk. "Write it."

The clerk wrote it.

Varro pressed the bull's head of his seal ring into the heel of his hand, and took it away, and the small white bull filled slowly red. Marcus watched him do it. He was too old a soldier to stand in a gap he had already lost.

"Then I ask one thing, Majesty," said Varro.

"Ask it."

"That their names be entered. Every man of them, by name, in the crown's book, as convicts pardoned for breaking the Vigil's house. So that it is known, in the city and after, who they are, and what they did, and that the crown pardoned it; and that the next man who thinks of lifting a door in this city knows his name will be written down too."

"Enter them," said the king. "Every one."

The clerk wrote that as well. Marcus saw that his hand was not quite steady.

"And that the pardon be cried," said Varro. "Publicly. By the city's crier, at the door of the house they broke, at noon today, before the order and before the city. So that the order has its satisfaction, Majesty, for it is owed one; and so that the city knows the crown does not count breaking houses a small thing, whatever it pardons."

The king was silent for a moment.

"Cry it," he said. "At noon. At the door the engineer will have hung by then." He turned his long grey shape a little toward Varro. "And cry the names, Marshal. Every one. As you say. So that it's known."

"Majesty."

There was something in Varro's face Marcus had not seen there before, at court or at the funeral or across the Lesser Hall at Midwinter. It was there and gone.

"Mother Abbess," said the king.

"Majesty."

"You have something to ask."

"The healer, Majesty." Cassandra did not shift her cap under her arm or move her eyes. "She was in the order's keeping, in the order's house, under the order's rule. She was taken out of it by night. I ask where she is."

"Is she here?"

"No, Majesty."

"Has she asked the crown for anything?"

The king did not turn to Marcus. Marcus answered anyway.

"Nothing, Majesty. She's asked if anybody was hurt, and when to eat."

"The crown has no business inside a holy house," said the king, to Cassandra. "The Marshal has told me so, often, and he is right. I'll not send my guard into your house, Mother Abbess, and I'll not send my guard to carry anybody back into it." He put the other hand on the table, beside the first. "If she is yours, rule her. If she is not, she may go where she likes."

Cassandra's teeth clicked, once.

"The order rules its own, Majesty," she said.

She made the king a soldier's bow, short, from the neck, with the cap under her arm, and turned, and walked out past Gaius without looking at him; and Marcus heard her go down the stair, quick and even, a long way down, until the tower had her.

"Marshal," said the king. "Engineer. Leave us."

He lifted two fingers off the table.

---

When the door was shut the king did not say anything for a time.

He sat with his back to the window. The light lay along the floor between them, and on the writing table, on the candle and the plain paper squared to the edge. Marcus stood in front of it as he had stood as a boy, to be told something, and waited to be told it.

The king put his hand inside his shirt, and took out the key on its chain, and leaned forward and unlocked the right-hand drawer, the deep one, and opened it.

He took out a paper and laid it on the table, face up, turned so that Marcus could read it.

Marcus had never seen it. He had known for two years that it was there, from Valeria and from Varro's letters and from the whole court's knowing; he had known what it said. He had not known it would be so beautiful. It was a single sheet of the palace's best, written from edge to edge in a hand like a run of music, every letter leaning the same way, every line the same distance from the last; the most beautiful hand in the palace, which everybody knew was Damien's. *By the King. The Prince Marcus, Prefect of Cyrene, is recalled.* It went on from there. It had a space at the foot for a name, and no name in it.

"The Marshal came up after Bastion," said the king. "He stood where you are, and asked me to sign it."

Marcus looked at the paper.

"He says you failed," said the king.

"I did."

"He says you lost a gate."

"I didn't buy it."

The king looked at him. Marcus could see his face now, with the light come round a little; long and grey and lined, the hair white at the back, the eyes deep and tired, and steady. He studied his son as he studied a column of figures, to be sure of it.

Then he took the recall, and pulled the drawer wide to lay it back, and tipped it to the light; and Marcus, at the table's corner, saw what lay at the bottom before it went down. A smaller sheet, plain paper, with two words on it in a hand he knew from the scrap of a letter that had come to Cyrene round a stone, cramped and slow, every downstroke pressed hard: *Leave granted.* The recall went down on top of it, in its beautiful hand, and covered it.

"It stays where it is," said the king.

He shut the drawer and turned the key, and put the key back inside his shirt.

"You turned them round at sea," he said.

"Yes."

"They'd have been at Cyrene by now."

"Yes."

"Where I'd have had to send for them." The king put his hands flat on the table again. "The whole council would have watched me send for them. It would have taken a month, and the songs would have had a month." He was silent. "You brought them up my hill yourself, to be judged. Before I asked."

"They're mine," said Marcus.

"So you said." His father looked at him a moment longer. "Have you eaten?"

"Yes."

"Eat again," said the king. "You're thinner than I am."

Marcus went down the stair slowly, a step at a time; and at the foot of it, where the council's table stood, Gaius was sitting on the end of the bench with his leg out, waiting for him, and got up.

---

The door went back on a little before noon.

Gaius did it with the cousins on the screw's bar and the jack set under the leaf's bottom strap, as it had been set on the night of the new moon, the other way about; and Marcus stood on the cobbles of the lane with his back to the warehouse wall and watched him do it, because there was nothing else on earth he would rather have watched. The leaf had stood all that time where the front rank had leaned it, against the porch wall beside the horn lamp, with the stone sun over it and the great iron bar fallen across the threshold behind it, inside, where nobody in the house had chosen to move it. The house had used its poor gate since. Its great door had stood open to the lane, with a sister on it.

The sister on it now was Agrippina.

She was big and raw-boned and dressed today, in her white, with a coif starched to creaking; and she held the horn lamp. She had taken it off its bracket and lit it, at noon, in high summer, with the sun straight down into the lane and the cobbles too hot to stand on barefoot, and she stood holding it up at the hinge end of the doorway, at arm's length, with the flame invisible in the daylight and her face like a magistrate's. She did not speak to Gaius. Gaius did not speak to her. He knelt on the porch slabs with the jack and his rule and a pot of tallow, and every time he wanted to see the pintles better he leaned toward the lamp, slightly, and she moved it, slightly, so that it was where he wanted it; and he said "Thank you, Sister," and she said nothing at all.

The cousins turned the screw. The leaf rose, and swung over, and settled onto its three pintles one after another, sweetly, and the jack came out from under it, and the leaf hung.

Gaius stood up, with his hand on the porch pillar for the knee, and swung it.

It swung. It turned on its pintles from the wall to the closed position without a sound, and met the left-hand leaf true; and he swung it open again, and stood with his hand flat on the oak.

Then he took something out of his pouch. Marcus had to step forward to see what it was: three small round caps of bronze, each with a lip, each no bigger than an acorn cup, still bright from the file. He must have had the smith on the Canal Steps make them before he ever climbed the hill. He held them out to Agrippina on his open palm.

She looked at them, over the lamp.

"Pintle-caps," said Gaius. "Set one over the top of each pintle, when the leaf's hung, and peen the lip over, and nobody lifts that door off from outside again. Not with a jack. Not with anything."

Agrippina regarded him over the lamp. Then she put the lamp in his other hand, without asking, and took the caps off his palm one by one, and stood on her toes, and set them herself over the three pintles of the right-hand leaf; and held out her hand for his hammer.

He gave it to her. She peened the lips over with three blows each, hard, not looking at him, like a woman nailing something shut that has been open too long. Then she gave the hammer back, and took the lamp, and blew it out, and shut herself inside.

Gaius stood on the porch with the hammer.

"It's a good door," he said, to nobody.

By then the lane was full.

It had been filling since before the door went up. It filled from both ends: down the canal walk from the bend, and up from the harbor end of the lane, and out of the backs of the warehouses where men had been loading. The dye-house women came with their blue arms folded. The fishwives climbed from the galley quay with the morning's last baskets on their hips, on their way somewhere else, stopping. The canal's poor came, from the champions' sand and the poor gate round the corner, in the rags they had been healed in. Children came, and climbed onto the warehouse walls. By the time the crier pushed down the lane from the bend with his bell and his staff and the palace's blue sash across his chest, and the Marshal's clerk behind him with a roll, there was no room to stand in the lane, and the crier had to be passed forward over a cart.

Marcus had sent for the cohort. They stood in the street before the porch, in a block, with their backs to the door Gaius had just hung, because there was nowhere else to put them; and the city stood round them on three sides and on the walls, and looked at them, and was very quiet.

The crier rang his bell. He was a little man with a great voice, which was the trade.

"*Hear the King!*" he cried. "*Aurelian, by the grace of the Sunstone King of Aeridor and the Isles, to his city: whereas certain men, being convicts under sentence and in the service of the crown at Cyrene, did by night enter the house of the Vigil by the Lower Canal, and did take out of it one Liora, a healer, and did draw no blood—*"

He stopped there, because he had to. The lane had made a sound. It was not a cheer yet. It went along the walls and the walk and back like wind going over standing barley.

"*—the King of his grace pardons them,*" the crier carried on, louder, "*every man, of their sentences and of that night; and wills that their names be entered in the crown's book, and cried at this door, that it be known who they are!*"

He held out his hand behind him without looking, and the Marshal's clerk put the roll in it.

"*Rufo,*" cried the crier, "*called the Berserker, optio, of the penal cohort!*"

The lane roared.

It came off the walls and the walk and the cart and the warehouse roofs all at once, a great flat crash of sound, and rose between the houses and broke back off the Vigil's white wall; and Rufo, who had been standing at the front of the block with his tally-stick in his fist, flinched as Marcus had not seen him flinch at Bastion. His hand went up to the plait above his ear, and stayed there.

"*Lucius, called Ear!*"

The roar again. Somebody on the warehouse wall shouted, "*Which ear?*" and a woman screamed with laughter, and Ear held up his left one, and that got the biggest roar yet.

"*Sennius of Lessa,*" cried the crier, and stopped, and looked at the roll, and read it again because it was written there: "*prisoner of war!*"

It was a Tharsian name in a capital that had buried its sons for five years. The lane did not know, for a moment, what to do with it. Marcus felt the silence come down, and heard a man say *Tharsian* somewhere among the fishwives, in the old voice; and Sennius, in the second rank, stood straight with his hands at his sides, and his face went the colour of the Vigil's wall.

"He held the healer's basin," shouted a woman up at the poor gate. "At the Baths. In chains. It's in the song."

It was not in the song. Marcus knew the song, all its wrong verses. But the woman at the poor gate had the voice to carry it, and a hand that Liora had mended, from the way she held it up; and the lane took her word over the song's, and roared for Sennius of Lessa as it had roared for Rufo, and louder. Sennius stood in the noise as he would have stood in rain, with his face working, and did not know where to put his hands.

So it went on. *Spurius Lento*, cried the crier, and nobody knew who that was until Fingers took his hat off, and a dye-house woman with blue arms to the elbow screamed "*SPURIUS!*" in a voice like a gull's, and Fingers put his hat back on and said something to her across the lane that Marcus could not hear and the women round her could, and they shrieked. *Rusticus and Salvius, brothers.* *Naevius and Lollius*, who stood side by side and said nothing, as ever, and were cheered for it. *Vatinius*, who shut his eyes. *Plancus. Strabo. Scaeva.* The sawyers of the slip-yard, by name, who were not convicts at all and had been written down with the rest because the Marshal's clerk had not known the difference, and were cheered as convicts and loved it. The men of the Silverwood cutting, one after another.

"*Gaius,*" cried the crier, last, "*engineer!*"

Gaius was still on the porch, with the hammer. The lane turned and saw him there, in front of the door he had taken off and put back, and the roar that went up then was different from the others: it had laughing in it. Gaius considered the hammer in his hand, and put it behind his back.

The crier rolled up the roll and rang his bell, and that was the end of the king's business.

It was not the end of the lane's. The lane came in.

---

They were carried to the Fishmarket Steps, as near as made no difference.

Marcus remembered afterward only pieces of the way. The canal walk solid with people from bank to wall, and the cohort in the middle of it like a boat in a flood. A man he had never seen pressing a skin of wine on Rufo, and Rufo handing it on to the next man without drinking. The Lantern's landlord standing in his door with his rag on his shoulder, watching it go by. Somebody shouting up at the Lantern's roof, *Angel! Angel!*, until the window under the eaves opened and Liora put her head out, with her hair down, and looked at the lane full of people from wall to canal; and then the window shut, and a little after the Lantern's door opened, and she came down into it, barefoot still, and Sennius pushed out of the cohort and fell in at her back, and the lane made room.

It made room all the way down the canal and round the harbor head and along the galley quay to the Steps. It went ahead of her and closed behind, so that she walked the whole way in a space the width of a cart, as she had walked once behind a barrow. Marcus walked behind her with Alana, and watched the city look at her. It did not touch her. Women held up children to see, and an old man with no teeth in his head stood on a bollard and wept, and nobody came nearer than the cart's width. She walked through it looking at faces, and when she saw one she knew from the poor gate she stopped and spoke to it; and then it was all right to come nearer, for that one, and it came.

The Fishmarket Steps went up from the water one house on another's shoulders, with the fish-bell at the bottom and the evening light on the top. The Steps had been at it all afternoon, while the lane made its slow way round the harbor head behind her. Somebody had dragged the fish-boards together on the landing above Gemella's step to make a table, and somebody else had lit fires in braziers along the quay at the bottom, and there were eels splitting on the coals and mutton turning, and a smell coming off the whole hill of fat and onions and charcoal and fish that Marcus would have known with his eyes shut, from any of a thousand mornings.

Felix's mother had Marcus before he had got his foot on the first step.

She was small and broad, in her shawl in spite of the heat, with forearms like a cooper's, and she came down the steps through the crowd as a barge comes down a canal, by not stopping. She looked at him from his boots to his face.

"You've not been eating," said Gemella.

"I've been eating."

"Not what I'd call eating." She put her hand on his arm, and he was sat down on her step, the bottom one, by the eel-board, before he knew he had been moved, with his back against her house-wall and a board on his knees. There was an eel on it, split and done on the coals with salt till the skin blistered, and a slab of mutton off the spit with the fat crackling at the edge, and a barley loaf torn in half, and onions roasted whole in their skins. "Eat that," said Gemella. "And then I'll see."

He ate it. It was good.

Felix was at his elbow with a cup, which was water; he had not seen Felix arrive, and he had not seen him fill it. Felix stood with the jug, looking out at the quay, with an expression of grave disapproval that Marcus had learned, over six years, meant Felix was happy.

"Your mother," said Marcus, with his mouth full.

"Strictly, Highness, it's her step."

"I know whose step it is."

"Then strictly you should have asked before you sat on it." Felix filled the cup again, which was not empty. "She'd have said yes. But strictly."

Valeria came by at dusk.

She was coming down the Steps from above, in her mail with the blue cloak over it, with two of the Lanciarii at her back and the Farrier on her shoulder; and she stopped on the step above him, and looked down at him eating Gemella's mutton on the bottom step of the Fishmarket with the city all round him, and her face, with the white scar from eye to jaw, was hard to read.

"Highness."

"Captain. You're going up the hill."

"All night. The Marshal's order." She said it with no expression at all. Then she said, "Did you hear it? Yesterday."

He did not pretend not to know what she meant. "I heard something."

"The whole tower heard it. The guard at the top sent down to the guard at the bottom, after, to ask what it was, and the guard at the bottom didn't know." Valeria looked out over the quay, at the fires, at the cohort round the braziers. "I waited at the foot of that stair for your brother more nights than I could tell you. Four years, near enough. Up at a run with a dispatch, down a step at a time with the answer." She was quiet a moment. "I never heard it once."

"Heard what?"

Valeria looked down at him with great patience.

"He laughed, Highness," she said. "Your father. It was him laughing."

She carried on down the Steps with her two men behind her, toward the hill and the palace wall and the long night, and Marcus sat on Gemella's step with his board on his knees and could not eat.

---

Domitia was at the wall.

He did not see her come. He looked up when Gemella took the board off his knees, and stood, and went up the three steps to the landing's edge; and there she was, across the landing at the foot of the second flight, standing with her back against the corner of a house where the Steps turned: in her white, with her coif and her mail, and the steel cap under her arm, and the belled spear grounded beside her. The crowd went round her as water goes round a post. Nobody on the Steps knew what she was, except that she was a Vigil sister, and three nights ago the Vigil had been the side that lost; and the fishwives gave her a little room for it, and eyed her sidelong, and she did not seem to notice.

She was looking at Liora.

Liora was on the landing above Gemella's step, by the fish-boards, with the canal's poor round her and some of the Steps' children, and Gemella's eldest granddaughter at her shoulder doing something to her hair. Somebody had got her a gown. It was green wool, a girl's best, with the hem too short for her by a span and the sleeves stopping at the elbow; she had let it be put on her as she would have let a dressing be put on a wound, and then looked down at herself in it and laughed. Her hair was down. The granddaughter had combed it out, and it fell heavy and honey-coloured over the green to her waist, and the light of the brazier under the landing lay in it.

Marcus had never once heard Domitia called anything but plain. She was heavyset, and forty-five, and had the forearms of a woman who had lifted men out of the mud for twenty years; and her face, under the coif, had been made for telling people they were wrong. She stood against the corner of the house with her spear and looked at the woman she had been sent to Cyrene to replace, whom she had never seen until this hour; and he saw her face do a thing he had not known it could do. It went quiet. Everything in it that had been ready to correct drained out of it, and left it open, and a good deal younger.

She stood so a good while. Then, slowly, she looked down at herself: at the white let out at the sides of her mail, where the seams had been opened and pieced; and lifted one finger, and tapped the pieced seam twice, as she tapped a thing before she put it right.

She did not say anything. She looked up again at Liora.

---

The piper on the Steps was blind, or said he was, and had played for the Fishmarket since before Felix could walk. He sat on an upturned eel-tub on the landing with a boy beside him on another tub, beating a fish-box with two bones, and he had played every tune the Steps knew, twice, and was starting on the third time round.

Liora crossed to him between tunes, and bent, and said something in his ear.

He listened, with his blind face turned up. Then he nodded, and wet his lips, and put the pipe to them; and what it gave was not a quay tune at all. It was slow and grave and went round on itself in long steps, like people walking round a hall in their best clothes; and the whole of the Steps, who went up the hill every Midwinter for the goose-ends, knew it at once from the far end of the Lesser Hall, where they had heard it played for princes. It was the Long Measure.

The landing cleared without being asked. The children were pulled back by their collars.

Liora took something out of the bosom of the green gown, and stooped, and laid it on the stones of the landing, in the middle of the clear space. It was a red woollen cap, a soldier's, gone dark and stiff and out of shape, with a crease across the crown where somebody had crushed it in a fist.

Marcus knew it. Every man of the cohort on the Steps knew it. He heard the knowledge go along the quay behind him in a breath, from brazier to brazier: Rufo's head coming up, and Ear's, and Fingers going still with a cup halfway to his mouth. It was Tappo's. He had jigged with it in his fist on the long quay at Cyrene one Midwinter night while this woman danced the court way round him, and he had died at Bastion bringing her to Roderic.

She stood over it a moment with her hands at her sides. Then she sank.

It was the deep reverence of the court, down and down until the short green skirt lay round her on the stones and her bowed head was over her knee; the one a girl is taught for queens. She made it to the cap. She rose out of it slowly, and held out her hand, palm up, to the empty air above it, where a man would have stood.

And she danced the court way round it.

The piper had the measure, and the boy on the fish-box had the beat under it, slow; and she went round the cap in the long walking steps of it, with her hand out to nobody and her head up, and her bare feet sure on the warm stones. Marcus had seen it danced by every lady of the court, every Midwinter of his boyhood, and by his brother; he had never seen it danced like this, on a fish-landing, to a red cap, by a woman in a borrowed gown with no shoes. She went round once with the space empty round her, and the whole of the Steps watching her from the stones and the windows and the roofs, and nobody breathing; and he saw, before she came round to face him, that her face was wet.

On the second turn her hand went out, as the court way sends it, into the air where nothing should have been.

Ear was in it.

He had come off the quay and up the steps and through the crowd, and across the clear space onto the landing, without anybody seeing him go; and he did not take her hand. He did not know how. He stood beside the cap with his long arms hanging, nineteen, freckled, with his crumpled ear, and began to jig.

He could not jig, either. Nobody in the cohort could. He jigged all knees and elbows and boots, with no notion of the beat at all; and it was not his dance he was doing, Marcus saw. It was another man's, as near as he could remember it.

Then Rufo was there.

Rufo took the steps in two strides and onto the landing and began to stamp, gravely, with his tally-stick still in his fist, like a bull trying to put out a grass fire; and then Sennius, white to the lips, who had never seen the jig and did it anyway; and Rusticus and Salvius, making it a contest; and the cousins, side by side, in silence, in perfect step with one another and with nothing else; and Fingers; and Plancus, and Vatinius with his eyes shut, and Scaeva, and the sawyers, and the men of the cutting. They came up off the quay and the steps and out of the crowd one after another and then all at once, and filled the landing round her, and jigged, every man of them as badly as a man can, round her and round the red cap on the stones. The piper played the Long Measure grave and slow through the middle of it, and the boy beat the fish-box under it, and the Steps began to clap, and then to stamp, and then to roar.

She stopped dancing. She stood in the middle of them with the cap at her feet and her hands over her mouth, and she was laughing, and she was crying, both, so that Marcus could not tell from the bottom step which it was, and he did not think she could.

Then she went round them, every one, as she went round a ward. She took Ear by the shoulders and pulled him down to her and held him, and he held her back, carefully, with both hands flat, with his face over her shoulder gone to pieces. She held Rufo, who had to bend nearly double, and who put his arms round her and lifted her off the stones without meaning to and set her down again, gently, and said something into her hair. She held Sennius, who stood rigid for a moment, and then did not. She held the cousins together, one in each arm, and they held her, and for once neither of them looked at the other. She held Fingers, who said something that made her laugh into his collar. She went round them all, every one, the men of the cutting whose names she knew from the pallets and the sawyers whose names she did not; and every one of them held her, and none of them let go first.

Marcus looked across the landing for Domitia.

She was still against the corner of the house, with the spear and the cap. She had not moved. Her face was wet too, and she had done nothing about it.

---

Alana came to him at the bottom of the Steps, by the water, when it had got dark.

He had come down there out of the noise to stand by the fish-bell, where the harbor slapped at the bottom step and the lights of the far side lay on the black water in long broken lines. The galleys were moored across from him, and beyond them, along the foot of the Old Hill, the old berths, and *Constancy* among them, with a lantern at her stern. Up on the hill the Lanciarii's lights were strung out along the palace wall. He could see the king's window from here, at the top of the tower, lit.

"There you are," said Alana.

She stood beside him at the edge of the step, close, with her shoulder against his arm. She smelled of woodsmoke and Gemella's onions, and faintly, as she always did, of ink. Her right hand was in the fold of her skirt, where she kept it when it was shaking and she did not want him to see. He had seen.

"Rufo is teaching the Steps to jig," she said. "It's the worst thing I've ever watched. Gemella has given Ear a pie. A whole one. He doesn't know what to do with it, so he's carrying it about." She looked up at the hill. "Felix says it's your father's window."

"It is."

"Is he watching?"

"I don't know." He watched the lit window. "Valeria says he laughed."

"I heard. The whole quay's heard. There's a verse already." She was quiet. "It's wrong, of course. It has him laughing in the Hall of Kings, from the throne, at the Marshal."

"That's a better story."

"It's not the one that happened." Alana's lips moved, a little, over something; a figure, or a line of the verse she was getting right for the weather-book. "I'll put down the one that happened."

He turned his head, and looked at her; at her dark hair coming down out of its pins, and the smudge of ink on the side of her jaw where she had pushed her hair back with a wet pen, and her eyes, in the dark, on the lit window.

"Marry me," he said.

She did not look away from the window. "At home, I said."

"We're at the capital."

"Then not yet."

"You said I should keep asking."

"I did." She took her right hand out of her skirt and put it in his, shaking, and let him hold it. "I like it very much. Keep doing it." Her eyes were laughing at him in the dark. "But not here, Marcus. Not with your father's window lit up over us like a lamp in a play. I'd never know which of us you'd said it to."

They stood on by the fish-bell with her shaking hand in his, and the music coming down the Steps behind them, the Long Measure and the jigging under it, and the city's lights on the water.

Then they climbed back to Gemella's step, where she was fed, and stayed until the piper stopped; and she went in at the door of the lodging-house at the foot of the Steps, where her old room was under the roof and her students' mothers on the second landing, and stopped on the threshold and looked back at him once in the light from the stair.

He walked the cohort home to the Lantern along the galley quay, in the dark, with Liora in the middle of them asleep on her feet and Sennius holding her elbow; and nobody in the lower town was in bed.

---

He slept on the bench under the canal window, with his cloak rolled under his head and his men on the floor all round him, breathing.

The window was over him. Through the empty frame above the sack he could see a strip of sky, and the steam going up off the canal into it, faintly lit from below by the lamp the landlord had left burning at the stair foot. The canal ran by under the window with the small sounds a canal makes at night, a slap, a trickle, a boat's fender knocking somewhere along the walk. Rufo was snoring. Somebody in the yard was talking in his sleep, arguing with his mother.

He woke in the black part of the night, and did not know why.

He lay still. The canal went by. Rufo snored. Up the stair, through the ceiling, he could hear nothing at all, which meant Liora was asleep and Sennius was on his stool outside her door, and awake.

Then it came again, the thing that had woken him: footsteps, on the canal walk, coming down from the bend. One person, walking alone, not hurrying. They came along the walk toward the window, and the sound of them dropped in through the empty frame over his head, small and clear; and they did not go past. They stopped at the Lantern's door.

The sky in the window had begun, barely, to grey.

Somebody knocked at the Lantern's door: three times, patient and even, as a person knocks who has every right to be let in, and means to wait until they are.
