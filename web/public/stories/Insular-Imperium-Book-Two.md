# Chapter One: The Dullest Day

**LUCAN**

There was ice on the ink.

Not much of it: a grey skin on every well down the long table, one well to each clerk, and the clerks broke it as they sat down, with the butts of their pens, and wiped the pens on their cuffs, and blew into their hands. The brazier at the far end of the room had been lit at the first hour, when they came up the stair, and not before. Lucan had worked out the figure in his first winter in the Spire: what it cost to keep a brazier in all night on this floor, against what it cost to lose the early part of the morning to cold ink and stiff fingers. The brazier had lost. So the clerks blew into their hands, and by the second hour the wells would run clear, and the room would smell as it smelled on any working day of the year, of lamp-oil and wet ink, and of wet wool drying on the benches.

He was cold himself. He noted it as he noted the weather: his feet in their felt shoes on the black floor; the ache that had come into the knuckles of his writing hand these last few winters, and went out of them by the third hour; the dryness the lamps put in his eyes. He was past sixty, and his body had begun to send him its accounts, small ones, quarterly, in arrears. He paid them, and did not dispute them.

It was a full table this quarter, and half of it had a cold. He could hear it: the small wet sounds of men who had come up a cold stair before it was light, and a cough at the far end that had come up the stair with its owner for a week of mornings, and would put a lamp in a window at home by the spring if it was not seen to. He had told Cluvius to see to it two days ago. They wore the Survey's black, with the closed hand worked white at the breast, and they sat in the order of their islands, each with his book and his well and the counting-frame at his elbow with its beads run all to one end; and no man spoke until he was spoken to.

The tall windows down the harbor side of the room gave nothing at this hour but sleet, and the grey beyond the sleet. He had looked out of them already. He had stood at the middle one at first light with the packet from the capital open in his hands, and watched the night's work end on the quay below: the last file of the working dead going down the slipway into the harbor, unhurried, in good order, as they went every morning before the town was up, until the water had closed over the last of them and there was nothing on the quay but the night's stone, stacked and squared, and the sleet coming down on it. From that height there was nothing of them to see but the tops of their heads, which was as much of them as the custom allowed in any case.

Out past the end of the mole a black ship lay at anchor, with lamps lit along her gold rail. They had been lit, by day and by night, for as long as she had lain there. The ship's book had come ashore by boat on the first morning. Its owner had not, because he had not sent for her.

The packet lay at his right hand under the sand-box: four sheets, close-written, come across from the capital in oilskin on the night tide, in the bottom of a boat that his books called the salt-boat, in a hand so fine that he had read them through once for the hand alone. He had been buying that hand since the summer. It was worth what it cost. It had brought him the last figures of the quarter, and the audit had waited for them, which it had never done for anything before.

At his left Vopiscus sat with the Survey's books squared in front of him, waiting until the last clerk had done blowing into his hands. He was the senior quaestor of the Survey, a heavy careful man whose voice went neither up nor down, and he had read Lucan the Survey's quarter at every audit there had ever been. He did not need to be told to begin. Someone had broken the ice on Lucan's own well before he came in. Lucan dipped his pen, and Vopiscus began.

He read it as it was always read, island by island, Thessa first, because Thessa had been first.

"Thessa," said Vopiscus; and read the stock in hand at the opening of the quarter, and the stock raised in it, and the stock lost, and what was carried. The clerk who kept Thessa's book had kept it since there was a Thessa to keep. He had a chilblain on the knuckle of his first finger that came back with the frosts, and he wrote round it. He ran the finger down his column, and moved a bead along its wire, and said, "Agreed." Lucan entered what was carried. Thessa had settled in a season, the first of them all, and had made the same return every quarter since, give or take a fishing boat, so that he could have entered it without hearing it read. He heard it read. That was what the audit was for. There was a rhythm in it that he had never minded: the figure read, the figure found, agreed, entered; and the next.

"Corvus." That was this island, under their feet. The clerk who kept it put his finger on a line and said, "Less one." One of the quay stock, lost off the end of the mole in the gale before the new moon, and not recovered. "Entered," said Vopiscus, without looking up from his book; and the clerk said, "Agreed." Then the rest, one after another, down the table, and Hollowmere last of them, which was new, and still settling, and short in the columns that would be long by the spring; and at each the one who kept it said agreed, and Lucan's pen went down the page, and against the windows the sleet went on with a small dry sound, like sand thrown on a wet page.

Then the islands that had made no return, which the form put at the end.

"The Survey of the Fourth Year," said Vopiscus. "Cyrene."

Nobody said agreed. There was no clerk at the table who kept Cyrene's book. Cyrene's book was at Cyrene, in the enemy's hands, with its quaestor dead in the town and the town's stone half cut.

"No return," said Vopiscus, and turned the page.

Lucan ruled a short line where the figure would have gone. Under it stood a line he had entered himself from the Navarch's last report but one: the sea-watch on the Kettle Bank, lost, by fire, in a night.

"Silverwood. The Survey's clerk for the wood: dead. No return." Vopiscus did not change his voice. Under it was the relief sent to the wood in the quarter: seven ships, and the stock they had put ashore under the mist, all of it entered lost.

Lucan ruled another line.

The Teeth came last. Vopiscus read them from the sheet Lucan had written out himself before the clerks came up, from the packet and from the black ship's book, and he read them in the voice he had read the islands in, which was the voice he read everything in: the ships by their names, and what had become of each, and what the sea had taken, which the Survey entered at nothing, since nothing could be raised from it. Down the table the clerks copied the names into their margins as they were read, because the form had a place for them. *The Navarch: taken. Silverwood's stone, in its lead: lost; how, not known. The wood: not burned.* And one line that was no business of the Survey's, which Vopiscus read because it was on the sheet: *The chair: not brought.*

Then the men held at Cyrene, by Cyrene's own count. Lucan had entered that figure himself, at the window, and did not need to hear it again. He let it be read. Down the table a pen stopped, and went on.

Then Vopiscus drew the last book out from under the others.

It was smaller than the Survey's books, and bound in plain black, with nothing on its spine: no closed hand, no title, no number. Every clerk at the table knew it by sight, and none of them looked at it. Vopiscus opened it at the quarter's page, and Lucan watched his thick finger go down the inner margin past the names, which were written very small and close in their columns, by place, a great many of them, without stopping at any, to the foot of each column, where the figures were.

"The first line," said Vopiscus, and read what had been drawn on it in the quarter, and what remained. "The second line." What had been drawn; and then a third figure, which was how far the draw had run over the census set for it; and he read that in the same voice as the rest, and waited a moment after it, the way a clerk waits who has read out a figure that may be asked about.

Nothing was asked. He went on.

"The third line." He read it. "The fourth line. Nothing drawn."

Lucan entered a nought. The fourth line was Tiberius's. It had drawn nothing since the day it was opened, which made it the cheapest line he kept; and he had never once been pleased by it.

He had taken the totals of that book every quarter since there had been a book, and never read a name in it. He had ordered it kept so himself, and it was the only book in the Spire that was. What the lines were drawn for, and what became of what was drawn, and why a draw should run over its census in a quarter when nothing had been asked of the one who drew it—

Vopiscus signed at the foot of the page, and closed the black book, and laid it under the others.

Lucan added the foot of his own page.

He added it twice, which was the rule for a quarter, and a third time, which was his own rule for a figure he had not expected; and it came out the same each time. For four years the foot of that page had closed. Every quarter since Thessa the losses had been made good inside the quarter they were lost in, with something over, and he had not had to write at the head of a new page a figure that belonged to an old one. This one did not close. The Teeth were too large for it. They had been too large for it when he first entered them, at the window, before the room was lit; he had added it then too, with the packet in his other hand.

He wrote the difference at the foot, very small, and did not rule the line beneath it.

Down the table nobody looked at him. They were Survey clerks. They knew what a foot without a line under it meant.

"The postings," said Vopiscus, "for the coming quarter," and read them: names and places, the Survey's people going where they were sent, a step up or a step along; a young man to Hollowmere, a woman to the works at Bastion; and in the middle of the list, in the same voice as the rest, "Quaestor Plautia Rusca, to the west. Stoneheart."

Lucan knew the name from her returns: a neat hand, and figures that agreed the first time. He heard the list to its end. Then he looked once more at the difference at the foot of his page, which a stone would carry, and laid down his pen.

The room heard it. Down the long table the pens stopped, and the clerks waited with their cold hands flat on their books; and Vopiscus turned his head, for the first time that morning, and looked at him.

"Stoneheart," said Lucan. "The stone, by the spring audit."

Vopiscus wrote it at the head of the Survey's instructions for the west, in his square plain hand, and turned the book on the table so that Lucan could read it.

*Stoneheart: the stone, by the spring audit.*

Lucan nodded once. He did not ask how. How was the Survey's; it was what the Survey was for. Vopiscus sanded the line and tipped the sand back into its tray, and down the table, one after another, the pens began again.

---

His study lay across the landing from the audit room, and was colder, because he kept no brazier in it. He worked better cold. It had one window, which looked down on the academy, and one door besides the door to the landing, which was shut; and a table with nothing on it but a lamp, the ink, the sand-box and a jug of water. Through the door across the landing he could hear the pens, which was the sound the Survey made when it was well.

The academy lay under the window as it had lain for two winters, roofless and black, its long walls standing open to the weather and the sleet going through them. Along the north range the towers stood with their stairs still in them and nothing at the top, and below them the ground went down in black terraces to the sea, which was the color of slate today and came in under the cliff in long slow rollers out of the Grey. He had left all of it as it was. A ruin cost nothing to keep, and a clearance would have cost a season; and the Spire had been raised out of what the fire left of the archive in one, which was a figure he had been pleased with at the time.

The black glass of his own walls he had had left dull, so that he was not followed about his rooms all day by a thin old man in black.

Cluvius brought the petitions up at the fourth hour. He was a narrow grey man who had been Lucan's secretary since before the war, and in all that time he had not brought a paper up the stair in the wrong order. There were six this morning. Five were the ordinary business of the quarter, and Lucan minuted them as Cluvius laid them down, a word or two in the margin of each, with his free hand drawn up into his sleeve against the cold. The harbor-master at Thessa wanted more stock for the new mole, and could have it in the spring. Two towns on the north coast at home asked to have their lamp-oil at the guild's price, and could not. A smith's widow at Lessa had sold the Survey nails in the summer and had not been paid; she was paid. A magistrate inland had hanged a man for a thing that was not a hanging matter, and wished to know whether he had done right; he had not, and would be told so, and would pay the family out of his own stipend, because a hanging that was not owed was a debt run up in the crown's name. And the Treasury asked, as it asked each quarter, whether the king would come home for Midwinter, and was given the answer it was always given, in one word. By the end of them his free hand had gone numb inside the sleeve.

The sixth was on the paper of the Guild of the Lamps, which smelled faintly of the oil it was stored with. Everything the guild owned did.

*To the King. From Aemilia Scaura, for the Guild of the Lamps, at the capital.*

*Majesty. My brother Lartius Scaurus, Navarch of the Southern Sea, is a prisoner at Cyrene. I ask that he be brought home, by exchange, or by ransom, or by any road Your Majesty thinks fit. The guild will bear what it can.*

*I have kept the lamps of this city a long time, for other women's sons. I would rather not keep one for my brother.*

The hand was large and plain, without a flourish in it anywhere. The guild tied a tag to every lamp it tended, with a name on it and the day the year was out, and she had written a great many of them; it showed. She wrote as if each word had to be read from the street. There had been no such guild when Lucan came to the throne. It kept the lamps in half the streets of the capital now, at a fair price, and paid for itself.

Pinned behind it was a letter from the Navarchy at Thessa, under the Navarchy's seal, from the officer who held the Southern Sea in the Navarch's absence. The Navarchy asked leave to act in the matter of its Navarch, and of its men and its hulls, held at Cyrene. Folded inside the letter was a second sheet under a second seal, endorsed in the same hand: *Particulars, for His Majesty's eye, if His Majesty wishes it.*

Lucan read the petition once and the letter once. Then he sat a moment with the sister's sheet under his hand.

The Navarch had been the best clerk the Navarchy ever had. He wrote everything down. He had made his name at the Teeth, on his first command, with a lead box and a still night, and Lucan had seen him once afterward: a long young man who came up the stair at home with his own book under his arm, and read his report out of it standing, because he had not been asked to sit, and did not once look up from the page until he reached the foot of it. Lucan had given him the Southern Sea before he was out of the room. He had lost both, the name and the sea, at the same reef, on a morning, to a drunk with a penal cohort.

"The lady's answer, Majesty?" said Cluvius.

"The Navarch is entered as a loss."

Cluvius's pen did not move at once. He was a careful man, and he had sat through the audit. "He is held, Majesty," he said. "At Cyrene. He is living."

"A loss is a figure," said Lucan.

A figure could be entered, and a line ruled under it, and the page turned. A man held in a fishing town could not. He was a line left open, which would want carrying from quarter to quarter at a cost nobody could name, until somebody else decided what he was; and Lucan did not keep lines open for other people to close.

Cluvius wrote it down, on the form for the answer to a petition, which had a place for the answer and no place for anything else.

The Navarchy's letter Lucan minuted himself, across its foot, in his own hand.

*The Navarchy may act, at its own cost.*

The second sheet he did not open. He turned it over once in his fingers. The wax was green and cold, with a galley's beak pressed into it, and someone at Thessa had pressed it very hard. He laid it on top of the letter, seal up, unbroken; and Cluvius, who had been his secretary long enough, took the two up together and did not ask. A seal unbroken was a thing he had not been told. Whatever the Navarchy meant to do, it would do out of its own books, and whatever it spent would come to him at the end, if it came at all, as one line at the foot of somebody else's page. If it spent men, they would be the Navarchy's men. It was the cheapest leave he had ever given.

"Thank you, Cluvius."

Cluvius took the petitions down the stair with the Navarchy's particulars on top, and the green seal whole.

---

When the stair was quiet he unlocked the bottom drawer of his table, the only one with a lock, and took out his own book.

It was not a Survey book. There was no hand on it. It was the latest of the Aeridor books, which he had kept since he was a young man in his brother's treasury with a lamp and other men's figures; the others stood on the shelf behind him, thirty years of them, with a famine in every fourth winter, the oldest with their spines gone soft as cloth. He kept the newest in the drawer because the drawer locked, and because of what lay under it.

The drawer smelled of dust and old paper. He did not take out what lay at the bottom of it. He knew what it said: a half-sheet in a poor hand: so many measures of wheat, received, against one shard of the stone of Thessa; and at the foot the name of Thessa's lord, who had not written his own name often enough in his life to do it well. Folded under it was the letter Thessa's lord had sent across with the shard, so that whoever had the shard should know he had asked his own king first: the crown's apologies, on excellent paper. It was excellent paper. Lucan had always thought so.

The shard had gone to the Teeth in a lead box, and Aeridor's fleet had followed it there.

He opened the book at the Aeridor pages, and warmed his writing hand a moment under his arm, and entered the packet under the capital, in the small upright hand that looked the same at the end of a day as at the beginning.

*The harbor bell rung in daylight: the first time since the year of Thessa.*

*The* Audit *berthed in the first of the royal berths, the* Concord*'s. Renamed: the* Answer.

He looked at that a moment. The Navarchy named its ships out of the ledger, and the boy had answered one. It was a good name. He would not have chosen it, and he could not fault it.

Three lines above it stood a figure he had not had to touch since the day he wrote it: *Royal berths under the Old Hill, empty: thirty-one.* He drew one line through the figure, and wrote above it: *thirty.* He had the names of the ships that had lain in them as well, in the oldest of the books on the shelf, with their masters and their burden, in a young man's hand. He had never said them aloud. There had been no reason to.

*The council to thank the prince in public; then to ask who gave him leave.*

He could have written that minute himself. Aeridor's council stood on the shelf behind him, every year of it, and it had never yet thanked a man without asking him afterward who gave him leave.

*The king refused the nephew the packet, and the Navarch.*

The packet called its writer the nephew all the way through, as if there were another hand in the isles that might be taken for that one. In the months Lucan had been buying it, Aurelian had refused it nothing. A king who had begun to refuse his nephew things was a line to be watched; Lucan made a small mark in the margin against it, which was how he watched a line.

Then he turned back to the page for the royal house.

*Aurelian. King.* Under it, *Titus. Heir.*, and three lines of Lucan's own, the best he had ever written of an Aeridorian: *Reads ground. Asks for what he needs. Is refused.* After them, in the ink of a few weeks ago: *Dead, at Hollowmere.*

And under Titus, the line that had been the second son's.

*Heir: none of consequence.*

The verdict had stood on the second son's line since the stair, three years ago. He had moved it up a line himself when Hollowmere was entered, and seen no reason to change a word of it in the moving; a drunk who had run from a stair was none of consequence wherever he stood on the page. Lucan had the name of the tavern.

He read the quarter's lines again, the packet's and his own. A fishing town retaken with a cohort out of a harbor prison, and held. A wood held, and signed to the crown. A Navarch taken with his squadron at a reef that could not be passed, and a Tharsian three-banker sent across the Grey to lie in the first royal berth, so that a whole city came down to the water to look at her; and a council that would have to thank the boy for it in public before it could ask him anything at all.

*None of consequence* had been the right entry since the stair. It was not the right entry now.

He drew one line through it, cleanly, so that it could still be read. Above it, small, he wrote:

*Heir: the runner.*

---

At the sixth hour the books went back into their presses, and the cloth was laid on the audit table, because the quarter's dinner had been eaten off that table since the Spire was raised and nobody had found a reason to move it.

They sat down to it in their order: Vopiscus at the king's left, and below him the quaestors who had come in for the audit, and below them their deputies, a dozen living officers of the Survey in their black, with ink in the grain of their fingers that no soap had shifted in years. The sleet had stopped. Through the tall windows the harbor lay flat and grey, and the stone on the quay had gone dark with the wet; and beyond the mole, all along the west, the Grey stood up out of the sea without a top or an end to it, so close in this weather that the black ship at her anchor seemed to lie against it. A white sun hung low over it and put no warmth through the glass. Nobody at the table looked at the black ship.

The steward and two boys brought the dishes in. A saddle of mutton roasted with garlic and rosemary, the fat crackled brown and running into the dish; an eel pie with pepper in the crust; a coil of black sausage, split and fried; and the white bread of the home granaries, warm, in baskets. The smell went down the table ahead of the dishes, meat and garlic and hot fat and the sharp green of the rosemary, and got into the room's own smell of oil and ink and wet wool and sat on top of it; and when the pie was cut, pepper, and the brown smell of the river the eels had come out of. The officers' faces changed when it reached them. They had been at their books since the first hour.

The wine was the red of the home vineyards, so dark in the cups that it looked black, and there was a jug of it at every second elbow. At Lucan's elbow the steward poured from another jug, and nobody remarked on it. The steward was an old soldier with one ear, who had poured for him since the old palace. Once, in the Spire's first winter, a young deputy had looked from the king's cup to his own and opened his mouth, and Vopiscus had trodden on his foot. The young man had been posted to Bastion the next quarter, which was preferment, and had nothing to do with it, though the Survey had never believed so.

Lucan ate two slices of the mutton, cut thin, and some bread. He tasted all of it: the garlic, and the resin of the rosemary, and the faint tallow at the edge of the fat where it had caught; and he stopped when he had had enough. The water came up from the cistern in a stone jug. It tasted of the stone it was kept in, and it was very cold, and it found the bad tooth on the left side of his jaw; he drank it all the same.

The Survey did not drink in the field. It was proud of that. At the king's quarter table it drank, because the wine was poured and to leave it would be a discourtesy; and it drank carefully at first, as if the cups might be audited. He watched the wine go into them the way damp gets into a ledger, unseen, until the pages will not lie flat.

They talked of the Survey's business, because there was nothing else they all knew. Ofella, who kept Hollowmere's book, told the table that the ford road at Hollowmere had been paved to the town in a month, all of it by night, so that the town had woken every morning to another stretch of it and never seen a stone laid; and the table was pleased, because it was true, and because it was theirs. The Navarchy had had a bad quarter, and the Survey, which had had a bad quarter too, found that a comfort. The quaestor beside Ofella said the Navarchy would want hulls by the spring, and men to row them, and would have to come to the Survey for the men, and the Survey would see what stock it could spare. Down the table they laughed, because the Navarchy rowed with the living and was proud of it, as the Survey was proud of its water. A deputy from Thessa said that the Navarch had been a fine clerk, whatever else he had been; and they drank to him, as if he were dead. Somebody said he would be writing it all down wherever they had put him, and would have the fishing town's census done by Midwinter, and that went down well too.

Below Ofella sat a young quaestor called Tadius, who had come out from home on the last boat before the audit and been read out that morning for Hollowmere. It was his first posting west. He told the end of the table about his wife, who had wept when the letter came and then gone out and told the whole street, because her father had been a clerk of the roads all his life and never been sent west; and while he told it he turned his cup round and round on the cloth by its foot, and did not drink from it. Then he asked Ofella what he ought to write home about Hollowmere.

Ofella had been at Hollowmere since the ford. She considered him.

"That it's cold," she said, and passed him the sausage.

Nobody who came home from the west described it; Tadius would learn that by the end of his first winter. He laughed, uncertainly, and took some of the sausage, and drank.

By the second cup he had stopped turning it. He had been at the Spire less than a week, and had not yet learned how far a voice carried in a room with a black floor. In a pause, while the steward was taking away the mutton, he leaned out past the man beside him and spoke up the table to the king.

"When it's settled, Majesty," he said. "Out there. All of it." His hand went out toward the windows, and the Grey. "When the Calm's come to the west, and there's nothing left to settle. What will the Survey do then?"

The table went still. Ofella looked at her plate. Vopiscus set his cup down without a sound, which in a heavy man was a kind of noise.

Lucan looked down the table at the young man for a moment: at his cup, and his clean cuffs, and the ink on his fingers.

"Count," he said.

They laughed. It was a little too loud, and it went on a little too long, and Tadius laughed hardest of them all, with relief, and lifted his cup.

"To the Calm, then," he said. "Out there."

They drank to it, out there, all down the table; and Lucan lifted his water, and drank to it too.

Vopiscus drank with them. He sat with his back to the windows; he had sat so at every quarter's table. His son had been quay stock since the winter the Spire was raised, and the quay was under them. Below the windows, below the quay, under the flat grey water of the harbor where they had walked down at first light, the night's men stood in their rows in the cold and waited for dark.

---

He sent the boat out for her at noon, and she came up the stair at the ninth hour, when the light was going.

The afternoon went as the afternoons of audit days went. The quarter's totals were copied fair, and the fair copies read against the foul, line by line, by two clerks who did not like each other and were kept together for that reason; the Survey's instructions for the west were sealed and sent down to the boat; and the clerks who had come in from the islands went down the stair in ones and twos to find their berths for the night. The wind had gone round into the north while they were at table and come on to blow, and all afternoon the black ship had pitched at her anchor beyond the mole with her lamps swinging along the rail. He had not had the study's lamps lit. There was light enough for what he had to say.

Lucan heard her come before he saw her: Cluvius's voice on the landing, saying the form of words, and the guards at the stair-head shifting their feet. They were men of the garrison who had drawn the lot for Corvus that year, and would not draw it again. They looked at the wall while she went by. A draught came up the stair with her and laid the flames over in the landing lamps; and Cluvius showed her in, and shut the door, and she stood just inside it with the last of the window's light on her face.

She had not changed; the dead did not. Black hair, with the two silver pins in it. Black silk worked with gold, in a pattern that moved when he did not look at it directly and lay still when he did. The violet eyes she had had alive, and kept. He supposed she was beautiful. He had been told so often enough to have entered it.

She held her right hand out from her side. The first two fingers and the thumb were grey to the second joint, a dry dull grey, like sealing-wax that has been melted too many times to take a mark. From her other wrist the thread hung over the back of her hand. He counted the lights on it before she had taken a step, and they came to thirty-six: the small ones, quick and bright, that moved on the thread all the time, and the larger ones, dimmer and steady, that did not move. She smelled of her ship, of tar and of the cold oil of the lamps that burned along her rail by day.

"Nine days," said Livia. "I counted them, Lucan. I thought you'd want them counted."

He did not answer. He had his pen in his hand, dry, over the page, and he left it there.

She waited to see if he would. She did not sit; she never sat in his rooms unless he told her to, and he did not tell her. Then she came a few steps into the room, and told it.

She told it well. It had been her gift at Corvus, when she was alive and a chair and talked to rooms of the young about books they would never read. She told it as a story is told to a table, with places left open in it where a listener would catch his breath; and he left them open. At first light on the morning of the Teeth she had gone into the bow of the prince's ship, in the middle of his fleet, and taken the little chair out of it, and nobody had seen her do it but an elf, who had shot a marine for it. She had taken her across to the Navarch's ship, and put her hands in the Corvus irons: the old pair out of the glass case in the long gallery, with the card on them.

"You'd have liked the card," said Livia.

She had meant to bring her to him by sunset, as he had asked. And then the prince had come through the Teeth on the young flood, with his ships and his drum and his fish-people, through a reef that could not be passed.

"The Navarch said so," said Livia. "He wrote it down."

She had watched it from the stern windows. The prince's people had made the sea go round: the whole pool behind the reef turning on itself, slowly, with the Navarch's ships in it, and something in the water under them that she had not been able to see. She told him that part with real pleasure. He did not enter the pleasure. He entered the sea going round, and the something under it, and that she had not been able to see what it was.

While they were coming through, the chair had heated the irons. With her own fire, from the inside, until the pins went soft. No rod.

"I always said she could do it without the rod." Something came into Livia's voice when she said it. He left it out of the account.

When the ships met and it was time to go, she had taken the chair by the wrist to bring her away, and the iron at the wrist had been hot. She lifted the grey hand an inch, as evidence, and let it fall. So the chair had broken an iron off on the corner of the Navarch's table and stood there with fire in her bare hand; and Livia had come home.

She held up the thread and let the lights swing.

"It cost me two," she said.

He had counted them already.

First light, she had said; and the Teeth forced on the young flood. There had been the best part of a morning between the two. The errand as he had given it had wanted an hour of that morning at the most: somewhere quiet, gently, and done, and then raised, and brought to him, to sit at this table and be asked what she knew. Livia had asked him for the errand, in the way she asked for things, so that he might think the giving had been his own idea; he had let her think he thought so, and given it to her because she asked. He did not ask why the chair had still been alive at the young flood. He had never asked any of them anything of that kind, in any quarter, and it was not because he could not guess.

A guess was not an entry.

Livia did not like the silence. She had never liked a silence in a room. She went to the window and looked down at the academy for some time, at the long black walls with the dusk coming into them, and the towers, and the gold moved in her silk while she stood there. She stood there longer than there was anything to see. He supposed she was looking for the long gallery, which had run along the side that faced the sea, and was not there. Then she came back, and touched his sand-box with one grey finger, and turned it a little on the table, so that it was not square.

"I told her why it burned," she said. "Corvus. Something on a shelf. I told her in the cabin, while we waited. I thought she ought to know what she was for." She watched his face. "She was Chair of the catalogue, Lucan. She had her hand on every card in that building. She'll work it out. She'll have it in a column by the spring, and wait till summer to be sure, because she always did."

Something under his breastbone went cold and small, and stayed so.

He had burned the shelf, and the building round the shelf, because something had been on it. He did not know what. He had not known then, and had burned it all the same, because a thing he could not count was a thing he could not let stand. Afterward he had had the chairs counted, the living and the dead, and the count had come to one short. He had sent for the one. He was still at one.

He felt it, as a living man feels a thing. He felt all of it, and gave her none.

She was watching for it. He knew what she watched for in a face: the moment a thing understood that it was hers. He gave her his face, which was a clean page with nothing written on it, and she read it from the top to the foot, and found nothing there, and read it again.

"I told her you'd be cross," said Livia. "When I left. I said, Lucan is going to be so very cross with me." She smiled. "Are you?"

The first line in the black book was hers. It had been hers since Corvus was entered, and it had never run over; she was careful with it, as she was careful with her lights.

He dipped his pen at last. He drew a half-sheet toward him and wrote one line on it for Vopiscus, and sanded it, and tipped the sand back into the box, and set the box square again, and laid the half-sheet face down on the table. Then he looked at her.

"You will draw nothing until the ledger balances. Close the door."

For a moment she did not move at all.

He had never seen her do that. He had not known she could. The small bright lights on her thread went on moving, all along it; and the rest of her stood quite still in the middle of his floor, in the last of the light, with her grey hand held out from her side and her face turned toward him and nothing in it.

Then she went out, and closed the door behind her, softly, with her good hand.

---

When the lamps had been lit he added the foot of the page once more, alone and without hurrying, in case the figures had been waiting for the room to empty. They had not. He turned to a clean page for the new quarter and wrote the difference at the head of it, and beside it a word he had not had to write at the head of any page since Thessa.

*Carried.*

He sat and looked at it while the ink dried.

Nothing in his books was meant to be carried long. Every figure he had ever entered was owed somewhere and would be paid somewhere, and the whole of the work, the stones and the Survey and the war, the roads paved by night, the Settling of the isles, was only the paying of it, quarter by quarter, until the day came when there would be nothing on any page that was owed to any other: every book closed, and nothing carried, and nothing owed, anywhere, ever again. He had a word for that day, the Balance, and he had never said it aloud, or written it in any book.

He had always known it was a long way off. He had not known, until today, that it could go further.

He put the book away and locked the drawer. He poured his water from the jug on the corner of the table, and took the cup in one hand and the day-book in the other, and went through the other door.

It was the coldest room on the floor, and kept so. Old Tullus stood by the door in a quilted coat and mittens; he was older than either of them now, and his hands shook when the cold got into them, and he would not sit down. There were shelves on three walls, and on the shelves twenty years of day-books in their order, the first of them from the old palace at home, with their spines gone pale. He had never taken one down again. There was nothing in any of them that had not been read aloud once already, to the only listener they had ever had. The window in the fourth wall gave on the quay, where the night's work had begun. He could hear it through the glass: the long scrape of a sledge on wet stone, and the knock of stone set down on stone, and no voices.

His brother sat with his back to the window.

Tullus had put him in the winter wool tonight, the dark grey with fur at the collar, and passed the razor over his face, as he did every morning, the razor he had used on him alive, though in twenty years it had found nothing to take; and combed his hair, and set his hands on the arms of the chair, and laid a rug over his knees, though there was no cold he could feel. The wool smelled of the cedar press it had come out of. His eyes rested on the floor in front of his feet. In the lamplight he looked as he had looked on the night he died, except that his face was calm; and it had never once been calm while he lived. It had wept at figures. It had wept at the famine figures in the bad winters, every one, and promised, and promised, and never done the sum. Lucan was older now than his brother had lived to be, by some years. He had passed him one winter without remarking it.

"He's in the winter wool, Majesty," said Tullus.

"Good."

Lucan sat down in his own chair across from him, and felt the cold of the room in his knees, and drank. The water was very cold, and found the tooth. He opened the day-book on his knee.

He read him the day.

He read it in the voice he used for everything, which was the voice the Survey read in; Vopiscus had learned it from him a long time ago, and neither of them had ever said so. He read him the Survey's quarter, island by island, Thessa first; and the granaries at home, which were full; and the price of lamp-oil at the capital, which had gone up in the quarter, as it went up in any quarter with a sea in it. He read him the petitions and the answers to them, and the postings, and the line for the west, and the line he had sent down to Vopiscus at the ninth hour. He read him the Teeth as Vopiscus had read them in the morning, ship by ship, without hurrying: the *Surety*, on the reef; the *Impost*, burned, as she had been fitted to be; the *Toll*, burned and sunk, with the fire-hulk that was driven onto her, a hulk the Navarchy had never named because it had been bought to be burned; the *Audit*, and the *Levy*, and the *Tithe*, and the *Arrears*, taken. The Navarch, taken. Silverwood's stone, lost. The wood, not burned. The chair, not brought.

His brother did not look up. He never did.

Lucan turned the page.

"Held at Cyrene," he read, "by Cyrene's own count. Four hundred and twelve."

There was a place here.

There had always been a place, after a figure like that. Once, at a figure like that, his brother would have said something. He would have been sorry out loud, at length, with his hands; he would have wanted to know whether they were warm, and whether they were fed, and whether anyone had written to their mothers. It would have been foolish, and kind, and it would have cost something, and in the morning Lucan would have gone down to the treasury and found out what.

He waited. He always waited, here.

Nothing came.

His brother's hands lay on the arms of the chair, where Tullus had put them. Below the window a sledge went by on the quay, and stone knocked on stone, and nobody spoke. Tullus, at the door, did not move.

Lucan drank his water. He did not enter it.

He read him the rest.
